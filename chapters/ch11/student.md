# Unit 11. Adverbial clauses

An adverbial clause places an action in time or relates it to a cause, purpose, result, concession or condition. First identify that relation; then consider how the speaker presents it. An indicative commonly asserts an event. A subjunctive can present an intention, an anticipated event, a concession or a supposition. It does not always mean that something is doubtful or false.

The fourteen translated examples, the exercises marked F, A, T, R, B and S, and “The fisherman and his son” are new teaching material. Cook IV.16–17 and V.18 retain their original English prompts. The two later readings are exact passages from Cook’s *First Book*, with separate context, help and original notes.

In the tables, 1, 2 and 3 denote person. Repeated spellings still occupy distinct grammatical cells. Nominative, genitive, dative and accusative forms are for use in composition within their stated meanings. Forms marked **reference** are for recognition. Supplied expressions may be used with their printed help. Preserve vowel lengths and the distinction between e and ę.

## 1. Time clauses

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-clause-roles-order -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-past-when-then -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-THA-CONJUNCTION -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-THA-ADVERB -->

For an event at a particular past time, **ðā**, “when”, introduces a subordinate clause, normally with the indicative. A second **ðā**, “then”, may introduce the main clause. The first connects clauses; the second is an adverb inside the main clause. The same spelling also belongs to familiar inflected forms of the article or demonstrative: identify its function from the construction.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE01 -->

Ðā se fiscere cōm, ðā sæt se sunu on ðǣm hūse.

When the fisherman came, the son was sitting in the house.

Cōm and sæt are past indicative forms. The first clause tells when the sitting took place. The second ðā resumes the narrative; English can omit “then”.

A dependent clause often puts its finite verb late. A main clause beginning with an adverb often puts its finite verb before its subject, as in ðā sæt se sunu. These are useful tendencies, not instructions to reverse or move every verb mechanically. Case and agreement still determine the participants.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-recurring-when-then -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-THONNE-CONJUNCTION -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-THONNE -->

Use **ðonne**, “when”, for recurring or prospective time. It too can be answered by the adverb **ðonne**, “then”. In the following example the visit and gift recur. Both finite verbs are present indicative. A future setting does not by itself require a subjunctive or a separate future ending.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE02 -->

Ðonne se munuc cymð, ðonne sęlð se mann him męte.

When the monk comes, the man gives him food.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-while -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-THA-HWILE-THE -->

**Ðā-hwīle-ðe**, “while”, introduces a period during which another action takes place. In a statement of actual simultaneous events, use the indicative.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE03 -->

Ðā-hwīle-ðe se munuc sang, se sunu dranc wæter.

While the monk sang, the son drank water.

The two actions overlap in time. The conjunction alone does not make either action the cause of the other.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-mid-thy-temporal -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-MID-THY -->

**Mid-ðȳ**, “when; while”, is another temporal conjunction. Learn the whole connection in this use; do not translate it word by word as a phrase of means.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE04 -->

Mid-ðȳ se sunu ǣt, se fæder sæt on ðǣm hūse.

While the son ate, the father sat in the house.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-before-anticipated -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-AER-CONJUNCTION -->

**Ǣr**, “before”, can introduce an event anticipated from the viewpoint of the main clause. After the following instruction, the monk’s coming is still expected, so **cume** is present subjunctive. The command itself is plural.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE05 -->

Ne farað, ǣr se munuc cume.

Do not go before the monk comes.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-until-anticipated -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-OTH-THAET -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-HER -->

**Oð-ðæt**, “until”, gives the endpoint of an action or state. Use the subjunctive for the selected anticipated endpoint. **Hēr** means “here”.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE06 -->

Wes hēr, oð-ðæt wē cumen.

Be here until we come.

Wes addresses one person. Cumen is present subjunctive plural: the people expected to come are wē. The English present “come” does not decide the Old English mood.

These are statements about viewpoint, not universal rules equating “before” or “until” with one tense or mood. A narrator can use a subjunctive for an event viewed as still ahead at an earlier point, even if it later happened.

## 2. Cause, purpose and result

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-causal-for-thon-the -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-FOR-THON-THE -->

**For-ðon-ðe**, “because”, introduces a stated reason. Use the indicative for the factual cause illustrated here. The earlier causal for-ðām remains available; the exact reading below also has shorter for-ðon.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE07 -->

Se mann healp ðǣm cuman, for-ðon-ðe hē wæs untrum.

The man helped the stranger because the stranger was sick.

Here hē is understood to mean the stranger. Masculine singular grammar by itself could also fit the man; the intended reference must be established from context. The reason clause explains why the help was given.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-purpose -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-THAET-CONJUNCTION -->

**Ðæt + subjunctive** introduces an intended purpose: “so that; in order that”. Unlike the earlier same-subject purpose infinitive, a finite purpose clause has its own expressed or understood subject. That subject may be the person acting in the main clause or a different person.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE08 -->

Ic cume, ðæt ic ðē helpe.

I come so that I may help you.

The first cume is indicative in the statement “I come”; helpe is subjunctive in the purpose. First-person singular present forms may have the same spelling in both moods. Function and context matter as well as endings.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE09 -->

Se fæder cōm, ðæt hē his suna hulpe.

The father came so that he might help his son.

Hulpe is third singular preterite subjunctive of helpan. The father’s purpose is to help his son; the sentence does not by itself assert that he succeeded. His suna is dative, the person helped, with his as genitive possessor.

The present purpose example uses helpe; the past example uses hulpe. For the already learned subjunctives, present singular -e contrasts with plural -en; the corresponding strong preterite uses the preterite plural stem with singular -e and plural -en. Thus helpe/helpen and hulpe/hulpen differ in tense and number. The examples here establish these particular present and past settings, without a rule mechanically replacing every subordinate tense.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-negative-purpose -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-THY-LAES-THE -->

**Ðȳ-lǣs-ðe + subjunctive**, “lest; so that … not”, states an outcome to be prevented. Its negative force belongs to the purpose clause. It does not negate the main instruction.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE10 -->

Heald ðæt dēor, ðȳ-lǣs-ðe hit ðone męte nime.

Hold the animal, lest it take the food.

Nime is third singular present subjunctive. The purpose is that the animal should not take the food. No actual taking is asserted.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-factual-result -->

An asserted result commonly has **swā … ðæt + indicative**, “so … that”. Swā marks the degree; the ðæt clause says what followed. Compare intended help with an actual consequence.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE11 -->

Se sunu wæs swā glæd, ðæt hē sang.

The son was so glad that he sang.

Sang is past indicative: the son did sing. A purpose clause would present singing as an aim. The earlier content clause, as after witan “know”, instead states what is known. The conjunction ðæt alone does not distinguish these three functions.

Cook also gives a result-like optative curse under his subjunctive examples. That special wish does not make every factual result subjunctive. Read the force of the whole sentence.

## 3. Concession, condition and comparison

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-concession -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-THEAH -->

**Ðēah**, “although; even though”, introduces a concession: a circumstance that might have been expected to prevent the main action. The selected construction normally has the subjunctive. The speaker can nevertheless accept the circumstance as a fact.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE12 -->

Ðēah se fiscere sīe eald, hē hilpð his suna.

Although the fisherman is old, he helps his son.

Sīe is third singular present subjunctive. The example concedes the fisherman’s old age; it does not deny it. Hilpð is indicative, asserting the help.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-contrary-present-condition -->

The earlier open **gif**, “if”, construction leaves the condition open. An explicitly **contrary-present** condition imagines something known not to be so now. In that setting both clauses use preterite subjunctive forms. Their past morphology need not give the sentence past-time reference.

Open condition: the speaker does not know whether the monk is here.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE13 -->

Gif se munuc is hēr, hē hilpð ðǣm męnn.

If the monk is here, he helps the man.

Contrary-present condition: the monk is absent now.

<!-- example: NEW-U11-EXAMPLE14 -->

Gif se munuc wǣre hēr, hē hulpe ðǣm męnn.

If the monk were here, he would help the man.

Wǣre and hulpe are preterite subjunctives referring to the imagined present. Do not translate them as completed past events merely because of their forms. This contrast does not introduce the full system of contrary-to-fact past conditions.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-ac-coordination -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-AC -->

**Ac**, “but”, coordinates contrasting clauses or predicates. It imposes no subordinate-clause mood. Keep the persons and numbers of both subjects even when English repeats “shall” or “will”; the already learned present can refer to the future.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-swa-comparative-clause -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-SWA -->

A finite **swā** clause can mean “as; in the same way as”, comparing actions. Use the indicative when both actions are asserted. This differs from the degree adverb swā in swā … ðæt.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-verbal-comparison-ma -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-MA -->

**Mā**, “more”, can compare the amount of an action. **Mā … ðonne** means “more … than”. The second subject remains nominative; a repeated predicate can be understood from the first clause. In a question about what people do, do not turn adverbial mā into an adjective describing the people. Distinguish comparative ðonne, “than”, from temporal ðonne, “when”, and interrogative hwonne, “when?”.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-temporal-relative-the -->

After a time noun, **ðe** can introduce a temporal relative: “the day when …”. It attaches the event to that stated day. It is neither a direct question with hwonne nor a conditional with gif. The ordinary subject-relative construction from Unit 8 also remains available: a demonstrative meaning “those” may be followed by ðe, “who”, with a plural verb for the people concerned.

## 4. Nouns: complete models and stem changes

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-LEXICAL-DIAGNOSTICS -->

For each new noun learn its meaning, gender and stem together. The diagnostic forms below are genitive singular, dative singular and nominative plural. Where a noun follows a printed model, choose the cell with the required case and number, replace the model stem by the listed stem, and keep the ending. An endingless cell stays endingless, except where an explicit full-headword rule says otherwise. Apply the operation to **all four cases in both numbers**.

Singular and plural are available in the stated meanings unless a limit is given. The plurals of **lēad, īsern, regn, frōfor, dēað, fȳr** and **seolfor** are reference forms: no new count meaning such as separate “rains” is required. **Sunne** and **mōna** denote the singular sun and moon here; their plurals are reference. **Hǣlend** is the proper title and has only the four assigned singular cells.

## 7. Additional meanings and constructions

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-HIERAN -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-hieran-obey-dative -->

**Hīeran**, besides “hear”, can mean **“obey” with a dative** person. Coordinated subjects require a plural verb. Do not infer an accusative from the English object “him”.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-DON -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-ON -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-don-placement -->

**Dōn**, “put; place”, takes an **accusative** object. For placement into or onto something, use **on + accusative** as the goal. This differs from the already taught on + dative for location. In a negative command, retain the taught ne with the appropriate singular or plural command.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-THURH -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-thurh-accusative -->

**Þurh + accusative**, “through”, gives the spatial path. Learn the þ spelling for composition. The exact sea reading has **ðurh**, with ð, and retains it in both its path phrase and the supplied expression ðurh eall, “entirely”.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-RISAN -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-UP -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-ONGEAN -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-risan-up-against -->

The familiar **rīsan**, “rise”, combines with **ūp**, “up”. For rising against an opponent or adverse power, add **ongēan + accusative**, “against”. Ūp itself gives no opponent. Keep the already learned unprefixed rīsan forms; this phrase does not require a new verb ārīsan.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-BUTAN -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-WIF -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-butan-dative -->

**Būtan + dative**, “besides; apart from”, excludes a group from the number just stated. In this use **wīf** means “woman”, as well as its earlier “wife”; it remains grammatically neuter. Keep the case governed by būtan when the phrase names women and children.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-BIDDAN -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-biddan-person -->

In the parenthetic **ic ðē bidde**, “I beseech you; please”, biddan takes the person entreated in the **accusative**. The same surface ðē can be dative elsewhere, but the verb determines its case here. This bounded request does not introduce every possible complement of biddan.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-DOM -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-OFT -->

**Dōm** can mean “doom; fate” in addition to the earlier “judgment”. Keep its familiar masculine forms. **Oft** means “often”; like hēr and mā it is an invariant adverb.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-NALAES -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-VOC-HOLUNGA -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-u11-nalaes-negation -->

Learn **nālæs hōlunga**, “not at all in vain”, as the selected emphatic phrase: nālæs, “not at all”, denies hōlunga, “in vain”. It says that the action was not fruitless. Do not mechanically add another ne. This phrase does not teach the general system of negative concord.

The ordinary senses and forms from earlier units remain in force: helpan governs a dative person; sęllan takes an accusative thing and dative recipient; clothing takes an accusative person; possessive his and hiera name the possessor without agreeing as an adjective. Familiar meanings do not erase the particular new case constructions just learned.

## 14. Æthelwald calms the sea

This exact connected episode is from Cook’s *First Book*, printed pp.142–143 / PDF pp.158–159: editorial line8 on p.142 through line14 on p.143. It begins after standan. in the preceding account and ends with heofonlice forgiefen wæs. The 195 words retain the original two paragraphs. Cook’s original notes, including the cross-reference to an earlier note, follow in §15.

**Earlier events.** Three visitors are returning by boat from the hermit Æthelwald on Farne. A sudden storm has stopped their progress. After struggling in vain, they look back toward the island. The narrator now tells how they were rescued.

<!-- source: FB-V-SEA-RESCUE-01 -->

Ðā wæs æfter langum fæce ðæt wē ūre gesihð feorr ūpāhōfon, ðā gesāwon wē in Farne, ðǣm īglande, Gode ðone lēofan fæder Æðelwald of his dīegelnessum ūtgangende, ðæt hē wolde ūrne sīðfæt scēawian, and gesēon hwæt ūs gelumpe, for-ðon hē gehīerde ðæt gebrec ðǣra storma and ðæs weallendes sǣs.

Mid-ðȳ hē ðā ūs ēac scēawode, and geseah in gewinne and in ōrmōdnesse gesętte bēon, ðā biegde hē his cnēowu tō Fæder ūres Dryhtnes Hǣlendes Crīstes, and wæs gebiddende for ūre hǣle and for ūrum līfe. And mid-ðȳ hē ðā ðæt gebed gefylde, hē ðā samod ætgædere ge ðone āðundnan sǣ gesmylte ge ðone storm gestilde, tō ðon ðætte ðurh eall sēo rēðnes ðæs stormes wæs blinnende, and gesyndge windas ðurh ðone smyltestan sǣ ūs æt lande gebrōhton. Mid-ðȳ wē ðā ūp cōmon tō lande, and ūre scip ēac swilce fram ðǣm ȳðum ūp ābǣron, ðā sōna se ilca storm ęft hwearf and cōm, se-ðe for ūrum intingan medmicel fæc gestilde, and ealne ðone dæg swīðe micel and strang wæs, ðætte męnn sweotollice ongietan meahton ðætte se medmicla fierst ðǣre stilnesse, ðe ðǣr becōm, tō bēnum ðæs Godes weres for intingan ūre hǣle heofonlice forgiefen wæs.

### Supplied help

Follow the shifts from what the travellers see to Æthelwald’s actions and back to the travellers. The complete expressions below are supplied. The sea remains masculine in these phrases, as in the earlier paradigm.

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S01 -->

**1. Ðā wæs æfter langum fæce ðæt.** Read the whole opening as “then, after a long time, it happened that ...”. Æfter governs the dative; langum fæce means a long interval. The opening ðæt belongs to this supplied time-introduction frame.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S02 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S02 -->

**2. wē ūre gesihð feorr ūpāhōfon.** Gesihð is feminine accusative singular, sight/gaze; ūre agrees with it. Feorr, far off. Ūpāhōfon, we lifted up, first plural preterite of ūpāhebban. The complete phrase describes looking up and seeing at a distance.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S03 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S03 -->

**3. in Farne ... of his dīegelnessum ūtgangende.** In Farne, on Farne island, with ðǣm īglande as dative apposition. Gode ðone lēofan fæder means the father dear to God: the adjective’s complement is dative. Æðelwald is in apposition to the accusative fæder. Of + dative means out of/from; dīegelnessum is the source plural for retreat/seclusion. Ūtgangende is the printed participle, coming out; Cook notes that a present participle is used for a Latin past participle here, so “having come out” is also possible.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S04 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S04 -->

**4. ðæt hē wolde ... hwæt ūs gelumpe.** The ðæt clause gives his purpose. Wolde with the infinitives scēawian and gesēon is read here “that he might watch and see” or “in order to watch and see”; do not make private desire the only force. Hwæt ūs gelumpe is a dependent question, what might happen to us. Gelumpe is third singular preterite subjunctive, and ūs is dative.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S05 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S05 -->

**5. for-ðon hē gehīerde ... weallendes sǣs.** For-ðon is the shorter source spelling of the causal conjunction. Gehīerde, heard, is a prefixed past form. Gebrec is the accusative noise/crash; ðǣra storma is genitive plural of storms, and ðæs weallendes sǣs genitive singular of the surging sea. Weallendes is an adjectival participle, surging/boiling. Ðǣra preserves the source genitive-plural spelling.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S06 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S06 -->

**6. Mid-ðȳ ... in gewinne and in ōrmōdnesse gesętte bēon.** The temporal clause contains both scēawode and geseah. With geseah, understand the earlier ūs as the people seen: he saw us to be situated in distress and despair. In governs the two datives gewinne and ōrmōdnesse. Gesętte is plural past participle here, not the identically spelled finite past; bēon is infinitive. This entire perception complement is supplied.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S07 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S07 -->

**7. ðā biegde hē his cnēowu tō Fæder ... Crīstes.** Biegde, bent, is the exact third singular preterite spelling; cnēowu, knees, accusative plural. Tō Fæder marks the direction of the prayer. Ūres Dryhtnes Hǣlendes Crīstes is a genitive group: of our Lord Jesus Christ, with the proper title Hǣlend and Crīst in apposition.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S08 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S08 -->

**8. wæs gebiddende for ūre hǣle and for ūrum līfe.** Cook143 note1 refers to142 note7: this was + participle wording is read as a simple past here, “prayed.” Do not infer a universal English progressive rule. For ūre hǣle, for our safety/rescue, uses accusative; for ūrum līfe, for our life, uses dative. Keep both exact constructions supplied.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S09 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S09 -->

**9. mid-ðȳ ... ðæt gebed gefylde ... samod ætgædere ge.** Gebed is prayer, accusative singular; gefylde is the already taught finished/accomplished. Samod ætgædere means together/at once. Paired ge ... ge coordinates both ... and; the first ge introduces the sea-calming and the second the storm-stilling. The narrated hē remains Æthelwald, the person who has just completed the prayer.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S10 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S10 -->

**10. ðone āðundnan sǣ gesmylte ge ðone storm gestilde.** Āðundnan, swollen, is a weak participial adjective agreeing with masculine accusative sǣ. Gesmylte, calmed, is third singular preterite; storm is accusative singular. Gestilde retains the taught transitive quell/settle sense here.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S11 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S11 -->

**11. tō ðon ðætte ðurh eall sēo rēðnes ... wæs blinnende.** Tō ðon ðætte introduces the result, so that. Ðurh eall is the exact adverbial expression completely/entirely. Rēðnes, fury, is the feminine subject; ðæs stormes is genitive. Wæs blinnende is the supplied periphrasis “ceased” here, with present participle blinnende; no new productive progressive tense is introduced.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S12 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S12 -->

**12. gesyndge windas ... æt lande gebrōhton.** Gesyndge is the exact syncopated source form beside taught gesyndige, favorable, nominative plural. Smyltestan is the weak masculine accusative superlative calmest/very calm. Ðurh governs ðone ... sǣ; ūs is the accusative people brought. Æt lande means to/at land in this exact arrival expression. Gebrōhton is the prefixed third plural preterite brought.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S13 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S13 -->

**13. Mid-ðȳ wē ... fram ðǣm ȳðum ūp ābǣron.** The temporal clause has the two completed actions: we came up onto land and carried our ship up from the waves. Lande is the locally supplied dative of land; ȳðum is dative plural waves after fram. Ābǣron is first plural preterite of prefixed carry. Ūre scip is the accusative object, not a new subject.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S14 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S14 -->

**14. ðā sōna ... se-ðe for ūrum intingan medmicel fæc gestilde.** Sōna, immediately; ęft, again; hwearf, returned. Se-ðe is the exact relative complex referring to masculine storm. For ūrum intingan, for our sake; intingan is dative singular. Medmicel fæc is accusative duration, a short interval. Gestilde is intransitive ceased/became still in this relative clause; the earlier transitive sense alone is insufficient. English had ceased may clarify the sequence.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S15 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S15 -->

**15. and ealne ðone dæg ... ðætte męnn sweotollice ongietan meahton ðætte.** Ealne ðone dæg is accusative duration, all that day. Strang, strong, agrees with storm. The first ðætte introduces the result: people could clearly understand. Sweotollice, clearly; ongietan, understand, is the infinitive governed by meahton. The second ðætte introduces what they understand, a content clause.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S16 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S16 -->

**16. se medmicla fierst ðǣre stilnesse, ðe ðǣr becōm.** Fierst is masculine nominative singular, interval/period; medmicla is its weak adjective, short. Ðǣre stilnesse, of the calm, is genitive singular. In the relative clause, ðǣr means there and becōm means occurred/came about in this context. Relative ðe is the subject of becōm and refers to the interval; it is distinct from the following adverb ðǣr.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U11-GRAM-NEW-U11-SRC-S17 -->

<!-- help: U11-SRC-HELP-S17 -->

**17. tō bēnum ðæs Godes weres ... heofonlice forgiefen wæs.** Tō bēnum, at/in response to the prayers, uses dative plural. Ðæs Godes weres means of the man of God; weres is genitive singular, with Godes depending on it. For intingan ūre hǣle means for the sake of our rescue: hǣle is genitive singular; exact ūre is supplied in this possessive expression. Heofonlice means from heaven; forgiefen means given/granted here, expressly not forgiven. The final passive refers to the brief period of calm. Here ūre is the first-person plural genitive used attributively within “our rescue”; this supplied phrase does not establish a new feminine possessive-adjective variant.
