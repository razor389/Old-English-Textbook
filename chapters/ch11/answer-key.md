# Unit 11. Answer key

These answers preserve each task's meaning and stated construction. Other grammatical word orders and documented forms are acceptable. Where a task specifies a conjunction, selected paradigm or viewpoint, distinguish a valid alternative from an answer that meets that particular target. Equivalent English translations need not reproduce the model's wording.

The key covers all 92 tasks. Cook's three complete sets retain their original English prompts and numbering; the Old English answers and explanations are new teaching material. The fourteen translated teaching examples are not additional exercises. [1]

In complete form tasks, supply every labeled cell even when several cells share a spelling. In translations, agreement follows grammatical gender and number. The new participles are base forms: the earlier bare-predicate passive permits the uninflected bases used below, including with plural subjects. Correctly evidenced inflected predicates may also be valid, but an extra ending is not required merely because the subject is plural.

Mood must be read with the clause's function and context. A purpose is an intended outcome; an asserted result is presented as occurring. A concessive subjunctive can admit a fact. A preterite subjunctive in the stated contrary-present condition refers to the present. Shared spellings, especially first-singular forms, do not identify mood by themselves. The local Cook and reading helps remain available: supplied expressions are not tests of unaided recall. [2]

## A. Clause analysis

**A01. Past when and then.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-A01 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-A01 -->

**Ðā se munuc cōm, ðā sealde se mann him wæter.**

When the monk came, the man gave him water.

The subordinate temporal clause is **ðā se munuc cōm**; cōm is third-singular preterite indicative. The main clause is **ðā sealde se mann him wæter**; sealde is third-singular preterite indicative in this factual narration. The first ðā means when; the second correlates with it as then. English may omit then. Him is the monk, the dative recipient. Neither ðā is an article here, and the spelling sealde alone does not prove subjunctive mood.

**A02. Repeated visits.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-A02 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-A02 -->

**Ðonne gē cumað, ðonne helpe ic ēow.**

Whenever you come, I help you.

**Ðonne gē cumað** is the temporal subordinate clause, with second-plural present indicative cumað. The second ðonne is the main-clause then; helpe is first-singular present indicative in the stated recurring situation. Ēow is the plural dative person helped. When you come, then I help you is equivalent. Translating ðonne as than misses the temporal relation; the first-singular spelling helpe alone cannot distinguish indicative from subjunctive.

**A03. Simultaneous actions.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-A03 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-A03 -->

**(a) Ðā-hwīle-ðe hē ǣt, ic bær wæter.**

**(b) Mid-ðȳ hē ǣt, ic bær wæter.**

Both statements mean **While he ate, I carried water**.

Ðā-hwīle-ðe and mid-ðȳ introduce the accompanying temporal circumstance. Ǣt is third-singular and bær first-singular preterite indicative. Neither sentence by itself says that the eating caused the carrying. As he was eating is an idiomatic English alternative; it does not change the Old English simple-preterite analysis. Because would assert a causal relation that these words do not themselves express.

**A04. Before and until an anticipated arrival.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-A04 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-A04 -->

**(a) Ne far, ǣr ic cume.**

**(b) Wes hēr, oð-ðæt ic cume.**

Do not go before I come. Be here until I come.

Far and wes are singular commands. In each selected temporal clause, cume is first-singular present subjunctive: the coming is still anticipated. Its shape is identical to the first-singular present indicative, so context and construction establish this analysis. Stay here until I arrive is an equivalent English rendering. No distinct future ending or willan is required, and the spelling cume alone does not settle mood.

**A05. A stated reason.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-A05 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-A05 -->

**Ic helpe ðē, for-ðon-ðe ðū eart untrum.**

I help you because you are sick.

**For-ðon-ðe ðū eart untrum** gives the reason; **ic helpe ðē** is the action explained by it. Eart is second-singular present indicative. Ðē is the dative person helped. Since you are sick is equivalent when since is causal. Reversing reason and action changes the statement, and so that would turn a stated fact into an intended purpose.

**A06. Three functions of ðæt.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-A06 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-A06 -->

**(a) Ic wāt ðæt se munuc cymð.**

**(b) Se munuc cymð, ðæt hē mē helpe.**

**(c) Se munuc is swā gōd, ðæt hē mē hilpð.**

| Sentence | Translation | Function and finite verb |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | I know that the monk comes. | Content; cymð, third-singular present indicative. |
| (b) | The monk comes so that he may help me. | Intended purpose; helpe, third-singular present subjunctive. |
| (c) | The monk is so good that he helps me. | Asserted result; hilpð, third-singular present indicative. |

The first clause supplies what is known; the second states why he comes; the third presents helping as the result. Comes/is coming and so that he may help/in order to help are defensible English choices. They do not make the three Old English clauses interchangeable. The spelling ðæt neither fixes the function nor requires subjunctive mood in every occurrence.

**A07. An outcome to prevent.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-A07 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-A07 -->

**Se mann hēold ðæt dēor, ðȳ-lǣs-ðe hit ðone męte nōme.**

The man held the animal lest it take the food.

Equivalently: he held it so that it would not take the food. **Ðȳ-lǣs-ðe hit ðone męte nōme** gives the negative purpose; nōme is third-singular preterite subjunctive. The intended prevention concerns the taking, and the clause does not assert that taking occurred. The conjunction supplies the negative purpose. Negating the main verb would instead say that the man did not hold the animal.

**A08. A concession that admits a fact.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-A08 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-A08 -->

**Ðēah hē sīe untrum, hē hilpð mē.**

Although he is sick, he helps me.

Sīe is third-singular present subjunctive of wesan. The concessive clause admits the illness as a circumstance despite which he helps. It is compatible with the explicitly accepted fact of his illness; subjunctive does not mean false. Though or even though gives the same relation. If would instead leave the illness as a condition and would miss this task's stated concession.

**A09. An open condition and an absent person.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-A09 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-A09 -->

**(a) Gif hē is hēr, hē hilpð mē.**

**(b) Gif hē wǣre hēr, hē hulpe mē.**

| Sentence | Translation | Finite forms |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | If he is here, he helps me. | Is and hilpð: present indicative, third singular. |
| (b) | If he were here, he would help me. | Wǣre and hulpe: preterite subjunctive, third singular. |

In (a), his presence remains open; a forward-looking he will help me can also fit its consequence. In (b), the stipulated absence is now, so both preterite subjunctives concern a contrary-present situation. They do not assert a past visit. Gif alone requires neither this subjunctive pair nor the indicative pair; English would does not compel an added willan.

**A10. Question, temporal clause and comparison.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-A10 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-A10 -->

**(a) Hwonne cymð se munuc?**

**(b) Ðonne se munuc cymð, ic helpe him.**

**(c) Se munuc is ieldra ðonne se fiscere.**

When does the monk come? When the monk comes, I help him. The monk is older than the fisherman.

Hwonne asks a direct question. The first ðonne introduces a subordinate temporal clause; the last ðonne introduces the comparison standard. In that comparison se fiscere is nominative, with the repeated copula understood. When is the monk coming? and whenever the monk comes are defensible contextual renderings of the first two. A single when or than gloss cannot account for all three constructions.

**A11. Who performs the intended action?**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-A11 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-A11 -->

**(a) Se fiscere cōm tō helpanne ðǣm męnn.**

**(b) Se fiscere cōm, ðæt se munuc ðǣm męnn hulpe.**

The fisherman came to help the man. The fisherman came so that the monk might help the man.

In the first sentence the understood helper in **tō helpanne** is the fisherman, the subject of cōm. In the second, **se munuc** is the explicit subject of finite hulpe, third-singular preterite subjunctive. **Ðǣm męnn** is the dative person helped in both. In order to and in order that are equivalent English renderings. Making the fisherman the helper in the second sentence would ignore its expressed purpose-clause subject.

**A12. The day when something happened.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-A12 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-A12 -->

**Hit is se dæg ðe hē cōm.**

It is the day when he came.

Equivalently: it is the day on which he came. **Se dæg** is the temporal antecedent of relative ðe; cōm is third-singular preterite indicative. The clause identifies the day by the event occurring on it. It asks no question and sets no condition. Replacing ðe with interrogative hwonne merely because both can be translated when would lose the relative construction.

## T. Controlled composition

**T01. When the father came.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-T01 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-T01 -->

Translate using past ðā … ðā: When the father came, the son gave him water.

**Ðā se fæder cōm, ðā sealde se sunu him wæter.**

Cōm is third-singular preterite; sealde is the factual past giving. The father is the first clause's subject and the dative recipient him; the son gives. **Ðǣm fæder** can replace him, and grammatical clause orders may differ, but both requested ðā words must remain. Reversing giver and recipient changes the event.

**T02. A recurring welcome.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-T02 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-T02 -->

Translate as a recurring event, using ðonne … ðonne: When the fishermen come, the king gives them food.

**Ðonne ðā fisceras cumað, ðonne sęlð se cyning him męte.**

Cumað agrees with plural fisceras; sęlð agrees with singular cyning. Him is dative plural, the fishermen receiving food. An explicit **ðǣm fiscerum** is equivalent. The specified ðonne … ðonne presents a recurring relation; past-event ðā or singular cymð would fail the requested meaning or agreement.

**T03. While the fishermen sat.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-T03 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-T03 -->

Translate with mid-ðȳ: While the fishermen sat in the house, the son carried water.

**Mid-ðȳ ðā fisceras sǣton on ðǣm hūse, se sunu bær wæter.**

Sǣton is third-plural preterite; bær is third-singular preterite. On ðǣm hūse is the stationary location, with dative hūse. A correlating ðā can begin the main clause. Ðā-hwīle-ðe could express the same relation, but the task expressly requests mid-ðȳ. The carrying is simultaneous with the sitting, not its intended purpose.

**T04. Before I come.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-T04 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-T04 -->

Address one person. The speaker has not yet arrived. Translate with ǣr: Do not go before I come.

**Ne far, ǣr ic cume.**

Far is the command to one person. Cume is first-singular present subjunctive in this selected anticipated-before clause, although its surface also occurs in the indicative. A different grammatical order is acceptable; plural farað would address the wrong number. The arrival is expected, so an English future setting does not require an extra future auxiliary.

**T05. Until the fishermen come.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-T05 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-T05 -->

Address several people. The fishermen have not yet arrived. Translate with oð-ðæt: Be in the house until the fishermen come.

**Wesað on ðǣm hūse, oð-ðæt ðā fisceras cumen.**

Wesað addresses several people; **bēoð** is also an available plural command. On ðǣm hūse has the locative dative. Cumen is third-plural present subjunctive in the selected anticipated-until clause; ordinary indicative cumað would not express the specified mood target. Singular wes or cume would lose a required number distinction.

**T06. Because the man is thirsty.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-T06 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-T06 -->

Translate with for-ðon-ðe and the adjective ðurstig: The monk gives the man water because the man is thirsty.

**Se munuc sęlð ðǣm męnn wæter, for-ðon-ðe se mann is ðurstig.**

The man receiving water is dative **ðǣm męnn**; the causal clause has nominative **se mann** and indicative is. Masculine singular ðurstig is the agreeing predicate. A clear hē may replace the repeated se mann, and clause order may vary. Do not make the person receiving water accusative or use a purpose subjunctive for this stated reason.

**T07. A different subject in the purpose clause.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-T07 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-T07 -->

Translate with a finite ðæt purpose clause: The fishermen came so that the monk might help them.

**Ðā fisceras cōmon, ðæt se munuc him hulpe.**

Cōmon has the plural fishermen as subject; hulpe is third-singular preterite subjunctive with se munuc. Him is dative plural, the people helped; **ðǣm fiscerum** makes the recipient explicit. An ordinary same-subject purpose infinitive would make the fishermen the helpers and would miss the required finite clause with a different subject.

**T08. An actual result.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-T08 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-T08 -->

Translate the result as an actual past event, using swā … ðæt: The men were so hungry that they ate the food.

**Ðā męnn wǣron swā hungrige, ðæt hīe ǣton ðone męte.**

Hungrige is strong masculine nominative plural, agreeing with męnn. The eating is asserted to have happened: **ǣton** is third-plural preterite indicative. Hīe may be replaced by a clear repeated noun phrase. A purpose interpretation would change the given result, and singular hungrig would lose the selected adjective agreement.

**T09. Preventing the animals from taking food.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-T09 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-T09 -->

Translate with ðȳ-lǣs-ðe: The fishermen held the animals, lest the animals take our food.

**Ðā fisceras hēoldon ðā dēor, ðȳ-lǣs-ðe ðā dēor ūrne męte nōmen.**

Hēoldon is third-plural preterite indicative; nōmen is third-plural preterite subjunctive in the negative purpose. Ūrne agrees with singular masculine accusative męte, not with the animals. A clear hīe may replace the second ðā dēor. The conjunction means lest/so that not; the sentence does not assert that the animals actually took the food.

**T10. Illness admitted in a concession.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-T10 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-T10 -->

The monk was in fact sick. Translate using the selected concessive pattern: Although the monk was sick, he helped the man.

**Ðēah se munuc wǣre untrum, hē healp ðǣm męnn.**

Wǣre is third-singular preterite subjunctive in the concession; healp is third-singular preterite indicative in the stated event. **Ðēah ðe** is a documented alternative to ðēah. The person helped is dative ðǣm męnn. The subjunctive does not deny the factual illness; replacing healp with hulpe would change the main clause's presentation.

**T11. The king is absent now.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-T11 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-T11 -->

The king is absent now. Translate the contrary-present condition: If the king were here, he would help us.

**Gif se cyning wǣre hēr, hē hulpe ūs.**

Both wǣre and hulpe are third-singular preterite subjunctives, referring here to the stated present situation. Ūs is the dative plural person helped. A grammatical reversal of clause order is acceptable. A factual past rendering or an added willan based solely on English would would miss the selected contrary-present construction.

## Cook, Group IV, Exercise 16

**16.1.**

<!-- item: EX-IV-16-01 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-IV-16-01 -->

Gold is more precious (64) than silver.

**Gold is dēorwierðre ðonne seolfor.**

Dēorwierðre is the weak neuter nominative singular comparative agreeing with mass noun gold. Seolfor is the parallel nominative standard after ðonne. A grammatical comparison using the taught genitive standard is also defensible. Positive dēorwierðe alone would omit more; a superlative would change the comparison.

**16.2.**

<!-- item: EX-IV-16-02 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-IV-16-02 -->

Righteousness is more precious than gold.

**Rihtwīsnes is dēorwierðre ðonne gold.**

Dēorwierðre is weak feminine nominative singular with rihtwīsnes. Its identical surface to 16.1 does not make the subject neuter. The standard gold has its own gender; it does not control the predicate adjective. A taught genitive comparison is an alternative, but more must remain comparative.

**16.3.**

<!-- item: EX-IV-16-03 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-IV-16-03 -->

The bath is hot.

**Ðæt bæð is hāt.**

Bæð and its article are neuter nominative singular; hāt is the agreeing strong predicate. Predicate-first Hāt is ðæt bæð preserves the meaning. Masculine se or plural baðu would fail the subject's gender or number.

**16.4.**

<!-- item: EX-IV-16-04 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-IV-16-04 -->

The baths are hot.

**Ðā baðu sindon hāt.**

Baðu is the selected neuter plural, so the copula is plural sindon. Long-stem hāt has an endingless strong neuter nominative plural. A grammatical predicate-first order is equivalent. The absence of an adjective ending does not make hāt singular; do not add masculine plural -e by analogy.

**16.5.**

<!-- item: EX-IV-16-05 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-IV-16-05 -->

The animals are living.

**Ðā dēor sindon cwicu.**

Cwicu means alive/living and agrees with neuter plural dēor. English are living permits this ordinary adjective predicate; it does not require a new progressive construction. Dēor is unchanged in nominative singular and plural, but the article, copula and predicate establish the plural here. Do not replace cwicu with a singular predicate.

**16.6.**

<!-- item: EX-IV-16-06 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-IV-16-06 -->

Some pearls are black.

**Suma męregrēotan sindon blaca.**

Męregrēotan is nominative plural of the weak feminine pearl noun. Suma and blaca are the selected strong feminine plural forms; blaca uses a before the vowel ending. A different grammatical order is acceptable. Masculine plural blace, used of men in 17.7, would give the wrong agreement here.

**16.7.**

<!-- item: EX-IV-16-07 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-IV-16-07 -->

The island is beautiful.

**Ðæt īgland is fæger.**

Īgland is neuter nominative singular and fæger its strong predicate. Fæger is ðæt īgland preserves the meaning with predicate first. The English ending of island offers no guide to Old English gender; masculine se would fail the selected noun agreement.

**16.8.**

<!-- item: EX-IV-16-08 -->

## R. The fisherman and his son

**R01. Complete translation.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-R01 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-R01 -->

The old fisherman came to the house so that he might help his son. When he came, the son was hungry. The fisherman gave him food. While the son ate, the fisherman carried water. Although the fisherman was old, he helped his son. The son said, “Be here until I come.” He went away and came back and gave his father water.

**Interpretation:** Preserve the purpose of the first arrival, the simultaneous eating and carrying, the concession about age, and the son's answering act of help. In order to help his son, as the son ate and stay here are equivalent English choices. Came back is a contextual rendering of cōm after the departure, not a separate return prefix. The final he is most naturally the son in this sequence; masculine gender alone does not rule out another referent. Do not omit the final gift or turn the admitted age into a condition.

**R02. Three relations in the account.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-R02 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-R02 -->

| Relation | Clause | Contribution and finite form |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | ðæt hē his suna hulpe | Why the fisherman came; hulpe is third-singular preterite subjunctive. |
| Simultaneous time | Mid-ðȳ se sunu ǣt | The son eats while his father carries water; ǣt is third-singular preterite indicative. |
| Concession | Ðēah se fiscere wǣre eald | Age is admitted despite which the help occurs; wǣre is third-singular preterite subjunctive. |

Purpose/intention, while/as and although/though are equivalent labels or translations when these distinctions remain. Calling all three clauses conditions loses their separate functions. The concessive subjunctive does not make the father's age false.

**R03. Givers, recipients and possessors.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-R03 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-R03 -->

The fisherman gives the first food, and his son receives it. **Se fiscere** explicitly names that giver; **him** is the dative recipient understood as the son. In **his suna**, his refers to the fisherman and suna is the dative son helped.

At the end, the son is the natural subject of **hē … sealde**, and his father receives the water. **His fæder** is a dative phrase; fæder has the selected endingless dative singular, while his is the son's genitive possessor. The pronouns' forms alone do not identify one of the two men uniquely. A reasoned alternative antecedent analysis should be distinguished from the account's intended reciprocal sequence, rather than rejected as morphologically impossible. Treating endingless fæder as necessarily nominative would make the recipient a new subject without justification.

**R04. The son’s expected return.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-R04 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-R04 -->

**Ic** is the son, whose speech is introduced by **se sunu cwæð**. When he tells his father **wes hēr**, his own coming is still expected. **Cume** is first-singular present subjunctive in the selected anticipated-until clause, formally identical to first-singular indicative cume. The later **cōm**, after **gewāt**, fulfills that expectation on the natural reading of the final hē as the son.

Until I arrive is an equivalent rendering. The first person belongs to the quoted speaker, not to the narrator or father; a claim that the ending of cume by itself proves the mood would overstate the morphology.

## S. Æthelwald calms the sea

**S01. Complete translation.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-S01 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-S01 -->

Then, after a long time, when we raised our gaze from a distance, we saw on Farne, the island, the father Æthelwald, dear to God, coming out of his secluded place, so that he might watch our journey and see what might happen to us, for he heard the crashing of the storms and of the seething sea.

When he then also looked at us and saw us placed in distress and despair, he bent his knees to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ and prayed for our safety and our life. And when he completed that prayer, he then at the same time both calmed the swollen sea and stilled the storm, so that the violence of the storm ceased entirely, and favorable winds brought us to land through the very calm sea. When we then came up onto land and also carried our ship up from the waves, the same storm soon returned and came again; it had ceased for a short time for our sake, and it was very great and strong all that day. Thus people could clearly understand that the short period of calm which had occurred there was granted from heaven at the prayers of that man of God for our rescue.

**Interpretation:** The opening can be recast as After a long time we lifted our gaze and saw …, provided the distant seeing and temporal sequence remain. Æthelwald is the person seen; **Gode** belongs with **lēofan**, dear to God. Our Lord and Saviour Christ is an alternative rendering of the title group. Our lives is idiomatic English for the collective situation, although līfe is morphologically singular.

The subject **hē** in the paired calming and stilling actions remains Æthelwald. The narrative attributes efficacy to his prayer; a translation should not silently replace the grammatical subject with God. Prayed and ceased are valid simple-past renderings of **wæs gebiddende** and **wæs blinnende**, as Cook's notes permit. A contextual English progressive is possible, but is not forced by the participle's form. Keep the landing, ship carried ashore, storm's return and final inference. Final **forgiefen** means given/granted, not forgiven. [5]

**S02. The order of the rescue.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-S02 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-S02 -->

- The sailors see Æthelwald.
- Æthelwald prays.
- The sea becomes calm.
- The sailors reach land.
- The storm returns.

Before its return they have both come ashore and carried their ship up from the waves. Reach land/come ashore are equivalent descriptions, but the ship's removal is a separate completed action in the same temporal clause. Putting the renewed storm before the landing, or stopping the episode at the calm, loses the resolution.

**S03. Purpose, question and reason.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-S03 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-S03 -->

The purpose begins **ðæt hē wolde ūrne sīðfæt scēawian, and gesēon hwæt ūs gelumpe**: so that he might watch our journey and see what might happen to us. The local help supplies wolde with its two infinitives in this purpose use; in order to watch and see is also an apt translation. **Hwæt ūs gelumpe** is the dependent question inside that purpose, not a second outer purpose clause.

The reason begins **for-ðon hē gehīerde ðæt gebrec ðǣra storma and ðæs weallendes sǣs**: because he heard the crashing of the storms and seething sea. It states the circumstance prompting him to come out. A rendering with since is valid if clearly causal. Reducing wolde to private desire alone, or treating the heard noise as an intended outcome, would lose the contrast.

**S04. The narrated result.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-S04 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-S04 -->

**Tō ðon ðætte** introduces the result: the storm's violence ceased entirely, and favorable winds brought the sailors to land through the very calm sea. The sailors are **ūs**, the accusative people brought; **gesyndge windas** is the subject of **gebrōhton**.

The supplied **wæs blinnende** can be translated ceased. Was ceasing may be defensible English in context, but no progressive translation is required merely by the present participle. The two coordinated outcomes are narrated as results, not just things Æthelwald hoped would happen. Leaving out the winds would obscure the expressed agent of the bringing.

**S05. Where the temporal clauses end.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-S05 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-S05 -->

The first requested clause extends from **Mid-ðȳ hē ðā ūs ēac scēawode** through **in gewinne and in ōrmōdnesse gesętte bēon**. It includes both his looking at them and his seeing their plight. The main action begins **ðā biegde hē his cnēowu**, then he bent his knees.

The second extends from **Mid-ðȳ wē ðā ūp cōmon tō lande** through **fram ðǣm ȳðum ūp ābǣron**. It includes coming ashore and carrying the ship up. The main action begins **ðā sōna se ilca storm ęft hwearf and cōm**, then the same storm soon returned and came again.

When or while can represent mid-ðȳ as context permits. The relevant boundaries, rather than one fixed English conjunction, are the target. Ending the second subordinate clause at lande wrongly detaches the ship's removal from the circumstances completed before the storm returns.

**S06. Wæs gebiddende.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-S06 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-S06 -->

Here **wæs gebiddende** can be translated **prayed**. Cook's note points back to the explanation that this be-plus-present-participle construction can have simple-past force. **Was praying** is an idiomatic contextual alternative, but the present participle does not require the English progressive category.

The auxiliary wæs gives the past setting; calling the whole event present because gebiddende is a present participle would confuse nonfinite form with finite tense. This local explanation supports the source passage without making every Old English be-plus-participle phrase equivalent to an English progressive. [5]

**S07. The meaning of the final inference.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-S07 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-S07 -->

People understand that the brief calm was granted from heaven, at the prayers of the man of God, for the sailors' rescue. **Ðæs Godes weres** identifies whose prayers are meant: Godes depends on weres, not on bēnum as though God were the person praying. **Ūre** in **for intingan ūre hǣle** identifies the sailors whose rescue is at issue.

Given/granted from heaven and for our safety/rescue are defensible translations here. Cook explicitly explains final **forgiefen** as given; forgiven would introduce a meaning that this clause does not express. The content of the inference concerns the specially granted interval of calm, not a permanent end to all storms.

**S08. Why the storm returns in the account.**

<!-- item: NEW-U11-S08 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U11-S08 -->

The calm is temporary. After the sailors and their ship are ashore, the same storm returns and remains very great and strong throughout that day. Its short suspension precisely when they needed rescue supports the narrator's explanation that the calm was granted in response to prayer for their safety.

A paraphrase such as the storm paused long enough for their escape preserves the point. Saying that the storm ended permanently conflicts with its explicit return; saying that the narrator sees no relation to the prayer omits the final inference.
