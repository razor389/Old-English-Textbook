## M. Meanings and infinitive complements

### M1

**Ic cann swimman. Wē cunnon singan.**

Cann and cunnon express learned ability, respectively 1 sg. and 1 pl. present indicative. Swimman and singan are plain infinitives.

### M2

**Hē mæg ðæt wæter beran. Hīe magon ðǣm męnn helpan.**

Mæg/magon express capability. Wæter is the accusative thing carried; męnn is dative singular because helpan takes the person helped in the dative. The course’s hooked ę is retained in męnn.

### M3

**Ic ðearf gān. Wē ðorfton gān.**

The first finite verb is present 1 sg.; the second is preterite 1 pl. Both select the same plain infinitive gān.

### M4

**Ðū dearst gān. Ðū dorstest gān.**

These are second-singular present and preterite indicative forms of “dare,” addressing one person. The second sentence states past daring, without adding a condition.

### M5

**Wē sculon ðæt weorc dōn. Hīe sceoldon ðæt weorc dōn.**

Sculon is present 1 pl. and sceoldon preterite 3 pl. in these clauses. The intended senses are present obligation and actual past obligation; dōn stays a plain infinitive.

### M6

**Ðū mōst ðone męte etan. Ðū mōstest ðone męte etan.**

Both finite verbs address one person. Mōst expresses present permission; mōstest expresses actual past permission. Męte is masculine accusative singular, hence ðone.

### M7

**Hē wille ðǣm fuglum wæter giefan. Hīe woldon ðǣm fuglum wæter giefan.**

Wille is present 3 sg.; woldon is preterite 3 pl. The birds are the dative recipient and water the accusative thing given. Bare wæter represents the English mass noun “water.” Sęllan would also express giving within the taught frame.

### M8

**Ic ne dorste gān. Wē ne magon ðæt scip beran.**

Ne precedes each finite verb. Dorste is past 1 sg.; magon is present 1 pl. The second sentence denies ability, not permission.

### M9

**Wit cunnon swimman. Git sculon unc helpan.**

Wit identifies “we two,” git “you two,” and dative unc “us two.” Dual subjects require plural finite verbs; helpan’s object remains dative.

### M10

**Hē wolde gān. Hē ēode.**

The first sentence says that he wanted to go. It does not assert fulfillment of the desire. The second directly reports his going as a past event. Within the taught intransitive vocabulary, **Hē wolde faran. Hē fōr.** is another defensible rendering.

### M11

**Ic cann swimman:** “I know how to swim,” emphasizing learned skill. **Ic mæg swimman:** “I can/am able to swim,” emphasizing capability in the relevant situation.

Cann and mæg are the finite present-indicative first-singular verbs. Swimman is the plain infinitive in each clause. English “can” may translate either; the distinction comes from the intended Old English sense and context, not an absolute one-to-one English word rule.

### M12

**Ðæt scip bið gearu.** — “The ship will be ready,” in the stated expected-later context.

**Ðæt scip is gearu.** — “The ship is ready.”

Bið is the bēo-series 3 sg. present indicative; is is the earlier-series counterpart. Gearu agrees with the neuter singular subject. The assigned context supports future-state translation; it does not make every bēo-series occurrence future.

### M13

**Ic wāt ðā ðing. Hē wiste ðā ðing.**

Ðā ðing is the accusative plural nominal object, referring to the stated matters already discussed. Wāt is present 1 sg.; wiste is past 3 sg. Witan here expresses knowing facts or matters. Cunnan with an infinitive expresses knowing how to perform an action.

## C. Food and water for the journey

### C1

The man asks for help. Two companions come, explicitly described as **twēgen gefēran**. Their stated purpose is to help, **tō helpanne**; the context identifies the man as the person requesting their help. The text does not say at this point that the requested work has already been completed.

### C2

| Imperative in the account | Addressee number in this context | Corresponding singular command |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cumað | second plural, the two companions | Cum |
| helpað | second plural, the two companions | help |
| Nimað | second plural, the two companions | Nim |
| berað | second plural, the two companions | ber |
| Giefað | second plural, the two companions | Gief |
| Sittað | second plural, the two companions | Site |
| etað | second plural, the two companions | et |

There are seven imperative tokens. The plural ending itself means multiple addressees; the narrative’s twēgen supplies the exact count of two.

### C3

Hine refers to **ðone męte**, the food, and is masculine accusative singular as the object of berað. It does not refer to the man merely because English would call the food “it.”

The water’s recipients are explicitly **ðǣm fuglum**, the birds, both in the command and in the later report of giving.

The narrative explicitly says that the companions ate, **ðā gefēran ǣton**, but gives no object in that sentence. The food already mentioned is the natural contextual inference. It is not an expressly repeated object, and the text does not say how much they ate or that they consumed all of it.

### C4

| Finite form in context | Parsing | Stated sense | Governed plain infinitive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| wille in Ic wille faran | present indicative 1 sg. | wish/want | faran |
| mæg in Ic mæg … beran | present indicative 1 sg. | capability | beran |
| sculon in Wē sculon … gefręmman | present indicative 1 pl. | obligation | gefręmman |

The printed subjects fix person where forms are shared. Each infinitive names the action of that finite verb’s own subject.

### C5

The man said, “I want to travel. My ship is ready. Come and help me.” Two companions came to help. The man said, “Take the food and carry it. Give the birds the water.” One of the companions said, “I can carry the food. We must perform the work.” The companions carried the food and gave the birds the water. The man said, “Sit and eat.” The companions ate. They travelled.

“The companion” is the closer literal rendering of **se gefēra**; “one of the companions” makes the discourse relation clear without adding another participant. Wille expresses desire, mæg ability, and sculon obligation. The commands request actions; the following bǣron, gēafon, ǣton, and fōron report actual past events. Final hīe most naturally resumes the companions; the sentence does not separately assert that the man joined their journey.

### C6

Commands to one companion: **Cum and help mē.**

With the first narrative reporting verb in the present and the original printed quotation retained:

**Se mann cwið: ‘Ic wille faran. Mīn scip is gearu. Cumað and helpað mē.’**

Only narrative cwæð changes to cwið. The quotation’s finite statements and plural commands remain as originally printed.

There is a minor instruction-reference ambiguity: after first asking for singular commands, “Then … keeping the quotation itself unchanged” could instead mean retain the just-revised quotation. On that reading the equally coherent transformed sentence is **Se mann cwið: ‘Ic wille faran. Mīn scip is gearu. Cum and help mē.’** The first answer above treats the two requests as separate operations on the original text. Neither reading licenses changing the quoted first-person verbs to third person.

## R. The fisherman’s gift and first direction

### R1

The fisherman sees the young man lying at his feet; that sight prompts his compassionate action. He divides his own cloak in two and gives Apollonius one half. The earlier actions also include lifting him up, leading him home, and setting provisions before him.

### R2

The relevant wording is **hē wolde … māran ārfæstnesse him gecȳðan**. Wolde is third-singular preterite indicative of willan in this clause, with the fisherman as subject. Gecȳðan is its supplied plain infinitive, “show.”

The fisherman wanted to show Apollonius greater kindness, according to his ability. The dative him is Apollonius, and accusative māran ārfæstnesse is the kindness to be shown. The supplied noun phrase be his mihte is not another finite modal.

### R3

| Source phrase | Identification | Familiar plain infinitive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tō bēodanne | tō plus inflected infinitive | bēodan, “offer” |
| tō sęllanne | tō plus inflected infinitive | sęllan, “give” |

The fisherman had provisions **available to offer** Apollonius and something **available to give** him. The supplied availability construction explains the relationship. Neither phrase means that the fisherman was compelled to offer or give; they are not modern obligation “have to.”

### R4

| Singular source command | Parsing in the speech | Plural command |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nim | niman, imperative 2 sg. | Nimað |
| gā | gān, imperative 2 sg. | gāð |

Both address the single recipient, Apollonius. Gā’s use here is a command, not a first-singular indicative statement.

### R5

**Cweðende** is the supplied present participle, “saying,” a nonfinite form accompanying the fisherman’s action. “Present participle” does not make the past narrative a present-tense account.

**Cwæð** is a finite preterite indicative form of “say,” as in se mann cwæð; in isolation its spelling is shared by first and third singular. **Gā** in the excerpt is a second-singular imperative directed to Apollonius.

It is **ðus cweðende**, “saying thus,” that introduces the fisherman’s actual words in this source. Cwæð is the comparison form, not the reporting word used there.

### R6

The fisherman says he has something available to give Apollonius. In the narrative context it is the half-cloak gift just described. His first complete spoken direction is: **“Take what I have to give you, and go into the town.”**

The supplied **ðæt ic ðē tō sęllanne hæbbe** is a definite free relative, “what/that which I have available to give you,” serving as the thing Apollonius is commanded to take. It is not merely a demonstrative followed by an independently supplied omitted noun, nor does it express an obligation to give. Ic is the fisherman and dative ðē is Apollonius. The support reads hæbbe here as first-singular indicative while acknowledging its isolated spelling is also shared with subjunctive forms.

### R7

The inflected-infinitive phrases are **tō bēodanne** and **tō sęllanne**. They contain a nonfinite verbal form after separate tō and belong to the supplied availability constructions.

**Tō his hūse** means “to his house,” the fisherman’s house, with supplied dative hūse. **Intō ðǣre ceastre** means “into the town,” with the actual supplied feminine dative phrase ðǣre ceastre. These are destination phrases containing nouns, not infinitives. The source’s motion does not license altering its cases or deducing a universal motion-preposition rule. The bound prefix in tōslāt is not an additional separate tō-plus-infinitive phrase.

### R8

Then, as soon as the fisherman saw that the young man was lying at his feet, he compassionately lifted him up and led him with him to his house, and set before him the provisions that he had available to offer him. Then he still wanted, according to his ability, to show him greater kindness; he then tore his cloak in two and gave Apollonius one half, saying thus: “Take what I have available to give you, and go into the town.”

The fisherman owns the feet, house, and cloak and performs these actions; the young man/Apollonius is lifted, led, fed, and given the gift. The relative and availability meanings are used as supplied, not reconstructed as obligation. The translation stops at the stated boundary and adds none of the continuing speech.

## X. Combining the constructions

### X1

**Wit woldon ðǣm fuglum uncerne męte giefan. Wit gēafon him hine.**

Wit keeps the two-person subject. Uncerne is the taught possessive “our two’s,” agreeing with masculine accusative singular męte; ordinary ūre would not preserve that particular possessor reference. Hine resumes the masculine food, while dative plural him resumes the birds. Woldon states the past desire; gēafon reports the completed gift. Sęllan/sealdon is another taught giving pair.

### X2

**Se ealda mann ne mæg ðæt micle scip beran. Helpað him.**

Ealda is weak masculine nominative singular after se; micle is weak neuter accusative singular after ðæt. Mæg denies the man’s ability in the negative clause. Helpað addresses several companions, and him is dative singular as helpan’s object.

### X3

**Hē cōm tō helpanne ūs. Wē woldon him męte giefan. Hē sæt and ǣt.**

Tō helpanne expresses the arriving man’s purpose; ūs is the dative person helped. Woldon plus plain giefan expresses our past desire; him is dative recipient and męte the accusative thing given. Sæt and the course-selected long-vowel ǣt report that he actually sat and ate. The third sentence has no explicit object of eating, matching the English.

### X4

**Gē gāð** is, in the stated comparison, a present-indicative statement: “You [plural] go/are going.” **Gāð** is a second-plural direct command: “Go.” The present indicative plural and imperative plural have the same spelling; their function follows the clause’s use and context. An expressed second-person subject can also accompany a command in other contexts, so the pronoun is not an infallible mood test.

**Hē dēð ðæt weorc** is a third-singular present-indicative statement, “He does/is doing the work.” **Dō ðæt weorc** is a second-singular command, “Do the work.” Here the forms themselves also differ: dēð versus dō. The accusative work remains the thing done in both.

### X5 — one connected composition

**Se mann cōm tō helpanne ðǣm gefērum.**

**Hīe woldon ðone męte beran.**

**‘Git berað ðone męte.’**

**Ðā gefēran bǣron hine.**

The third sentence is the man’s direct address to the companions. Git explicitly identifies his two addressees, keeping the composition within the lexical bank plus permitted articles/pronouns without adding a numeral. The two companions are the same group referred to by ðǣm gefērum, hīe, and ðā gefēran.

Translation: “The man came to help the companions. They wanted to carry the food. ‘You two, carry the food.’ The companions carried it.”

The three new constructions are: **(1)** same-subject purpose, cōm tō helpanne, with dative ðǣm gefērum; **(2)** past willingness/desire, woldon plus plain beran; **(3)** a second-plural direct imperative berað addressed to the dual subject git, expressed before the verb as the learner permits. Sentence four reports the completed carrying with bǣron; masculine accusative hine refers to męte. No reported-command clause or additional verbal complement is required.
