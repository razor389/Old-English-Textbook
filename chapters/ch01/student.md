# Unit 1. Nouns, the article, and the copula

## 1. Spelling and case

<!-- teaching: CH01-GRAM-ORTHOGRAPHY CH01-GRAM-GENDER CH01-GRAM-CASES -->

**Æ æ** is a single vowel letter. **Þ þ** (thorn) and **Ð ð** (eth) represent the sounds written *th* in modern English; the choice between the letters does not by itself distinguish the sounds of *thin* and *this*. This unit follows Cook's use of **ð**. His hooked **ę** is an editorial form of *e*, retained in such words as **męte** and **męnn**. A macron marks a long vowel or diphthong: **ā, ǣ, ē, ī, ō, ū, ȳ; ēa, ēo, īe**. Preserve the marks printed in each word; do not add them by analogy.[1]

Nouns have masculine, feminine, or neuter gender, independently of the sex or nature of the thing named. Learn the gender with the noun. A noun's declension determines its case endings; its nominative ending alone does not identify either gender or declension.

| Case | Uses introduced here |
|---|---|
| Nominative (N) | Subject; a noun identifying the subject after “be.” |
| Genitive (G) | A dependent expressing possession or another relation, often English “of.” |
| Dative (D) | Recipient; the noun after the selected prepositional constructions below. |
| Accusative (A) | Direct object. |

Singular and plural forms may coincide, and one spelling may serve several cases. Its construction and the agreement of accompanying words determine the analysis. The tables give all four cases, even when their forms are identical.[2]

## 2. The article

<!-- teaching: CH01-GRAM-ARTICLE -->

The definite article means **the**. It agrees with its own noun in gender, number, and case. The plural forms are common to all three genders.[3]

| Case | Masculine singular | Neuter singular | Feminine singular | All genders plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | se | ðæt | sēo | ðā |
| Genitive | ðæs | ðæs | ðǣre | ðāra |
| Dative | ðǣm | ðǣm | ðǣre | ðǣm |
| Accusative | ðone | ðæt | ðā | ðā |
| Instrumental: reference | ðȳ, ðon | ðȳ, ðon | as dative | as dative |

Recognize **ðȳ** and **ðon** as instrumental singular forms; their uses are not required in composition here. Nouns have no separate instrumental forms in these paradigms. The source also permits **ðām** for **ðǣm** and **ðǣra** for **ðāra**; the tables and exercises select **ðǣm** and **ðāra**.

## 4. Strong neuter nouns

<!-- teaching: CH01-GRAM-WEIGHT CH01-PAR-NEUT-WEIGHT -->

Neuter nominative and accusative are identical in each number. The genitive and dative endings match those of **fisc**. In the ordinary monosyllabic pattern, the nominative/accusative plural has **-u** after a short stem, but no ending after a heavy stem. A short vowel followed by one consonant gives the short pattern here; a long vowel or long diphthong, or a short vowel followed by two consonants, gives the heavy pattern. A doubled consonant counts as two. Thus **hof** has **hofu**, whereas **sǣd, gærs, cynn** have no added N/A plural ending. Vowel length and syllable weight are not the same thing.[5]

| Case | hof singular | hof plural | sǣd singular | sǣd plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | hof | hofu | sǣd | sǣd |
| Genitive | hofes | hofa | sǣdes | sǣda |
| Dative | hofe | hofum | sǣde | sǣdum |
| Accusative | hof | hofu | sǣd | sǣd |

The heavy pattern also covers **lēoht, līf, gēar, dēor, anginn, lyft, weorc**. In **anginn**, apply the rule to the stressed stem **-ginn**, with **nn**. Plural forms of mass or abstract nouns do not automatically have the same sense as the singular; the vocabulary below marks those whose plurals are only for recognizing the morphology.

## 8. The indicative copula

<!-- teaching: CH01-GRAM-COPULA -->

These are the complete present **eom** and preterite **wæs** series of the indicative “be.” The preterite expresses a past state. Other present forms, negative forms, moods, and nonfinite constructions will be treated separately.[9]

| Person and number | Present | Preterite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 sg.: I | eom | wæs |
| 2 sg.: you | eart | wǣre |
| 3 sg.: he, she, it | is | wæs |
| 1–3 pl.: we, you, they | sindon | wǣron |

English pronouns label the persons. **1–3 pl.** gives the shared form for “we,” plural “you,” and “they.” No Old English pronoun forms are required for this table. Cook also gives **sind, sint** beside **sindon**.

## 9. Basic constructions

<!-- teaching: CH01-GRAM-SUBJECT-PREDICATE CH01-GRAM-AGREEMENT -->

The subject is nominative. A noun that identifies it after “be” is also nominative; English **a/an** need not receive a separate word in such a predicate. The copula agrees with the subject in person and number.[10]

<!-- example: CH01-EXAMPLE07 -->

**Se hwæl is dēor.** — The whale is an animal.

<!-- teaching: CH01-GRAM-COORDINATION -->

**And** joins words or phrases. When definite noun phrases are joined, each article agrees with its own noun. Gender belongs to that noun; it is not taken from the other noun in the pair.

<!-- teaching: CH01-GRAM-ON -->

For the location of an entity, use **on + dative**, “in/on.”

<!-- example: CH01-EXAMPLE01 -->

**Se fisc is on ðǣm wætere.** — The fish is in the water.

<!-- example: CH01-EXAMPLE02 -->

**Ðā męnn wǣron on ðǣm hofum.** — The men were in the dwellings.

<!-- teaching: CH01-GRAM-GENITIVE -->

A dependent genitive expresses possession or a relation such as the source, contents, or kind of something. In the phrase pattern used for Cook's Exercise 3, place the genitive first and omit the article of the **head noun**, while retaining the article belonging to the dependent. The head remains in the case required by its own role. A proper name such as **God** takes no article here.[11]

<!-- example: CH01-EXAMPLE03 -->

**ðæs mannes fugol** — the man's bird

Here **ðæs mannes** is the dependent genitive; **fugol** is the head, nominative in the isolated naming phrase. The construction does not mean that every English “the” disappears.

<!-- teaching: CH01-GRAM-NESTED-GENITIVE -->

The same dependent may stand inside a prepositional phrase. Its genitive does not change the case required for the head.

<!-- example: CH01-EXAMPLE08 -->

**on ðæs mannes hofe** — in the man's dwelling

**Hofe** is dative after locative **on**; **ðæs mannes** remains genitive. **Ðæs** agrees with **mannes**, not with **hofe**.

<!-- teaching: CH01-GRAM-OBJECT -->

The direct object is accusative. In the following example, **geseah** is supplied: “saw,” third-person singular preterite. Its conjugation is not required here.[12]

<!-- example: CH01-EXAMPLE04 -->

**Se mann geseah ðone fisc.** — The man saw the fish.

**Mann** and **fisc** have identical nominative and accusative singular spellings, but their articles distinguish subject **se** from object **ðone**.

<!-- teaching: CH01-GRAM-RECIPIENT CH01-FRAME-GIVING -->

The recipient of a gift is dative; the thing given is accusative. The supplied **sealde** means “gave,” third-person singular preterite.[12]

<!-- example: CH01-EXAMPLE05 -->

**God sealde ðǣm fugle męte.** — God gave the bird food.

**Ðǣm fugle** is the recipient; **męte** is the direct object. English may express the recipient with “to,” but this Old English construction uses the dative without a preposition.

<!-- teaching: CH01-GRAM-FRAM -->

Use **fram + dative** for what something is separated from. The supplied **gedǣlde** means “separated/divided,” third-person singular preterite.[12]

<!-- example: CH01-EXAMPLE06 -->

**God gedǣlde ðæt lēoht fram ðǣm ðīestrum.** — God separated the light from the darkness.

The thing separated, **ðæt lēoht**, is accusative; **ðǣm ðīestrum** is dative after **fram**. These are particular constructions. They do not establish one case rule for all spatial prepositions.

## 16. The first day of Creation

Cook, *A First Book in Old English*, Reader I, pp.123–124. The following is a continuous extract through the close of the first day. Only note cues and printed line/page breaks have been removed.[15]

<!-- source: FB-I-CREATION-FIRST-DAY-01 -->

> On anginne gescēop God heofonan and eorðan. Sēo eorðe sōðlice wæs īdel and ǣmtig; and ðīestru wǣron ofer ðǣre nēowolnesse brādnesse; and Godes gāst wæs gefęred ofer wæteru. God cwæð ðā, “Geweorðe lēoht”; and lēoht wearð geworht. God geseah ðā ðæt hit gōd wæs; and hē gedǣlde ðæt lēoht fram ðǣm ðīestrum. And hēt ðæt lēoht Dæg, and ðā ðīestru Niht. Ðā wæs geworden ǣfen and morgen ān dæg.

### Supplied help

<!-- teaching: CH01-READ-SUPPORT CH01-GRAM-ORDER -->

Use this help for the questions and translation. It supplies the actual forms and constructions occurring here; their independent production is not required.

| Expression | Local meaning and analysis |
|---|---|
| **On anginne** | “In the beginning”: a supplied temporal use of **on**, with dative **anginne**. |
| **gescēop** | “Created,” third-person singular preterite. In the opening, the subject **God** follows the verb; word order alone does not identify the object. |
| **heofonan** | “Heaven”: accusative **singular** of weak feminine **heofone**, a variant of the masculine **heofon** learned above. It is not masculine **heofon**'s plural. |
| **sōðlice; īdel and ǣmtig** | Connective “now/for its part”; “empty and void.” The two adjectives are predicate feminine nominative singular with **eorðe**. |
| **ofer ðǣre nēowolnesse brādnesse** | “Over the surface of the deep.” **Ofer** here governs dative **brādnesse**, the head. **Ðǣre nēowolnesse** is its genitive dependent; **ðǣre** belongs to that dependent. |
| **wæs gefęred ofer wæteru** | “Was borne/carried over the waters,” contextually “moved over the waters.” **Wæs gefęred** is a passive phrase; **gefęred** is a past participle. Here **ofer** has accusative plural **wæteru**, unlike the preceding dative construction. |
| **cwæð ðā** | “Then said”: **cwæð** is third-person singular preterite; this **ðā** is the adverb “then,” not the article. |
| **Geweorðe lēoht** | “Let light come to be.” **Geweorðe** is a finite third-person singular present subjunctive (also called optative), expressing a wish or command. |
| **lēoht wearð geworht** | “Light was made/came into being.” **Wearð** is a third-person singular preterite used here with past participle **geworht** in a passive phrase. |
| **geseah ðā ðæt hit gōd wæs** | “Then saw that it was good.” **Geseah** is third-person singular preterite; **ðā** is “then”; **ðæt** is the conjunction “that.” **Hit**, “it,” refers to neuter **lēoht**; **gōd** is a neuter nominative singular predicate adjective. The copula stands at the end of the embedded clause. |
| **hē gedǣlde** | “He separated”: **hē** is nominative singular, referring to God; **gedǣlde** is third-person singular preterite. The object and **fram** phrase use §9. |
| **hēt ðæt lēoht Dæg, and ðā ðīestru Niht** | “[He] called the light Day, and the darkness Night.” **Hēt** is third-person singular preterite, with God/he understood as subject. **Ðæt lēoht** and **ðā ðīestru** are accusative objects; **Dæg** and **Niht** are accusative names applied to them. Here **ðæt** and **ðā** are articles. |
| **Ðā wæs geworden ǣfen and morgen ān dæg.** | “Then there came to be evening and morning: one day.” **Wæs geworden** expresses a resulting state or completed coming-to-be, “had come to be”; **geworden** is a past participle. **Ān dæg** is “one day,” not the ordinal “first day.” Treat this closing formula as a whole. |
