## Analysis and reference: twelve tasks

### NEW-U13-A01

Both clauses mean **“The bishop greeted the monk.”** In each, se bisceop is the nominative subject, ðone munuc the accusative direct object, and grētte the finite preterite singular verb. The demonstratives se and ðone settle the contrasting cases even though each noun's bare singular shape could otherwise be nominative or accusative. Fronting ðone munuc changes order, not the participants.

### NEW-U13-A02

**“When the monk came, the bishop greeted the brothers.”**

`[Temporal: Ðā se munuc cōm], [main: ðā grētte se bisceop ðā brōðor].`

Cōm is finite preterite singular with subject se munuc; grētte is finite preterite singular with subject se bisceop. First Ðā is the temporal conjunction “when”; second ðā is the main-clause correlative adverb “then”; third ðā is the accusative plural demonstrative with brōðor, the people greeted. A plural object does not require a plural grētte.

### NEW-U13-A03

**“The bishop whom the monk greeted sat in the house.”** Main finite verb: sæt; its subject is se bisceop. Relative finite verb: grētte; its subject is se munuc. The antecedent of invariant ðe is se bisceop: subject of the main clause but direct object/person greeted inside the relative. The relative ends at grētte.

### NEW-U13-A04

**“The monk greeted the bishop and gave him the food.”** The bishop receives the greeting; the monk performs both grētte and sealde. Se munuc is expressed once and is understood as the second verb's subject. Ðone bisceop and ðone męte are accusative objects of their respective verbs. Him is the dative recipient, naturally the bishop in this context; morphology identifies its dative role but does not itself name that recipient. A different referent would need additional context. Do not turn the preceding accusative bishop into the giver merely because it is the nearer noun.

### NEW-U13-A05

After **Se sang wæs swēte**, the second sentence is **“It was pleasant.”** Hē refers to se sang. Masculine singular morphology agrees with the grammatical gender of sang; the preceding context selects the song, not a human being.

After **Se bisceop cōm**, the second sentence is **“He was in the house.”** Hē refers to the bishop. Morphology permits a masculine singular antecedent; the stated context identifies that person. Nominative hē is the subject of its own wæs in both examples.

### NEW-U13-A06

Hē can refer to **the bishop or the monk**. Both are masculine singular; gender and number do not select one.

- Bishop: **Se bisceop sæt on ðǣm hūse.**
- Monk: **Se munuc sæt on ðǣm hūse.**

The two explicit versions mean “The bishop sat in the house” and “The monk sat in the house.”

### NEW-U13-A07

**“The monk said, ‘I heard the song. Did you hear it?’”** Ic is the speaking monk; ðū is the bishop whom he addresses, singular; hine is the song, masculine accusative singular as the object of hīerdest. The first finite verb in the quotation is first-singular hīerde; the question has second-singular hīerdest before its subject. Cwæð outside the quotation has the monk as subject.

### NEW-U13-A08

**“The monk said that he was in the house.”** The content clause is **ðæt hē on ðǣm hūse wǣre**. Its finite verb is preterite subjunctive wǣre; its subject hē denotes the monk under the task's express statement that he reports his own presence. The conjunction ðæt is neither that subject nor an article.

### NEW-U13-A09

**“The bishop was in the church, and the brothers were in the house.”**

Expanded second clause: **and ðā brōðor wǣron on ðǣm hūse.** The recovered predicate is “be in the house,” with plural wǣron because the second clause's own subject is plural. Repeating singular wæs would lose agreement.

### NEW-U13-A10

1. **“The monk sat in the house.”** On ðǣm hūse is the initial locative constituent of the main clause; sæt is its finite verb and se munuc its subject.
2. **“When the monk sat in the house, the bishop came.”** The initial Ðā-clause belongs to the temporal frame of the whole sentence: `[Ðā se munuc on ðǣm hūse sæt]`. Its finite verb is sæt, subject se munuc. The following main clause is `[ðā cōm se bisceop]`, with cōm and se bisceop. Its ðā is “then.”

Initial position alone identifies neither an actor nor a subject. The opening phrase can be locative, temporal, or an object; case, agreement and the verb's construction determine roles.

### NEW-U13-A11

**“When the monk whom the bishop greeted came, the brothers sang.”**

```text
[Temporal clause
  Ðā se munuc
     [relative: ðe se bisceop grētte]
  cōm],
[main clause: ðā sungon ðā brōðor].
```

Cōm has se munuc as subject. Grētte has se bisceop as subject; ðe refers to the monk as its object. Sungon has plural ðā brōðor as subject. First Ðā means “when”; the ðā before sungon means “then”; the ðā before brōðor is the nominative plural demonstrative. The embedded relative does not make the bishop the subject of cōm.

### NEW-U13-A12

First, main-clause finite-second order counts **constituents, not written words**, and is a tendency. In Ðone sang hīerde se munuc the complete two-word object phrase is first and hīerde is the next constituent. The checked fragment **Æfter ðon hē underðēodde** goes further: the initial temporal phrase is followed by subject pronoun hē and then the finite verb. Even constituent counting does not make V2 exceptionless.

Second, a dependent finite verb is often late but need not be last. In **ðæt hē ǣrest gehīerde fram ēastsūðdǣle heofones**, the origin phrase follows finite gehīerde inside the content clause. The supplied context identifies hē as Owini and the earlier voice/song objects as dependent on gehīerde.

Cook 25.7 can choose a final bebēode in its particular relative clause as an exercise target. A requested choice among attested orders does not imply that every other order, or every dependent clause, is ungrammatical.

### NEW-U13-C05

The repeated noun phrase expressly identifies the monk as the person who then went.

### NEW-U13-C06

**Se munuc cwæð: “Ic wille mid ēow sprecan.”**

Ic is the monk, and ēow is dative plural for the several brothers addressed. Wille is first singular; sprecan is the plain infinitive.

### NEW-U13-C07

**Se munuc geseah ðone bisceop and grētte hine.**

The monk is expressed once as subject of both actions. Hine is the masculine singular accusative referring naturally to the bishop greeted.

### NEW-U13-C08

**Ðā se bisceop, ðe se munuc grētte, cōm, ðā sungon ðā brōðor.**

[Temporal: Ðā se bisceop
  [relative: ðe se munuc grētte]
 cōm],
[main: ðā sungon ðā brōðor].

The bishop comes; the monk greeted him; the brothers sing. This preserves all three clause subjects and places the relative next to the bishop.

I select plural “your.” Miclu is the previously selected strong feminine nominative of micel, agreeing with mēd; the documented feminine variant micel would also be valid. Heaven is explicitly plural, dative after locative on. For one addressee, substitute ðīn for ēower.

### EX-VII-24-09

**Gif God swā gescrȳt ðæs æceres gærs, ðæt ðe tō-dæg is and tō-morgen on ðone ofen āsęnd bið, micle mā hē ēow gescrȳt, ēalā gē lȳtles gelēafan.**

God clothes accusative gærs; ðæs æceres is its dependent genitive. The §87.b control, checked against the primary page, selects **ðæt ðe**, whose neuter singular antecedent is gærs and whose role in the relative is subject. The coordinated relative predicates are is and āsęnd bið. Bið is retained as Cook specifies; on ðone ofen is directional accusative. The main clause retains the singular finite gescrȳt and plural object ēow. Micle mā, tō-morgen and the genitive-quality address are the printed supplied material. The exact main-clause order after micle mā is not claimed as compulsory.

### EX-VII-24-10

**Bętere is sēo sāwol ðonne męte, and se līchama is bętera ðonne his rēaf.**

The comparative is weak in both predicates: feminine bętere with sāwol and masculine bętera with līchama. The compared nouns after ðonne are nominative. I express the second predicate fully; it may be omitted and recovered, but recovering it still requires the masculine form. His refers to the body's raiment.

### EX-VII-24-11

**Behealdað heofones fuglas; ēower heofonlica Fæder fēt hīe.**

I address several people. Fuglas is accusative after the command. Heofones is genitive singular. Fæder is singular subject of fēt; hīe refers to the plural birds as object. The weak adjective after the possessive is an allowed choice; strong heofonlic would also be defensible. Singular **Beheald … ðīn …** is another consistent interpretation if only one person is addressed.

### EX-VII-24-12

**Hwæt sculon wē etan, oððe hwæt drincan?**

Sculon is first plural with wē. The second question shares the understood sculon wē; both etan and drincan are plain infinitives, and each hwæt is the thing eaten or drunk.

## Cook VII.25: twelve translations

### EX-VII-25-01

**Sē ðe his brōðor ne lufað, ðone ðe hē gesiehð, hū mæg hē God lufian, ðone ðe hē ne gesiehð?**

The initial relative describes the person later resumed by hē in the question. His brōðor is the person not loved. The object relative ðone ðe after brōðor follows §87.b; I use the same strategy for the final God-relative. The two accusative relatives represent the persons seen or not seen. Mæg governs plain lufian. Ne stays with its respective finite verb; it does not negate seeing the brother in the first relative.

### EX-VII-25-02

**Sēo tīd mīnre forðfōre is nēah.**

Forðfōre is the genitive departure, with agreeing mīnre; tīd is the nominative feminine subject. Nēah is the predicate adverb “near/at hand.”

### EX-VII-25-03

**Hē dyde hwæthwega swilces ðā-hwīle-ðe his gefēran tō byrig ēodon.**

I use the supplied indefinite and its genitive expression. The while-clause has plural gefēran as subject and ēodon as past verb; the simple Old English past can render English “were going.” Tō byrig uses the town's dative.

### EX-VII-25-04

**Wes mid ðǣm brōðrum.**

I address one person; mid takes dative plural. **Wesað mid ðǣm brōðrum** is the corresponding plural command.

### EX-VII-25-05

**Geopena ðæs hūses ēagðȳrlu.**

I address one person. The windows are neuter accusative plural with the syncopated stem; the house is genitive singular. Plural command **Geopeniað** is also possible if several people are addressed.

### EX-VII-25-06

**Lufiað ēowre fīend, dōð ðǣm wel ðe ēow hatiað, ðæt gē sīen ēowres Fæder bearn, sē ðe on heofonum is.**

Lufiað and dōð address the plural audience. Ēowre fīend is the accusative object loved. Ðǣm is the dative beneficiary of doing well; ðe represents those people as subject of plural hatiað, with ēow as accusative object. The purpose clause has plural present subjunctive sīen. Bearn is nominative plural, and ēowres Fæder the dependent genitive. The §87.b relative **sē ðe** refers to the masculine singular Father as subject of is; the long sē is supplied. Heaven remains plural.

### EX-VII-25-07

**Gē sindon mīne frīend, gif gē dōð ðā ðing ðe ic ēow bebēode.**

Gif contains indicative dōð as directed. Ðā ðing is its object and antecedent of invariant ðe, the strategy specified by §87.d. Inside that relative ic is subject, ēow the dative addressee, and ðe the thing commanded. Bebēode is last in the relative. The conditional clause has its own order and is not being subjected to a universal final-verb rule.

### EX-VII-25-08

**Ðā fuglas sungon ðone swētestan sang, wynsumne tō hīeranne.**

The birds are plural subject. The superlative is weak accusative masculine after ðone; wynsumne is the separately predicated strong accusative masculine adjective, also referring to sang. Tō hīeranne is the taught inflected-infinitive expression, not plain hīeran.

### EX-VII-25-09

**Ðǣre cirican hrōf is swīðe hēah.**

The church phrase is genitive; masculine hrōf is nominative subject, and hēah its strong nominative predicate.

### EX-VII-25-10

**Ðā wæs sęmninga mid ðǣm ęngle getæl heofonlices weorodes, God hęriendra and ðus cweðendra.**

Ðā follows Cook's stated choice for this opening. Getæl is the singular subject of wæs; mid governs dative ęngle. I use the supplied bare genitive phrase heofonlices weorodes for the contextually identified heavenly host. Hęriendra and cweðendra are the supported substantival genitive plural participles in the collective expression, not finite plural verbs. God is accusative object of praising; ðus modifies saying. I have not supplied words said, since the task itself ends there.

### EX-VII-25-11

**Gode sīe wuldor on hēanessum, and on eorðan sibb mannum gōdes willan.**

The first wish has dative recipient Gode, optative sīe and nominative wuldor. The second has an understood parallel sīe with nominative sibb and dative recipient mannum. Gōdes willan describes those men through the supplied quality expression. Hēanessum is plural dative; eorðan is locative dative. The supplied formula permits this translation without independently generalizing the quality genitive.

### EX-VII-25-12

**Hē ęfthwearf ðȳ ilcan wege ðe hē ǣr cōm.**

Ęfthwearf is singular preterite. The whole route expression is taught/supplied; no extra preposition translates “by.” The relative with ǣr and simple cōm expresses the earlier coming, so English “had come” does not force a new perfect auxiliary.

## New reading: four tasks

### NEW-U13-R01

The bishop sat in the house, and the monk was there. When the monk heard the song, he went to the bishop. The monk said, “I heard the song, which was very pleasant. Did you hear it?” The bishop said, “Go now, and bring the brothers here.” Then the monk went to the church and came back with the brothers. The bishop saw them and said, “You are my friends. I want to speak with you.” The monk sat with the brothers, and they heard the bishop's words.

“Was sitting” is a possible contextual English rendering of sæt, without making its Old English simple preterite a progressive form. The translation preserves the monk's question, both parts of the bishop's command, and the final address to several people.

### NEW-U13-R02

| Form in its actual context | Reference and role |
| --- | --- |
| hē after ēode | The monk; nominative singular subject of going to the bishop after hearing the song |
| hine in Hīerdest ðū hine? | The song; accusative masculine singular object of hearing |
| hīe after geseah | Naturally the returning group, the monk with the brothers; at minimum the brothers are the salient plural group. The pronoun alone does not settle whether the monk is included in this particular plural reference. |
| Ic in the bishop's final speech | The bishop who is speaking; nominative first singular subject of wille |
| ēow in that speech | The several people he addresses, naturally the returned group. A narrower address to the brothers is also consistent with the grammar. It is dative after conversational mid. |

Hine need not denote a man: sang is grammatically masculine, and the pronoun agrees with that noun's gender. English therefore uses “it.” Neither masculine gender nor the accusative case establishes human reference.

### NEW-U13-R03

- Temporal clause: **Ðā se munuc ðone sang hīerde**. Finite hīerde, subject se munuc, accusative object ðone sang.
- Its main clause: **ðā ēode hē tō ðǣm bisceope**. Finite ēode, subject hē = the monk; initial ðā is correlative “then.”
- Relative: **ðe swīðe wynsum wæs**. Finite wæs, subject ðe = the song, whose antecedent ðone sang is an object in the enclosing spoken sentence.
- Coordinated predicate: **and cōm ęft mid ðǣm brōðrum**. Finite cōm has the unexpressed repeated subject se munuc from **Ðā ēode se munuc tō ðǣre cirican**.
- A second instance is **and cwæð** after **Se bisceop geseah hīe**: both geseah and cwæð have the bishop as subject. The intervening plural object does not become the speaker.

### NEW-U13-R04

The monk first hears the song, then goes to the bishop and asks whether he heard it. The bishop commands him to go and bring the brothers. The monk goes to the church and returns with them. The bishop then addresses the returned listeners as his friends and says that he wants to speak with them.

The bishop, the monk and the brothers are present at the end. **Se bisceop geseah hīe and cwæð** establishes the bishop's continuing presence and speech; **Se munuc sæt mid ðǣm brōðrum** explicitly places the monk with the brothers. The final **hīe hīerdon ðæs bisceopes word** presents the listeners hearing the bishop's words. Plural pronoun membership need not be determined by grammatical number alone to identify these participants.

## Source reading: eight tasks

### NEW-U13-S01

One day, when Owini was doing something of this kind outside, and his companions had gone to town, to the church, as they often did, and the bishop was alone in the oratory, occupied either in reading books or in prayers, he suddenly heard—as he afterwards related—the sweetest and most beautiful voice of people singing and rejoicing, descending from heaven to earth.

He said that he first heard the voice and the song from the southeastern part of the sky, that is, from the height of the winter sun's rising. From there they drew near him little by little, until it—the song—reached the roof of the oratory in which the bishop was; entering, it filled everything and enveloped the place all around. Owini then attentively directed his mind to the things he heard. Then, about half an hour later, he again heard that same song of joy rise from the roof of the same oratory and return up to the heavens with indescribable sweetness, by the same way that it had come before.

He remained there for a while, wondering and amazed, and with an attentive mind thought and pondered what these things might mean. Then the bishop opened the window of the oratory and made a signal with his hand—as was his custom if anyone was outside—for that person to come in to him. Owini immediately went in to him. The bishop said to him, “Go quickly to the church, and tell our seven brothers to come here to me; and you be with them too.”

Translation decisions and limits:

- The opening and later human “he” who hears, attends, wonders and enters is Owini, supplied by the learner's context. Explicit English names clarify that reference without purporting to alter the Old English text.
- “Had gone” gives the contextual sequence of simple ēodon; “were going” is another possible way to render the temporal background. It is not evidence that the Old English contains an auxiliary perfect.
- Voice and song first take plural nēalǣcton; singular hē becōm then denotes the song, as original note 152.9a states. I preserve that transition by spelling out “the song.”
- “From the height of the winter sun's rising” is intentionally close to the wording. “From high in the region of the winter sunrise” expresses the same supplied spatial explanation more naturally. The source does not say that Owini himself rises or follows the sound.
- Eall is the accusative thing filled. “The place” in the English continuation makes the surrounding/enveloping relation intelligible; it is inferred from the oratory, not an additional Old English noun. A closer rendering is “filled everything and surrounded it all around.”
- “What these things might be” is a closer alternative to contextual “might mean.” The subjunctive sceolden belongs to the embedded question; it is not a statement that these things actually were a particular thing.
- The two buildings remain separate. The bishop is in the oratory; the summoned brothers are at the community church. The final words order Owini to join the seven; the selected extract does not narrate the subsequent arrival.

### NEW-U13-S02

The opening has a temporal frame with three coordinated situations, a comparison nested in that frame, a main hearing clause, and a parenthetical report. The heard event is an infinitival complement, not another finite clause:

```text
[Temporal frame: Ðā
  [hē ðā sume dæge hwæthwugu swilces ūte dyde],
  and [his gefēran tō byrig tō ciricean ēodon,
       [comparison: swā hīe gelōmlice dydon]],
  and [se bisceop, āna in ðǣre ciricean,
       oððe in bēcrǣdinge oððe in gebedum geornfull wæs]],
[main: ðā gehīerde hē sęmninga,
  [parenthesis: swā-swā hē ęft æfter ðon sægde],
  [perceived event:
   ðā swētestan stefne and ðā fægerestan,
   singendra and blissiendra,
   of heofonum oð eorðan āstīgan]].
```

| Finite verb | Subject |
| --- | --- |
| dyde | hē = Owini |
| ēodon | his gefēran = Owini's companions |
| dydon | hīe = those companions |
| wæs | se bisceop = Chad |
| gehīerde | hē = Owini |
| sægde | hē = Owini, who later reported what he heard |

First Ðā introduces “when.” The second ðā, after hē, is a temporal adverb within that opening clause. The ðā immediately before main gehīerde is the correlative “then.” The later ðā before the superlative adjectives are accusative feminine singular demonstratives, not additional “then” markers. Āstīgan is the infinitive giving the voice's descending action; singendra and blissiendra are the supplied genitive plural participles.

### NEW-U13-S03

In **hē becōm**, hē refers to **se sang**, the song: Cook's original note 152.9a explicitly says so. In **ðe hē ǣr cōm**, hē again refers to that song, now called the same blissesang. Masculine hē therefore becomes English “it.” The monk/Owini does not make this journey.

The sound descends from heaven to earth. Owini first hears it from the southeastern part of the sky, described by the height of the winter sun's rising. It approaches him little by little, reaches the oratory's roof, enters, fills everything and surrounds the place. About half an hour later the same song of joy rises from that roof and returns by its former route up to heaven, with indescribable sweetness.

### NEW-U13-S04

Owini hears the approaching sound and then **geornlice his mōd āðęnede** toward what he heard: he directs his attention to it. He hears the song rise and return, remains there wondering and amazed, and **ðōhte and smēade** what these events might mean. Once called by the signal, he **ēode … sōna in tō him**, immediately entering to Chad.

Chad **ontȳnde … ðæt ēagðȳrel**, opens the oratory window, and **mid his handa slōg tācen**, makes a signal with his hand. The customary signal tells the person outside to come in. Chad then speaks the command to fetch the brothers and to accompany them.

**Ðā ontȳnde se bisceop** explicitly introduces Chad as the window-opener after the account of Owini's thoughts. **Cwæð hē, se bisceop, him tō** explicitly identifies the subsequent speaker: hē is Chad, while him is Owini. The apposition does not make earlier hē referring to Owini or the song denote Chad. In the habitual conditional, hwilc mann is a generic person outside; in this actual occurrence Owini is that person, and the him toward whom he enters is Chad.

### NEW-U13-S05

The antecedent of **ðe** is **ðǣre ciricean**, the oratory, not its roof. The later **in** governs the relative: “in which.” The relative has its own subject **se bisceop** and finite **wæs**. The preposition remains after the relative marker in the actual Old English.

Complete phrase with the preceding destination: **tō ðæcean ðǣre ciricean ðe se bisceop in wæs** = **“to the roof of the oratory in which the bishop was.”** Without the external tō, the noun phrase is “the roof of the oratory in which the bishop was.” The form ciricean here is genitive as dependent on roof; its role as the relative's antecedent does not turn the genitive noun itself into an object of in.

### NEW-U13-S06

The two fronted objects are **Ðā stefne** and **ðone sang**, both accusative singular. Their governing verb is **gehīerde**, not cwæð. This is exactly the relation identified by Cook's note 152.8, “Acc. after gehīerde,” and the printed local help.

```text
[fronted objects: Ðā stefne and ðone sang]
[reporting clause: hē cwæð
  [content clause: ðæt hē ǣrest gehīerde ___
    fram ēastsūðdǣle heofones ...]].
```

The gap in this analytical diagram marks the relation of the earlier objects; it is not a word missing from the source. Both hē forms denote Owini. The reporting assertion is “he said”; its content is “that he first heard the voice and song ….” A connected English rendering is **“He said that he first heard the voice and song from the southeastern part of the sky.”** Treating the two accusatives as the subject of cwæð would lose both case and the actual singular subject hē.

### NEW-U13-S07

The speaker is **Chad**, explicitly **hē, se bisceop**. The addressee is **Owini**, the person who has just entered. Within the quotation:

- **ūre** identifies the brothers as members of the speaker's shared community; in context Chad and Owini both belong to it. It modifies accusative plural **seofon brōðor**, the commanded actors of cuman.
- **mē** is Chad, the destination/person to whom they are to come.
- **ðū** is singular Owini, explicitly commanded to be with them too.

The seven brothers at the community church are to come to Chad in his oratory: **Gang hraðe tō ciricean, and hāt ūre seofon brōðor hider tō mē cuman**. Owini is included in the intended gathering in addition to those seven through **and ðū ēac swilce mid wes**, “and you be with them too.” He is not merely to deliver a message and stay behind.

Original note **152.5**, attached to the earlier ciricean, distinguishes the bishop's **oratorio loci** from the church to which the monks had gone, and points to p.153 line 7. The learner's orientation and reading help 2 state the same distinction. The identical noun therefore does not mean that Owini must fetch the seven from the room where Chad is already sitting.

### NEW-U13-S08

Main-clause example: **Ðā ontȳnde se bisceop ðæt ēagðȳrel ðǣre ciricean**. The finite verb is ontȳnde; subject se bisceop; first constituent the temporal adverb Ðā. The finite verb follows that one constituent and precedes the nominal subject, fitting the chapter's common main-clause pattern. The article ðæt begins the window object and is not a content conjunction here.

Dependent-clause example: **ðæt hē ǣrest gehīerde fram ēastsūðdǣle heofones**. The initial marker ðæt introduces the content clause; its first internal subject phrase is hē, Owini. The finite verb is gehīerde. It follows hē and ǣrest but precedes the fram origin phrase inside the same clause. This is an actual nonfinal dependent finite verb. The separately fronted accusative objects belong to this verb, as analysed in S06. The source illustrates a tendency with limits; it should not be rearranged merely to make a universal rule appear true.

## Initial findings retained with the blind witness
