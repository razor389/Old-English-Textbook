# Unit 4. Strong verbs I–III

Strong verbs form their preterite chiefly by changing the stem vowel. This unit gives the complete selected present and preterite indicative of Classes I–III, with the stem changes needed to use them. The noun, pronoun, adjective and weak-verb systems of Units 1–3 remain in use.

In the tables, **1**, **2** and **3** denote first, second and third person; **sg.** and **pl.** denote singular and plural. A column labelled **1–3 pl.** gives the same form for all three plural persons. Dual pronoun subjects also take these plural forms. Use the selected forms when requested; the documented alternatives remain valid Old English. Macrons and the distinction between e and ę are retained.

## 1. Principal parts and finite stems

<!-- teaching: NEW-U04-GRAM-STRONG-WEAK-CONTRAST -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U04-GRAM-PRINCIPAL-PARTS-REFERENCE -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U04-GRAM-INDICATIVE-SCOPE -->

The weak verbs already learned add a dental element in the preterite. A strong verb instead has a series of stems: the vowel of its present, singular preterite and plural preterite may differ. The **four principal parts** identify this series. They are conventionally listed as infinitive, singular preterite, plural preterite and past participle.

<!-- table: U04-PRINCIPAL-PART-POSITIONS -->

| Position | Function |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Infinitive | Names the verb and identifies the present stem. |
| 2. Singular preterite | Identifies the ordinary first/third singular preterite stem. |
| 3. Plural preterite | Identifies the stem used in all plural persons and in the second singular. |
| 4. Past participle | Completes the cited stem series; its syntax is not yet required. |

Thus drīfan, drāf, drifon, drifen display the Class I sequence ī : ā : i : i. Drāf and drifon are finite indicative forms. The infinitive and participle columns are **supplied citations**: they may be consulted, and their independent recall or use in infinitive, participial, perfect or passive constructions is not required here. A principal-part entry identifies a stem; the complete tables below identify the person and number.

Present indicative forms state present actions or conditions; preterite indicative forms state past actions or conditions. Every finite table in this unit covers those two tenses and all three persons in singular and plural. The two rare reference verbs in §5 are for recognition only.

## 2. Endings and stem changes

<!-- teaching: NEW-U04-GRAM-PRESENT-ENDINGS -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U04-GRAM-PRETERITE-ENDINGS -->

<!-- table: U04-ENDINGS -->

| Person and number | Present | Preterite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 singular | -e | singular preterite stem; no added ending |
| 2 singular | -st, with the listed stem changes | plural preterite stem + -e |
| 3 singular | -ð, with the listed stem changes | singular preterite stem; no added ending |
| 1, 2, 3 plural | -að | plural preterite stem + -on |

Contracted verbs have the special present forms printed in their tables. The strong **second singular preterite** uses the plural stem, not the ordinary first/third singular stem: drife belongs with drifon, while drāf is the first/third singular. Findan has an additional third-singular exception, given in §4. The indicative tables keep every person explicit.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U04-GRAM-PRESENT-CONSONANTS -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U04-GRAM-GRAMMATICAL-CHANGE -->

The second and third singular present often lose the vowel of older -est and -eð endings. The resulting consonant groups change. Dental d or t before ð gives tt, sometimes written t; d before st gives tst. Stem-final s combines with either ending as st. A stem-final ð may disappear before st, and doubled ð may be reduced. Doubled consonants may also simplify before a consonantal ending. The selected forms below show the result for each verb.

Some verbs change a consonant between their principal-part stems: snīðan and sēoðan have d in their plural-based preterites, while cēosan has r. Such alternation belongs to the particular verb and stem position. Likewise, g/h alternatives occur in the stated environments after a long vowel or a liquid; they are not interchangeable throughout a paradigm.

Full -est/-eð forms also occur. Their vowels may retain an older change or follow another part of the paradigm by analogy. The notes list documented alternatives; the selected tables do not license automatic manufacture of every possible full-ending spelling.

A request for the selected pattern requires the forms in the table, but these alternatives are not grammatical errors.

Their complete forms and whole-word meanings are learned separately. The prefixes do not by themselves supply a general rule for creating other verbs or predicting their meanings. Forwierðst is valid beside selected forwierst.

## 7. Ordinary clauses

<!-- teaching: NEW-U04-GRAM-DIRECT-OBJECTS -->

The following selected uses take an **accusative object**: drīfan with the being driven, bindan with the being bound, cēosan with the person or thing chosen, findan with the person or thing found, and drincan with the liquid drunk. The verb’s particular use determines its construction. These object patterns do not follow merely from membership in a strong class.

The eight examples in this section are newly composed teaching material. The other verbs in the tables may be practised as labelled finite forms without assuming additional object or complement constructions.

<!-- example: NEW-U04-EXAMPLE-01 -->

Ic drīfe ðā nīetenu.

I drive the cattle.

Nīetenu is the accusative plural object. The singular first-person subject takes drīfe.

<!-- example: NEW-U04-EXAMPLE-03 -->

Ðū cīest ðæt scip.

You choose the ship.

Scip is the neuter accusative singular object. Cīest is the selected second singular present of cēosan.

<!-- example: NEW-U04-EXAMPLE-04 -->

Se gefēra dranc wæter.

The companion drank water.

The companion is the subject; wæter is the accusative object. Dranc is the third singular preterite.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U04-GRAM-INTRANSITIVE-ACTIONS -->

Rīdan “ride” and singan “sing” may stand without a nominal object. A familiar adverb may qualify the action. Riding here has its ordinary horseback sense; these clauses introduce no direction or means phrase.

<!-- example: NEW-U04-EXAMPLE-02 -->

Ðū rītst and hē rītt.

You ride, and he rides.

Each singular subject has its own finite verb. Both forms preserve the dental changes of rīdan.

<!-- example: NEW-U04-EXAMPLE-08 -->

Wit sungon wel.

We two sang well.

The dual subject takes the plural preterite sungon. Wel qualifies the singing.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U04-GRAM-HELPAN-DATIVE -->

The selected construction of **helpan** puts the recipient of help in the **dative**. A person or another being helped can occupy this position. The genitive also occurs with this verb; it is valid Old English but differs from the dative construction requested here. The use with birds in the cumulative practice is an ordinary application of the same helping sense.

<!-- example: NEW-U04-EXAMPLE-05 -->

Hē hilpð ðǣm męnn.

He helps the man.

Męnn is dative singular, marked also by ðǣm. It is the recipient of help, not an accusative object.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U04-GRAM-BECOME-PREDICATE -->

Ordinary **weorðan** means “become”. Its predicate adjective is nominative and agrees with the subject in gender and number, as a predicate with the copula does. This ordinary use is distinct from the passive expressions previously supplied in readings.

<!-- example: NEW-U04-EXAMPLE-06 -->

Se gefēra wearð glæd.

The companion became glad.

Glæd is strong masculine nominative singular, agreeing with gefēra. Wearð is the third singular preterite of weorðan.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U04-GRAM-SIMPLE-NEGATION -->

Place **ne** before the noncontracting strong finite forms used here, just as before the ordinary weak forms already learned. The form of the verb otherwise keeps its person, number and tense. Contracted negative copulas and other negative systems are not part of this rule.

<!-- example: NEW-U04-EXAMPLE-07 -->

Ic ne finde ðæt scip.

I do not find the ship.

Ne negates the first singular present finde. The object remains accusative.

## 10. Source reading: The shipwreck and the survivor

## 12. Source and variant notes

1. The principal-part, indicative and stem rules follow Cook, **A First Book in Old English**, §§92–95 and 101–104, printed pp.54–55 and 58–63. The selected tables preserve the edition’s quantities. Full finite cells formed from a checked stem and the ordinary endings are transparent applications of the pattern; the tables do not claim a separate surviving quotation for every person.

2. Wright’s **Old English Grammar** corroborates the selected stem series and recorded alternatives. In particular, its Class III discussion permits first- as well as third-person funde. Its discussion of contracted Class I verbs records analogical movement into Class II; the wrēon table here consistently selects Cook’s Class I pattern. Alternatives in the notes are available for recognition and comparison, not additional independent-recall requirements.

3. The rare fēolan reference combines Cook’s principal parts and ordinary endings with Bosworth–Toller’s filhst/filhþ entries. The displayed filhð changes þ to the course’s ð spelling only. The supplement transfers the earlier entry’s passages to fēolan. This is a complete synthesized reference, not a claim that each printed cell is individually attested. Cook’s folen and Wright’s folgen/fōlen remain distinct citations.

4. The ordinary case frames are independently documented: Cook’s direct-object and predicate rules (§§168 and 150), his occurrences of findan, cēosan and singan, and the Bosworth–Toller entries for drīfan, bindan, rīdan, drincan and helpan. The animal and ship/dwelling objects in the new examples are ordinary applications of these senses. Attested human dative recipients of help support its ordinary application to birds; the dictionary also records a genitive construction.

5. The original extract is Cook, **A First Book in Old English**, Reader XII, **Apollonius of Tyre**, printed pp.165–166, one-based PDF181–182. It begins within p.165 line 6 and ends at the first period in p.166 line 4. These line counts expand the edition’s printed five-line intervals. The line-crossing compound Ðǣr-tō-ēacan is joined, and note cues are moved below; the Old English spelling and punctuation are preserved. The English setting is editorial context.
