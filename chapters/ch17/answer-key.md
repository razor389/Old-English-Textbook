# Unit 17. Answer key

The composition models are new teaching material. The reading translation renders Cook's actual Old English and was prepared independently before comparison with other authorial translations. Each model is one defensible answer: preserve the participants, essential meaning, case relations, agreement, mood and clause connections, together with the task's explicit lexical, number, omission and order controls. A grammatical alternative can miss a stated target. All seven Cook sets retain the original joint group **XII, XIII**.

The supplied phrases and exact dictionary hints remain visible. Copying a given citation is not a test of independent recall; selecting the required ending, person, number or role may still be the task. The complete local numeral expressions, unusual reading frames and figurative supports are not invitations to reconstruct an untaught system unaided. English analytical answers may use equivalent precise terminology.

Except where an order is specified, grammatical rearrangements that preserve the relationships are acceptable. Documented spelling and inflectional variants must be distinguished from mistakes: for example, **ðām** beside selected **ðǣm**, or a licensed feminine plural **-e** beside a selected **-a**. Such variants do not excuse wrong case, person or sense. The selected tables govern tasks that expressly ask for their forms.[^key-sources]

## A. Clauses and participants

### A01

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-A01 -->

**Task:** Analyse the report. The man’s goodness is presented as simultaneous with the king’s past statement.

**Input:** Se cyning sægde ðæt se mann wǣre gōd.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-A01-O001 -->

**Identify the two subjects and their finite verbs.**

**Se cyning** is the nominative subject of **sægde**, preterite indicative third singular. **Se mann** is the nominative subject of **wǣre**, preterite subjunctive third singular. The king speaks; the reported goodness belongs to the man.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-A01-O002 -->

**Classify ðæt and explain the reported time relation.**

**Ðæt** introduces the content of the statement, “that the man was good”; it is a conjunction here. The task places that goodness at the time of the king's past statement. Thus past **wǣre** does not by itself mean that the goodness preceded the statement. Calling ðæt a relative pronoun would give it an unneeded participant role.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-A01-O003 -->

**Does wǣre require the conclusion that the man was not good? Explain.**

No. The subjunctive marks the embedded report; it does not establish that the man was bad or that the report was false. A translation such as “The king said that the man was good” preserves both the report and the stated simultaneity. An interpretation of earlier goodness or present contrary fact misses the supplied context.

### A02

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-A02 -->

**Task:** Analyse the comparison; then replace the first subject by the men while keeping the king singular.

**Input:** Se mann sang bęt ðonne se cyning dyde.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-A02-O001 -->

**Translate the sentence.**

“The man sang better than the king did.” The man is the better singer in this comparison. “Better than the king sang” expresses the same meaning in English; reversing the two performers does not.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-A02-O002 -->

**State the action repeated by dyde and explain why it is singular.**

**Dyde** substitutes for the action “sang.” It is third singular because its own subject is **se cyning**, independently of the first clause's subject. It does not mean that the king performed some unspecified different action.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-A02-O003 -->

**Write the complete revised Old English sentence.**

**Ðā męnn sungon bęt ðonne se cyning dyde.**

**Ðā męnn** and **sungon** replace the first singular subject and verb. The singular **se cyning dyde** stays intact. Pluralizing dyde would change the second performer; retaining sang would leave the first clause without the requested plural agreement.

### A03

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-A03 -->

**Task:** Analyse the relative clause; then replace the man by the men.

**Input:** Ic geseah ðone mann ðe sang.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-A03-O001 -->

**Give the outer case and role of ðone mann.**

**Ðone mann** is accusative singular, the direct object of **geseah**: the person whom I saw. Its role in the seeing clause determines that case.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-A03-O002 -->

**Give the role represented by ðe inside the relative clause.**

Inside the relative clause, **ðe** represents the singer and functions as subject of **sang**. The particle is invariant. Its antecedent's accusative case in the outer clause does not make the relative's internal role accusative.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-A03-O003 -->

**Write the complete revised Old English sentence.**

**Ic geseah ðā męnn ðe sungon.**

The object becomes **ðā męnn** and the relative verb becomes **sungon**. **Ic geseah** remains first singular: the same speaker saw several men. A different defensible order is possible, but changing the speaker to a plural subject changes the task.

### A04

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-A04 -->

**Task:** A concerns a possible visit tomorrow. In B the speaker treats his coming now as contrary to fact. Analyse both clauses in each sentence.

**Input:** Gif hē cymð, ic fare.

**Input:** Gif hē cōme, ic fōre.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-A04-O001 -->

**Translate A and name the mood of each verb.**

A: “If he comes, I shall go.” **Cymð** is present indicative third singular; **fare** is present indicative first singular in this stated setting. Tomorrow supplies future reference. An indicative condition is possible after gif; no separate future auxiliary is needed.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-A04-O002 -->

**Translate B and name the tense and mood of each verb.**

B: “If he were coming now, I would go,” or “If he came now, I would go.” **Cōme** is preterite subjunctive third singular and **fōre** preterite subjunctive first singular. Here those past forms express the stipulated contrary-to-fact present situation, not a completed past visit.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-A04-O003 -->

**Explain which evidence supplies the time and factual status.**

The task's possible visit tomorrow versus contrary-to-fact coming now supplies the time and factual setting. The forms distinguish the two selected constructions, but **gif** alone fixes neither a universal mood nor a time. A translation of B as “If he had come, I would have gone” changes the given time relation.

## F. Forms and grammatical roles

### F01

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-F01 -->

**Task:** Use cniht in its explicitly taught servant sense and bōc book. Translate the four expressions. In each, choose the noun's case from its role rather than from an English word ending.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F01-O001 -->

**the servant's book**

**ðæs cnihtes bōc**

**Ðæs cnihtes** is the singular genitive possessor; **bōc** is the singular head. The book does not become genitive merely because its owner is genitive. The assigned sense of cniht is servant.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F01-O002 -->

**the servants' books**

**ðāra cnihta bēc**

**Ðāra cnihta** is genitive plural and **bēc** is the plural head: several servants' books. The changed vowel in bēc belongs to the book paradigm; a regularized plural would miss the selected form.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F01-O003 -->

**I give the servant the book.**

**Ic giefe ðǣm cnihte ðā bōc.**

**Ic giefe** is first singular present; **ðǣm cnihte** is the dative recipient and **ðā bōc** the accusative thing given. Changing the order of the two objects can preserve the meaning; exchanging their case roles cannot.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F01-O004 -->

**We give the servants the books.**

**Wē giefað ðǣm cnihtum ðā bēc.**

**Wē giefað** is first plural. Both objects change number: dative **ðǣm cnihtum**, accusative **ðā bēc**. Giefað is an assertion with wē here, despite sharing its spelling with a plural command form.

### F02

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-F02 -->

**Task:** Use the selected endingless accusative of cwēn queen, while retaining its genitive/dative ending. Translate the three expressions, then explain the contrast.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F02-O001 -->

**I saw the queen.**

**Ic geseah ðā cwēn.**

The queen is the accusative object of seeing: **ðā cwēn**. The endingless cwēn is the explicitly selected accusative. Documented **cwēne** can be a valid accusative alternative, but does not meet this task's endingless-form target.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F02-O002 -->

**I gave the queen the book.**

**Ic geaf ðǣre cwēne ðā bōc.**

**Ðǣre cwēne** is the dative recipient; **ðā bōc** is the accusative gift. The endingless accusative option does not license dropping the dative -e.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F02-O003 -->

**the queen's book**

**ðǣre cwēne bōc**

**Ðǣre cwēne** is genitive singular, dependent on **bōc**: the book belongs to the queen. Its spelling matches the preceding dative, while its grammatical function differs.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F02-O004 -->

**Explain what the three queen forms show about identifying case from an ending alone.**

**Cwēn** is accusative in the seeing clause even without an ending; **cwēne** is dative as recipient and genitive as possessor. The governing construction and article identify the case. An unchanged ending is not proof of an unchanged grammatical role.

### F03

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-F03 -->

**Task:** Use fox fox, hol hole and nest nest. Translate each sentence. Keep ordinary locative on with the dative and distinguish the two neuter plural patterns.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F03-O001 -->

**The fox is in the hole.**

**Se fox is on ðǣm hole.**

**Se fox** is nominative masculine singular. **On ðǣm hole** is a dative location, not movement into a hole. **Is** agrees with the single fox.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F03-O002 -->

**The foxes are in the holes.**

**Ðā foxas sindon on ðǣm holum.**

**Ðā foxas sindon** gives plural subject and verb; **on ðǣm holum** gives dative plural location. Changing foxas alone would leave the other requested number changes incomplete.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F03-O003 -->

**The nest is in the tree.**

**Ðæt nest is on ðǣm trēowe.**

**Ðæt nest** is the singular subject. The tree is locative dative **trēowe**, with the documented w before the ending. This is a nest located in a tree, not a nest moving toward one.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F03-O004 -->

**The nests are in the trees.**

**Ðā nest sindon on ðǣm trēowum.**

Nominative plural **nest** has no overt plural ending; **ðā** and **sindon** make its plural function clear. **Trēowum** is dative plural. An unchanged nest does not justify singular is.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F03-O005 -->

**The holes are large.**

**Ðā holu sindon miclu.**

The short neuter **hol** has nominative plural **holu**, whereas heavy **nest** has a zero ending. Predicate **miclu** is strong neuter plural. An article in the subject does not turn the separate predicate adjective weak.

### F04

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-F04 -->

**Task:** Use hęrian praise. Supply only the finite form required in each stated setting, then explain the contrast. The dictionary form is visible reference help.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F04-O001 -->

**A narrator states: they praise. Give present indicative third plural.**

**hęriað**

Present indicative third plural: several people praise. This is the narrator's assertion, not a wish.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F04-O002 -->

**A speaker commands several listeners: praise! Give second-plural imperative.**

**hęriað**

Second-plural imperative: several listeners are commanded to praise. Its spelling coincides with the indicative answer, but its addressees and function differ.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F04-O003 -->

**A speaker wishes: may they praise. Give present subjunctive third plural.**

**hęrien**

Present subjunctive third plural: may they praise. The wish is expressed by the **-en** form; adding a verb meaning “be permitted” would change the construction.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F04-O004 -->

**Which answers are spelled alike, and what distinguishes their functions?**

The first two answers, **hęriað**, are spelled alike. The assertion has third-plural subject reference; the command addresses the second plural. **Hęrien** has the separate subjunctive ending. The supplied infinitive identifies the verb; choosing the finite ending and analysis is the work required.

### F05

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-F05 -->

**Task:** Use the explicitly taught present participles of ondrǣdan fear and hyngran be hungry. Translate the two nominal phrases, then distinguish each phrase's outer case from any object governed inside it.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F05-O001 -->

**to those fearing him: use a dative plural participle without an expressed people noun; him is the one feared**

**hine ondrǣdendum**

The nominalized participle **ondrǣdendum** is dative plural, naming the beneficiaries. **Hine** is accusative singular, the person they fear. No separate people noun or finite fear verb is required.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F05-O002 -->

**the hungry: give the weak accusative plural phrase used as a direct object**

**ðā hyngrendan**

**Ðā hyngrendan** is the article plus weak accusative plural participle: the hungry people as direct object. It does not express an impersonal finite “they hunger” construction.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F05-O003 -->

**State the two grammatical relations in the fear phrase.**

The whole fear phrase is dative because of its beneficiary role. Within that phrase, **ondrǣdan** governs accusative **hine**. The dependent object does not copy the phrase's outer dative case.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-F05-O004 -->

**Explain why a present participle can name people here without being a finite verb.**

A participle can be used substantivally for people characterized by an action or condition. It then takes the case, number and strong/weak endings required by that noun phrase. “Present participle” names its form; it does not supply finite person agreement or an independent present-tense assertion.

## C. Composition

### C01

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-C01 -->

**Task:** Translate: The king saw the men who sang. Use an invariant relative particle.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-C01-O001 -->

**Write one complete Old English sentence.**

**Se cyning geseah ðā męnn ðe sungon.**

The king is the singular subject of **geseah**; **ðā męnn** is its accusative plural object. The invariant **ðe** represents those men as the subject of plural **sungon**. A demonstrative relative can be grammatical elsewhere, but this task requests the invariant particle.

### C02

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-C02 -->

**Task:** Translate: The king is as good as the men are. Express equal degree with swā … swā.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-C02-O001 -->

**Write one complete Old English sentence.**

**Se cyning is swā gōd swā ðā męnn sindon.**

The two **swā** markers express equal degree: the king is as good as the men. **Gōd** is the singular predicate of the king, while **sindon** agrees with the men in the comparison clause. A comparative meaning “better than” would reverse the requested equality.

### C03

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-C03 -->

**Task:** A visit is possible tomorrow. Translate: If he comes, give (plural) him the book.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-C03-O001 -->

**Write one complete Old English sentence.**

**Gif hē cymð, giefað him ðā bōc.**

**Cymð** is an indicative in the open future condition. **Giefað** addresses several listeners; **him** is the dative recipient and **ðā bōc** the accusative gift. A singular command would be valid for one listener but would miss the explicit plural target.

### C04

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-C04 -->

**Task:** Translate: The man says that the king is good. Present the content as a report, using the present subjunctive.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-C04-O001 -->

**Write one complete Old English sentence.**

**Se mann sægð ðæt se cyning sīe gōd.**

**Se mann** speaks; **se cyning** is the subject of the reported goodness. **Sīe** is present subjunctive third singular, as explicitly requested. An indicative can assert certainty in a reported clause, but would not meet this stated mood target. The report need not be false.

## Cook 37

**Cook's instruction:** [In this exercise, omit the article and the second personal pronoun throughout.]

This instruction applies to all fourteen items. Retain necessary third-person pronouns such as **hīe** in the praise wishes, but omit an article and an independent second-person subject throughout. The noun catalogues in 37.8–12 continue the address; they need not be converted into fresh finite sentences. Accompanying descriptive adjectives use the selected weak address forms, while **eall** retains its separately taught strong pattern.

### Cook 37.1

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-01 -->

Hęriað Dryhten of heofonum; hęriað hine on hēanessum.

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-37-01-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** Praise ye the Lord from (of) the heavens; praise him in the heights.

**Target and explanation:** Both **hęriað** forms are plural commands. **Dryhten** and **hine** are the persons praised, in the accusative. **Of heofonum** gives the origin and **on hēanessum** the location, both with dative plurals. Adding a second-person subject or an article would miss the whole exercise's omission rule; treating praise as an assertion would change its force.

### Cook 37.2

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-02 -->

Hęriað hine, ealle his ęnglas; hęriað hine, eall his mægnu.

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-37-02-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** Praise ye him, all his angels; praise him, all his powers (mægen).

**Target and explanation:** The angels and powers are the groups addressed, not extra objects of praise. **Ealle ęnglas** is masculine plural; **eall mægnu** is neuter plural, with the explicitly taught sense “powers.” **His** belongs to both groups. Neither the English collective sense nor the familiar strength gloss licenses a singular reading here.

### Cook 37.3

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-03 -->

Hęriað hine, sunne and mōna; hęriað hine, ealle steorran and lēoht.

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-37-03-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** Praise ye him, sun and moon; praise him, all ye stars and light (lēoht).

**Target and explanation:** Sun and moon are singular entities in the address; **steorran** is plural, while **lēoht** is the supplied mass noun “light.” **Ealle** modifies the stars. Keep both commands and all four named entities. English ye does not override the instruction to omit the second-person pronoun.

### Cook 37.4

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-04 -->

Hęriað hine, heofonas heofona, and wæteru ðe ofer heofonas sindon.

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-37-04-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** Praise him, ye heavens of heavens, and ye waters that are above (ofer, 172) the heavens.

**Target and explanation:** **Heofona** is genitive plural dependent on the addressed **heofonas**. **Wæteru** supplies the plural antecedent of **ðe**, the subject of **sindon** inside the relative clause. The selected **ofer heofonas** retains the local accusative target. A genitive near the relative does not determine its internal subject role.

### Cook 37.5

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-05 -->

Hęrien hīe naman Dryhtnes, for-ðon-ðe hē gecwæð and hīe wǣron worhte; hē bebēad and hīe wǣron gesceapene.

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-37-05-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** Let them praise [praise they] the name of the Lord; because he spake (gecweðan), and they were made (wyrcean); he commanded, and they were created (p. 190, l. 19).

**Target and explanation:** **Hęrien hīe** is a third-plural wish, “may they praise,” rather than a command addressed to them or a permission construction. **Naman** is the accusative object and **Dryhtnes** its genitive dependent. Keep the specified **gecwæð** and both passive predicates. **Worhte/gesceapene** are selected plural predicate participles after **wǣron**; worhte here is not a second finite past verb. Uninflected predicate participles are also attested, but the model illustrates the assigned plural forms.

### Cook 37.6

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-06 -->

Hē sętte hīe ā ā ā; hē sętte bebod and ne gewīt.

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-37-06-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** He hath appointed them for ever and ever; he (om. hath) made (sęttan) a decree (bebod, 47), and (om. it) shall not pass (gewītan).

**Target and explanation:** The two **sętte** predicates appoint the entities and establish a decree. The simple preterite may render English hath appointed; the second clause expressly omits hath. The final understood subject is **bebod**, the decree, not God or plural hīe. **Ne gewīt** has future reference here. The entire compressed ending and **ā ā ā** remain supplied; restoring it would miss the explicit omission.

### Cook 37.7

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-07 -->

Hęriað Dryhten of eorðan, dracan and ealla nēowolnessa.

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-37-07-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** Praise the Lord from the earth, ye dragons (draca, 53), and all deeps (nēowolnes).

**Target and explanation:** **Dracan** and **ealla nēowolnessa** are plural addressees; **Dryhten** is the object praised. **Of eorðan** gives the origin. The selected feminine plural quantifier is ealla. A documented -e variant need not be ungrammatical, but differs from that selected table form. Do not turn the dragons and deeps into the things being praised.

### Cook 37.8

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-08 -->

Fȳr and hægel, snāw and īs; gāstas ȳsta ðe his word dōð;

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-37-08-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** Fire and hail (hægel, 43); snow (snāw, 43) and ice (īs, 47); spirits of the storms (ȳst, 51. b) which fulfil (dōn) his word;

**Target and explanation:** This catalogue continues the preceding address. **Ȳsta** is genitive plural, “of storms”; **ðe** represents the spirits as the subject of plural **dōð**. **His word** is their accusative object in the taught fulfilment sense of dōn. The source fragment needs neither an invented new main verb nor an added “things” noun for the listed substances.

### Cook 37.9

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-09 -->

Muntas and ealle hyllas; wæstmbǣran trēowu and ealle cēderbēamas;

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-37-09-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** Mountains, and all hills (hyll, 43); fruitful (wæstmbǣre, 59) trees, and all cedars;

**Target and explanation:** All four groups remain plural. **Wæstmbǣran** is the selected weak adjective in direct address, agreeing with **trēowu**; the given dictionary form wæstmbǣre does not itself supply that ending. **Ealle** follows its own strong pattern with hills and cedars. Strong **wæstmbǣru** is a real neuter plural form, but does not instantiate the selected weak-address construction. Keep short **æ** in wæstm.

### Cook 37.10

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-10 -->

Dēor and eall nīetenu; nædran and gefiðredan fuglas;

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-37-10-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** (Wild) beasts, and all cattle; serpents, and winged (gefiðred) fowl;

**Target and explanation:** **Dēor** is endingless neuter plural here; **eall nīetenu** also has neuter plural agreement. **Nædran** names the serpents. The birds are plural **fuglas**, with supplied weak address adjective **gefiðredan**. English cattle and fowl do not independently determine OE gender or allow singular agreement. ### Cook 37.11

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-11 -->

Cyningas eorðan and eall folc; ealdormęnn and ealle dēman eorðan;

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-37-11-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** Kings of the earth, and all peoples; princes, and all judges of the earth;

**Target and explanation:** Kings, peoples, princes and judges form four addressed plural groups. **Eorðan** depends on kings and judges where it occurs. **Eall folc** is neuter plural despite the endingless noun; **ealle dēman** is masculine plural. Changing the outer case because a noun has a genitive dependent confuses the two relations.

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-12 -->

**Target and explanation:** **Geongan** and **ealdan** are weak plural adjectives used for the addressed young and old people. Calling the adjectives substantival explains their use without creating separate new nouns.

### Cook 37.13

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-13 -->

Hęrien hīe naman Dryhtnes; for-ðon-ðe his ānes nama is ūpāhafen; his wuldor is ofer eorðan and heofon.

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-37-13-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** Let them praise the name of the Lord; for the name of him alone is exalted (ūpāhębban), his glory (wuldor) is above (ofer, 172) the earth and heaven.

**Target and explanation:** The opening is again a third-plural wish. **His ānes** means “of him alone”; it does not mean merely “his only name.” **Ūpāhafen** is a passive participle after **is**, distinct from the finite present in 37.14. Glory is above both earth and heaven in the selected **ofer** phrase. Preserve all three predications and the causal connection.

### Cook 37.14

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-37-14 -->

Hē ūpāhęfð horn his folces, lof ealra his hālgena, bearna Israeles, folces him nēalǣcendes.

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-37-14-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** He exalteth the horn (horn, 43) of his people, the praise of all his saints (hālga, 53), of the children (bearn) of Israel (Israel), of the people drawing nigh (om. unto) him.

**Target and explanation:** Retain literal **horn**. Raising it in this praise context can be understood as exalting the people's strength or power; that interpretation explains the image without establishing a replacement dictionary gloss. **Lof** is appositional praise, followed by the chain of genitives. **Hālgena** is from the explicitly cited weak noun **hālga**, not the adjective hālig. In **folces him nēalǣcendes**, the participle agrees with genitive singular folces, while him is the dative person approached. The whole dense phrase is supplied; removing the image or making him genitive loses a distinct relation.

## Cook 38

### Cook 38.1

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-38-01 -->

Se cyning worhte micel elpenbǣnen setl and oferworhte hit mid ðǣm bętstan golde.

<!-- output: EX-XII-XIII-38-01-O001 -->

**Cook's prompt:** The king made a great ivory (elpenbǣnen, 57. 3) throne, and overlaid (oferwyrcean) it with (mid) the best gold.

**Target and explanation:** **Micel elpenbǣnen setl** is the neuter accusative throne, resumed by **hit**. **Oferworhte** meets the explicit oferwyrcean target; another grammatical verb for covering would not meet that lexical instruction. **Mid ðǣm bętstan golde** gives the material, with the selected weak superlative after the article. Keep both making and overlaying.

### Cook 38.2

<!-- key: KEY-EX-XII-XIII-38-02 -->

Sumu cwēn wæs on ðǣm dagum be sūðan, hāten Saba.

**For 40.9, explain what seed denotes and identify whose seed it is.**

In 40.9 **his sǣde** denotes Abraham's offspring or descendants collectively. The possessor is Abraham, not the speaker or God. “His seed” preserves the image; “his descendants” explains the newly taught collective sense. This analysis task keeps the lexical help available.

**State what the familiar inflection alone cannot establish in these two examples.**

Recognizing a noun's case or paradigm does not determine its contextual sense. The same familiar shape could occur in a literal horn or agricultural seed context. Here context guides interpretation of the horn image, while seed requires its newly taught collective meaning and the correct possessor. The task's help remains available; an ending alone establishes none of these choices.

**Give the referent of hēo in 174.7.**

**Hēo** is the king's daughter. Its feminine singular form is compatible with that referent, but the preceding context identifies the particular woman. The form alone does not name her.

**Hine** refers to masculine **swēgcræft**, musical art. It is the accusative object of **geleornod** in the negative perfect: she has not learned it well. Masculine grammatical gender does not make the referent the king or Apollonius.

**Give the referent of hē in the reported clauses 175.7–8.**

Both instances of **hē** refer to Apollonius, the man whom the audience sees. The clauses report their supposed identification: not Apollonius, but Apollo. They do not change the actual referent into a god.

**Him eallum** refers to all the listeners and is dative plural, the people pleased by the performances. Context supplies the group; the dative construction with **līcode** supplies their experiencer role. They are not its grammatical subject.

### R03

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-R03 -->

**Task:** Identify the clause relationship in each cited expression and distinguish it from a similar construction elsewhere.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-R03-O001 -->

**Classify sōna swā 174.8 and explain its time relation.**

**Sōna swā** introduces “as soon as.” The start of the harp playing is immediately accompanied by the blending of harp sound and song. Here swā is part of the supplied temporal expression, not an assertion that two performers are equally skilled.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-R03-O002 -->

**Classify gif 174.14–15 in its context.**

**Gif ðū mē gelīefst** is an open permission condition: “if you permit me [to speak].” **Gelīefst** is present indicative second singular; the following **ic sęcge** states what the speaker will say. It is neither a request to believe a proposition nor a condition assumed contrary to fact.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-R03-O003 -->

**Classify swā … ðæt 175.6–7.**

In **swā stōd ðæt**, the manner or degree of his stance/appearance leads to a result: the audience takes him for Apollo. This **swā … ðæt** is not the equal-degree **swā … swā** of C02 or the temporal sōna swā at the reading's beginning.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-R03-O004 -->

**Explain why the two ðæt clauses 175.7–8 do not assert that Apollonius actually is Apollo.**

The two clauses are the contents of **wēndon**, “they supposed”: that he was not Apollonius and that he was Apollo. **Nǣre/wǣre** are past subjunctives in that report. The stated thought explains the mistaken identification; the narrator does not assert an actual transformation.

### R06

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U17-R06 -->

**Task:** Explain the argument of the episode from the actual Old English.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-R06-O001 -->

**State the difference between the king’s reading of silence and Apollonius’s explanation.**

The king treats Apollonius's silence as blame and therefore as bad conduct toward his daughter. Apollonius explains a technical judgment: she has encountered music but has not learned it well. He seeks permission to speak and offers a demonstration, rather than denying that his silence conveys criticism.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-R06-O002 -->

**Identify two kinds of evidence by which Apollonius answers the criticism of his conduct.**

He demonstrates skilled harp playing joined with pleasant song, then displays further striking performances after putting down the harp. The listeners respond by shouting praise and taking pleasure in what he presents. Equivalent answers can describe the two kinds of performance in their own words; a bare claim that he is talented does not identify the evidence in the passage.

<!-- output: NEW-U17-R06-O003 -->

**Explain how the final sentence combines novelty with approval.**

The performances are unfamiliar and unusual to the people, yet each greatly pleases all of them. Novelty and approval coexist. “Unknown” does not mean bad or displeasing here, and the final dative experiencers identify the approving audience.
