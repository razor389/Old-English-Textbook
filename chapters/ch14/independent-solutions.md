## S01 — complete translation

Every high office here in the world is beset with dangers; indeed, the greater the worldly honours are, the fiercer the dangers are. Thus, from that, we can recognize and understand the examples.

The tree, then, which grows in the forest so that it towers up above all the other trees and spreads itself out—when a storm and the strong wind suddenly assail it, then it is shaken and buffeted more violently than the rest of the forest.

It is likewise also with the high cliffs and crags: when they tower far up above the rest of the earth, they then suddenly begin to fall and to collapse very violently to the earth.

Likewise also with the high mountains and hills, which stand high above the whole world: nevertheless they suffer the penalty of their pre-eminence, in that they are often punished and battered by heavenly fire, and struck by thunderbolts.

So the high powers here in the world fall and tumble and come to destruction; the riches of this world turn to sorrow; and these earthly wonders come to nothing.

I render the implicit object of the storm's assault as “it” for connected English. “Lightning” is also the supplied contextual sense of heavenly fire and of the thunderbolts. The three final outcomes are destruction, sorrow and nothingness; they have three distinct subjects.

## S02

The opening claim is that high position is surrounded by dangers, with greater worldly honour bringing fiercer danger. The examples, in order, are:

1. A tree rising above the other trees receives more violent shaking from storm and wind.
2. High cliffs and crags, standing far above the surrounding earth, suddenly collapse with great violence.
3. High mountains and hills pay for their elevated position by repeated blows of heavenly fire and thunderbolts.

The conclusion transfers these physical examples to human eminence: high powers fall into destruction, worldly riches become sorrow, and earthly wonders come to nothing. Elevation and apparent magnificence do not provide security.

## S03

There are three occurrences of `hit` in the tree sentence:

| Occurrence | Referent | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ðæt hit hlīfað | the tree | nominative subject of “towers” |
| and brǣt hit | the same tree | accusative reflexive object, “spreads itself” |
| ðonne bið hit | the same tree | nominative subject of the passive “is shaken and buffeted” |

The relative `ðe wiexð on ðǣm wudubearwe` identifies the tree growing in the wood. The following `ðæt` introduces the result of that growth: it towers above the other trees and spreads itself. It is not another demonstrative naming an additional tree.

The temporal pair is `ðonne sęmninga storm gestęnt … ðonne bið hit …`: “when the storm suddenly assails … then it is ….” The initial parenthetic `ðonne` after `Ðæt trēow` is a connective “then” in the presentation of the example. The final `ðonne` after `swīðlicor` is comparative “than,” introducing `se ōðer wudu`. That expression is “the rest of the forest,” the comparison group, not a separate other forest. The collective singular `wudu` stands for the remaining trees.

## S04

Both occurrences of `hīe` refer to the high cliffs and crags (`hēaclifum and torrum`). The finite plural verb `onginnað` governs both `feallan` and `hrēosan`: they begin to fall and collapse. These paired infinitives reinforce one collapse, rather than asserting two unrelated events. `Full ðearlice`, “very violently,” intensifies the falling; `sęmninga` makes it sudden, and `tō eorðan` gives its destination. ## S05

`Ealdordōmes` divides into `ealdor-` + derivational `-dōm` + inflectional `-es`. The supplied base `ealdor` is a chief or king; the derived masculine noun `ealdordōm` means supremacy or pre-eminence. `Eorðlican` divides into `eorð-` + adjective-forming `-lic` + inflectional `-an`. It is the weak nominative neuter plural of supplied `eorðlic`, “earthly,” agreeing with nominative plural `wundor` after `ðās`. The whole phrase `ðās eorðlican wundor` means “these earthly wonders”; it is the subject of the final “come to nothing.” The suffix makes the adjective, while the ending expresses agreement in this particular clause.
