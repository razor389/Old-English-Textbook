# Unit 14. Word formation and lexical families

A familiar part can help you understand a word. It does not settle the whole word’s meaning. This unit distinguishes compounds and derived words from their inflected forms, then applies that distinction to the complete Cook exercises and a connected argument about the dangers of high station.

Sections 1–5 supply the forms, meanings and uses needed by the practice. The four translated examples and Exercises F, A, C and S are new teaching material. Cook VIII.26–27 retain their original prompts. The reading preserves the selected Old English; its modern orientation and helps are separate additions.

**Scope and notation.** Productive forms may be used in the senses and constructions taught here or earlier. A table marked “plural reference” or “singular reference” supplies that number for recognition only. Infinitive and principal-part citations identify a verb; they do not by themselves assign a new syntactic use. Supplied help stays visible during its task and is not tested by unaided recall. In verbal tables, 1, 2 and 3 denote person. Preserve vowel length and the distinction between e and ę.

## 1. Formation and inflection

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-DERIVATION-VERSUS-INFLECTION -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-COMPOUND-ANALYSIS -->

**Derivation** forms a lexical word; **inflection** gives that word a form required in a sentence. Rihtwīs, “righteous”, and rihtwīsnes, “righteousness”, are different words and different parts of speech. The -e of rihtwīsnesse instead marks an inflected form of the noun. An ending can serve more than one case: the clause, agreement and governing word determine which one is present.

A **compound** joins lexical components. In the selected noun compounds the final noun is the head: it identifies the kind of thing named and supplies the inflectional pattern. Goldhord combines gold and the supplied citation hord, “treasure; hoard”. The whole noun means “treasure; hoard of gold”; the ending is added after the complete stem goldhord-. A phrase containing an adjective and a noun instead inflects the adjective for agreement as well as the noun for case and number.

The first component can specify material, source or a quality. Heofonfȳre has heofon + fȳr; hēaclifum has hēah + the supplied clif, “cliff”, with hēa- as the combining form. The local helps give these source forms’ meanings and cases. Do not turn that particular hēah/hēa- relationship into a rule for dropping h elsewhere. Learn the attested whole-word sense: gōdwębb means “purple cloth”, and wyrtgemang “spice; mixture of spices”. English component substitution will not reliably recover such meanings.[^formation]

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-DERIVED-NOUNS -->

A suffix follows the family stem. The selected noun suffixes include -ung for an activity, -scipe for a state or relationship, -nes for an abstract quality, and -dōm for a state, position or authority. Agent nouns in -end name a doer. These are aids to analysing attested words, not instructions to invent a noun from any base. The derived noun has its own gender, meaning and declension.

| Family | Parts of speech and learned meanings | Formation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| leornian → leornung | verb “learn” → feminine noun “learning; study” | leorn- + -ung |
| gefēra → gefērscipe | masculine noun “companion” → masculine noun “companionship” | gefēr- + -scipe; the citation -a is not retained |
| rihtwīs → rihtwīsnes | adjective “righteous” → feminine noun “righteousness” | rihtwīs- + -nes |
| īdel → īdelnes | adjective “empty; void” → feminine noun “idleness” | īdel- + -nes; learn the noun’s established meaning |

Leornian is supplied here as a dictionary citation of the earlier verb. Īdelnes demonstrates the limit of a family resemblance: the previously learned noun means “idleness”, not automatically “physical emptiness”. In nouns ending in -nes, s is doubled before endings: -nesse, -nessa, -nessum. That stem spelling belongs to the noun’s inflection.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-ADVERB-FAMILIES -->

The adjective-forming -lic gives heofonlic, “heavenly”, from heofon, “heaven”. The adjective then takes the ordinary strong or weak endings. In an inflected -lic adjective, separate the suffix from the following agreement ending. The source adjective eorðlican is analysed with its supplied case, number and gender below.

| Citation base | Adverb | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| swīð, “strong” | swīðe | very |
| sōð, “true” | sōðlice | truly |

Swīð and sōð are supplied adjective citations, not new full adjective paradigms. The attested adverbs use -e or -lice and do not agree with a noun. A final e is not enough to identify an adverb: it also occurs in many noun and adjective forms. Use the whole word and its clause function.[^adverbs]

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-PREFIX-LEXICAL-LIMITS -->

A prefix precedes the family stem. In rihtwīsnes / unrihtwīsnes, “righteousness / unrighteousness”, un- marks opposition. Compare these supplied verb citations with the familiar finite verbs:

| Base | Prefixed word | Learned relation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| berstan, “burst” | tōberstan, “break up; go to pieces” | tō- has an asunder sense here |
| weorðan, “become” | forweorðan, “perish” | the prefixed whole word has a destructive-result meaning |

Each whole verb keeps its own meaning, stem changes and government. The prefix for- does not simply equal English “for”, and tō- does not always mean the same thing. Likewise, ge- can belong to a verb’s lexical form or occur in a participle; neither its addition nor its removal is automatic. Use the selected forms below.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-NOMINALIZED-EXPRESSIONS -->

An adjective can stand for people without an expressed noun: the earlier ðā rihtwīsan means “the righteous people”. It remains an adjective, with weak inflection after the article and case determined by the clause. The new neuter noun gōd, used in the plural for “goods; property”, has its own lexical entry and noun paradigm. Endings can coincide across these categories; the absence of a following noun does not decide which category a word belongs to.[^nominal]

## 4. Subjunctives, participles and commands

### Five complete subjunctive systems

In each tense the subjunctive has one singular form for all three persons, ending in -e, and one plural form, ending in -en. Strong preterite subjunctives use the preterite plural stem; weak ones retain the dental past stem. Syncretism does not remove a distinction of tense or mood: sęnde and gelīefde require their clause context. ## 5. Meanings and ordinary uses

A verb’s forms and its government are separate facts. The following uses apply with the systems assigned above and earlier. In particular, prefixes do not preserve a base verb’s government automatically.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-VOC-FORLAETAN -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-VOC-ON -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-PAR-ON -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-VOC-MID -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-PAR-MID -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-GEWENDAN-RETURN -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-AHREOSAN-ON -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-GAN-ON-GOAL -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-BERAN-TRANSPORT -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-ARISAN-SOURCE -->

**Motion and destination.** Gewęndan, “return”, is used intransitively with tō + dative for the endpoint. Āhrēosan, “fall”, takes on + accusative when falling upon a goal. Gān takes on + accusative for entry or ascent into or onto a place. Beran carries an accusative person or thing to a destination marked tō + dative. Ārīsan is intransitive; of + dative marks the source from which something arises.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-GELIEFAN-ON -->

**Belief.** Gelīefan on + accusative person means “believe in” that person. This is a lexical use of on, not a physical-motion construction. Gelīefan may also stand without an expressed complement, “believe”.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-FORLAETAN-TRANSFER -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-FORGIEFAN-GIVING -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-AETIEWAN-SHOW -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-GIEFAN-UNEXPRESSED-THING -->

**Giving, leaving and showing.** Forgiefan here means “give; grant”, with an accusative thing and a dative recipient. Existing forlǣtan has the same cases in the selected expression “leave peace to someone”; its earlier abandonment meaning alone did not teach this use. Ætīewan shows an accusative thing to a dative person. With giefan the thing given may be left unexpressed when the context supplies it; the recipient remains dative.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-GEBIDDAN-REFLEXIVE -->

**Prayer.** Gebiddan uses an accusative personal pronoun referring to its subject. Keep that reflexive in both the purpose clause and the factual when-clause of Cook 26.8. English normally translates the whole construction simply as “pray”. The purpose clause uses subjunctive; the factual past when-clause uses indicative.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-LICCIAN-OBJECT -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-AHEBBAN-OBJECT -->

**Affected objects.** Liccian, “lick”, takes an accusative object. Āhębban, “raise; lift”, also takes an accusative object; ūp may be added as a separate adverb.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-WEORPAN-GOAL-PASSIVE -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-GEGLENGAN-MATERIAL -->

**Passive complements.** Weorpan throws an accusative thing into or onto an on + accusative goal. Its passive retains that goal. Geglęngan adorns a person; the passive makes that person the subject and may add mid + dative for the material or clothing.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-ONFON-DATIVE -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-GEMILTSIAN-DATIVE -->

**Dative government.** Onfōn, “receive”, takes the dative thing received. This differs from the earlier accusative after fōn, “catch”. Gemiltsian, “have mercy on”, takes the dative person.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U14-GRAM-U14-TILIAN-GENITIVE-REFLEXIVE -->

**Providing friends.** In the selected tilian construction the friends acquired are genitive; the personal beneficiary “for yourselves” is a dative reflexive. Both case requirements must be expressed. Singular and plural commands use the same government.

**Earlier clause uses.** The bare relative particle ðe can introduce a relative clause without a preceding demonstrative inside that clause. The noun’s role in its main clause need not match the relative’s role. The purpose clauses in Cook 26.2 use subjunctives without an added “may” auxiliary. For Cook 27.9, use the earlier hālsian request construction: the addressee is accusative, and a following ðæt-clause expresses the requested action in the subjunctive. Sęndan retains its earlier accusative object and dative recipient where a recipient is expressed.

<!-- example: NEW-U14-EXAMPLE01 -->

**Example 1.**

**Ðæt goldhord is on ðǣm hūse.**

The treasure is in the house.

On + dative gives the location of the treasure. The compound is one neuter noun.

<!-- example: NEW-U14-EXAMPLE02 -->

**Example 2.**

**Ic forlǣte ēow sibbe.**

I leave you peace.

Sibbe is the thing left; ēow names the plural recipients.

<!-- example: NEW-U14-EXAMPLE03 -->

**Example 3.**

**Hē onfēng ðǣm golde.**

He received the gold.

The thing received is dative after onfōn.

<!-- example: NEW-U14-EXAMPLE04 -->

**Example 4.**

**Gemiltsa mē.**

Have mercy on me.

The singular command governs dative mē.
