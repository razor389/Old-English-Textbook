# Unit 15. Answer key

The Old English composition answers are new teaching material. The three reading translations render the wording printed by Cook. A model gives one defensible answer; the notes distinguish its grammatical target from other valid language.

Preserve the meaning, case relations, agreement, tense and clause connections. Cook’s explicit lexical hints, omissions and word-order controls are separate requirements. Original composition directions likewise name their required patterns. Elsewhere, a different grammatical order or an evidenced spelling variant is acceptable. Supplied expressions and reading helps remain available during the tasks; their unaided recall is not required.

N = nominative, G = genitive, D = dative, A = accusative. Complete tables below answer each requested position, including positions whose spellings coincide. Negative concord normally requires one negative English proposition, rather than a word-for-word accumulation of English negatives.

## A. Analysis

### A01

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-A01 -->

<!-- output: NEW-U15-A01-O001 -->

1. **Nān** and **ne**: “No man comes.” The denial excludes any person’s coming.

2. **Nǣfre** and **ne**: “The king never came.” The denial covers every occasion in the relevant past.

3. **Nān**, **nāwiht** and **ne**: “No man has anything.” The negative expressions reinforce one denial; they do not affirm possession.

“Nobody comes” and “nobody has anything” are idiomatic alternatives. Turning the third clause into a positive statement loses its essential meaning.

### A02

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-A02 -->

<!-- output: NEW-U15-A02-O001 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-A02-O002 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-A02-O003 -->

**Ic self gā, and nā ōðer** affirms the speaker’s going and excludes **ōðer**, another person, as the goer. **Nā** negates that contrasted participant. **Ic ne gā** instead denies the speaker’s going: finite **ne** negates the proposition.

“I myself go, and no one else” and “I do not go” preserve the distinction. Do not translate the first clause as the speaker’s refusal or failure to go.

1. **Hwā cymð?**: interrogative, “Who comes?” Hwā is the nominative singular subject.

2. **Gif hwā cume, ic him bōc sęlle**: indefinite, “If anyone comes, I shall give him a book.” Hwā is the subordinate subject; ic is the main subject.

“If someone comes” is possible for the indefinite clause. The repeated swā marks the unrestricted choice in the third; it is not another question asking the person’s identity.

1. **Ðēos bōc is swā gōd swā sēo bōc**: equality, “This book is as good as that book.” Sēo bōc is the standard after the second swā, with is gōd understood.

2. **Ic lufie lēoht swīðor ðonne ðīestru**: preference, “I love light more than darkness.” Darkness is the contrasted object, accusative like light.

The proportional statement connects two degrees; it does not call size and goodness equal. In the second clause “rather than” can express the preference, provided the two objects remain clear.

In **ānum uncer**, **ānum** is dative singular because the setting makes the selected person a recipient. **Uncer** is the personal genitive dual, “of us two”, naming the group from which that one is chosen.

The identical spelling does not erase the distinction between a personal genitive dependent on ānum and a possessive agreeing with hūs.

## C. Original composition

### C01

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-C01 -->

Nān mann ne hæfð bōc; gehwā hæfð męte.

<!-- output: NEW-U15-C01-O001 -->

**Nān … ne** gives one denial; **gehwā** is singular “each person”. Bōc and męte are accusative objects. A different grammatical order is possible, but the directions require nān and personal gehwā; a generic plural all would not meet that distributive target.

### C02

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-C02 -->

Ic næfde ðā bōc, and ic nolde gān.

<!-- output: NEW-U15-C02-O001 -->

**Næfde** denies past possession, and **nolde** expresses past unwillingness with plain **gān**. The two meanings must remain separate. Uncontracted ne hæfde/ne wolde can express valid language, but the specified task requires the contractions.

### C03

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-C03 -->

Hæfð se cyning ǣnige bōc? Hē ne hæfð nāne bōc.

<!-- output: NEW-U15-C03-O001 -->

**Ǣnige** and **nāne** agree with accusative feminine singular bōc. The question is not already negative; the reply uses **nān** with finite **ne**. A contracted negative possession verb is also possible in the reply if agreement and the specified nān are preserved.

### C04

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-C04 -->

Gif hwā bōc hæbbe, ic him pęning sęlle.

<!-- output: NEW-U15-C04-O001 -->

The directions make the condition potential: **hwā** is indefinite and **hæbbe** present subjunctive. **Him** is the main-clause recipient; the present **sęlle** expresses the promised giving. An indicative condition can be grammatical in a factual setting but would not meet the stated potential-clause target.

### C05

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-C05 -->

Swā hwā swā gā, ic him pęning sęlle.

<!-- output: NEW-U15-C05-O001 -->

The complete **swā hwā swā** expression means whoever. **Gā** is the requested present subjunctive; **him** resumes the person as dative recipient. Omitting the free-choice frame and writing an ordinary question changes the meaning.

### C06

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-C06 -->

Ordinary willa and the modal willan are different lexemes; neither their spelling nor the passive licenses exchanging those roles.

### Cook X, XI.31.4

The supplied yfele is abstract evil. Do not silently turn it into an identified evil person or make the recipient the source of separation.

### Cook X, XI.31.5

### Cook X, XI.31.6

An unprefixed replacement or a noninitial verb would miss an explicit source control.

### Cook X, XI.31.7

<!-- key: KEY-EX-X-XI-31-07 -->

Nān cyning Engla næs swā micel swā Ælfred.

<!-- output: EX-X-XI-31-07-O001 -->

**Nān … næs** gives one negative statement. **Engla** is the genitive plural group, of the English; **swā micel swā Ælfred** is an equality standard denied for any king in the statement. The comparison concerns greatness, not numerical quantity, and the title’s historical claim is simply being translated.

### Cook X, XI.31.8

<!-- key: KEY-EX-X-XI-31-08 -->

The four predicates retain fighting, judging, translating and being a poet. **Wið ðǣm Dęnum** uses the given people’s dative plural; **his folc** is the object judged; **āwęndednessa** is the translations made. The supplied **of Lǣdene** follows Cook’s glossary spelling with long ǣ. The reading’s exact **Lædene** remains a source spelling of its own. Do not regularize that reading silently to match this composed model.

### Cook X, XI.31.9

<!-- key: KEY-EX-X-XI-31-09 -->

<!-- output: EX-X-XI-31-09-O001 -->

The supplied **hātte** is the special was-called form, matching hight. The second clause can leave that same verb understood or repeat it. Keep Osburg as the mother and Æthelwulf as the father; active “called someone” is not the required name relation.

### Cook X, XI.31.10

<!-- key: KEY-EX-X-XI-31-10 -->

<!-- output: EX-X-XI-31-10-O001 -->

**Twā and fīftig gēara** is the supplied complete expression for fifty-two years. Its higher-number construction takes genitive plural gēara. The task does not license inferring a new complete higher-numeral paradigm. The meaning is his life’s duration, not his age at the reading lesson in the next item.

### Cook X, XI.31.11

<!-- key: KEY-EX-X-XI-31-11 -->

<!-- output: EX-X-XI-31-11-O001 -->

**Bēcrǣdinge** is what he learned. The supplied **æfter ðǣm ðe** introduces the past temporal clause; **twęlf wintra eald** is the supported age expression, with the number and genitive plural years. Preserve after: neither before nor a simple coincident when conveys the same relation.

### Cook X, XI.31.12

<!-- key: KEY-EX-X-XI-31-12 -->

His mōdor hæfde āne bōc Engliscra lēoða, ðe hēo gehēt swā hwæðrum hiere twēgra suna swā hīe ǣrest leornode.

<!-- output: EX-X-XI-31-12-O001 -->

**Engliscra lēoða** is genitive plural, the poems contained in the book. Relative **ðe** is the object of **gehēt**, with the mother as subject: the book which she promised. The entire **swā hwæðrum … swā … leornode** is supplied: the recipient form is dative by attraction while the chosen son performs the learning; **hīe** is the feminine book learned. The preterite subjunctive conveys the prospective choice. The regular internal-nominative/resumptive construction practised in C06 can express a defensible alternative if both clauses are fully reconstructed; do not generalize this exceptional dative into a rule for every relative subject.

### Cook X, XI.31.13

<!-- key: KEY-EX-X-XI-31-13 -->

<!-- output: EX-X-XI-31-13-O001 -->

**Ānum uncer** is one recipient selected from us two: dative ānum plus the personal genitive dual uncer. **Ðǣm ðe …** further identifies that recipient. The feminine book is **hīe**, object of **ongiett**; the person who understands is the relative subject. The agreeing possessive uncer, “our”, would not supply the stated partitive relation.

### Cook X, XI.31.14

<!-- key: KEY-EX-X-XI-31-14 -->

<!-- output: EX-X-XI-31-14-O001 -->

**Asser** is the writer and **Ælfredes bisceop** his appositional description. **Āne bōc** is the accusative object; **ðæs cyninges līfes** is its content genitive, with the king as possessor of the life. The earlier taught bisceop is sufficient for the English bishop. A spatial of-phrase would not demonstrate Cook’s specified genitive relation.

## D. Duties of the Rich toward the Poor

### D01

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-D01 -->

<!-- output: NEW-U15-D01-O001 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-D01-O002 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-D01-O003 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-D01-O004 -->

The fault is withholding the speaker’s goods and disregarding the gift entrusted to the rich man. The announced consequence is exclusion from that bounty and poverty in worldly life. The promise is hundredfold repayment of a good deed done in the speaker’s name and eternal heavenly joy in return for gifts to the least. The speaker is the Savior; the initial addressee is the rich man, followed by an embedded gospel saying addressed to a plural audience.

Wlanc also permits proud as a lexical rendering; keep the passage’s wealth and giving context. Do not introduce poor people as the subject of **mann gedēð**: that clause says one/a person does the good deed.

### D02

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-D02 -->

<!-- output: NEW-U15-D02-O001 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-D02-O002 -->

**Noldest** is negative willan, preterite indicative second singular, governing plain **geðęncean**: “why were you unwilling to consider …?” Inside its content clause **ic wille** states the speaker’s present or continuing intention to repay.

A past question does not force mechanical backshift of a proposition still presented as true. Wille does not become past merely because noldest is past.

### D03

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-D03 -->

<!-- output: NEW-U15-D03-O001 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-D03-O002 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-D03-O003 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-D03-O004 -->

**Swā-hwæt-swā** names the thing given, whatever you give. The immediate recipient is **ānum of mīnum ðǣm lǣstum**, one of the least belonging to the speaker. **Mē** identifies the speaker himself as the recipient of that same gift. **Hit** resumes the thing, not the person receiving it.

“Whatever you give to one of the least of mine, you always give it to me” retains the two recipient descriptions. Supplying brothers as an explanatory gospel allusion is possible, but it is not a separate printed noun in this extract.

### D04

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-D04 -->

<!-- output: NEW-U15-D04-O001 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-D04-O002 -->

**Ānum** is dative singular, the selected recipient. **Of** governs the dative-plural group **mīnum ðǣm lǣstum**. **Wið ðǣm** is the dative exchange expression, in return for that.

English of does not make the first group genitive in Old English. The second relation concerns repayment, not a person opposed to or standing beside the speaker.

### D05

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-D05 -->

<!-- output: NEW-U15-D05-O001 -->

The Savior said to the rich man: “Why were you so withholding of my goods, which I gave you? Why did you disregard the gift that I gave you? I now remove you from my bounty, which I bestowed on you; then you will be poor in worldly life. Why were you unwilling to consider that I intend to repay each person for a good deed that one does in my name? I repay it to him a hundredfold, as it is declared and stated in my gospel: ‘Whatever you give to one of the least of mine, you always give it to me, and I shall grant you eternal joy in heaven in return for that.’

The assigned opening argument ends here; the outer speech continues beyond it. The unmatched outer quotation follows that boundary. Future “I shall remove/repay” is also defensible for the presents in context. Bounty/distribution, poor/needy and eternal joy/delight are suitable alternatives. Retain the change from rebuke to promise, each recipient, the hundredfold repayment and the gospel quotation.

## P. Alfred’s Preface to Boethius

### P01

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-P01 -->

<!-- output: NEW-U15-P01-O001 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-P01-O002 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-P01-O003 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-P01-O004 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-P01-O005 -->

The two methods are **word be worde**, word for word, and **andgiet of andgiete**, sense from sense. Various and manifold worldly cares troubled Alfred in mind and body; the troubles that befell his kingdoms are said to be hard to enumerate. After learning and translating the book into English prose, he made it into verse. He asks readers to pray for him and not blame him if they understand it more correctly than he could.

The cares constrain what he could explain; they are not a separate benefit bestowed by the translation. Preserve the sequence from the earlier translation to its later versification.

### P02

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-P02 -->

<!-- output: NEW-U15-P02-O001 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-P02-O002 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-P02-O003 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-P02-O004 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-P02-O005 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-P02-O006 -->

In the requested order: **hē** before gebidde = the reader; **hine** after for = Alfred; **him** before ne wīte = Alfred; **hē** after gif = the reader; **hē** after ðonne = Alfred. Thus the reader may understand more correctly than Alfred could.

A repeated masculine pronoun need not keep one referent throughout. Reading every hē as Alfred destroys the comparison and the request addressed to the reader.

### P03

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-P03 -->

<!-- output: NEW-U15-P03-O001 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-P03-O002 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-P03-O003 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-P03-O004 -->

**Ǣgðer ge on mōde ge on līchaman** includes both settings, in mind and in body. In the next sentence **ðā bisga** is the plural subject and **sind** the finite verb. The first following relative clause describes the troubles that befell the kingdoms: its relative is subject of becōmon. The second describes the kingdoms that Alfred had received: its relative is object of underfangen hæfde, with hē as subject.

The two relatives have different antecedents and roles. Do not attach both to the troubles or interpret the kingdoms as the agent that received Alfred.

### P04

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-P04 -->

<!-- output: NEW-U15-P04-O001 -->
<!-- output: NEW-U15-P04-O002 -->

**Rihtlicor … ðonne** compares the reader’s more correct understanding with what Alfred could understand; ongietan/understand is recoverable after meahte. **Ne** negates wīte, blame, in the content of the request.

**And him ne wīte** coordinates with the prayer request after ðæt. It is not a separate past assertion that someone did not blame him. “Not find fault with him” is a natural alternative.

### P05

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-P05 -->

<!-- output: NEW-U15-P05-O001 -->

King Alfred was the translator of this book, and translated it from book-Latin into English, as it is now done. At times he set word for word, at times sense from sense, as clearly and intelligibly as he could explain it amid the various and manifold worldly cares that often troubled him both in mind and in body. The troubles that befell in his days the kingdoms he had received are very hard for us to enumerate. Yet, after he had learned this book and translated it from Latin into English prose, he afterwards made it into verse, as it is now done. And now he asks, and entreats in God’s name, each of those who may wish to read this book to pray for him and not blame him if the reader understands it more correctly than Alfred could; for every person must, according to the measure of his understanding and according to his leisure, speak what he speaks and do what he does.

Literary Latin/Latin, translator/interpreter, cares/occupations, and prose/discourse are defensible contextual choices. The translation must retain both methods, the impediments, the later versification, the charitable request and the differing comparison subjects. “He” in the last request may be retained in English if its changing reference remains clear. These are the preface’s statements; the translation does not settle modern questions of authorship.

In **Sīe ðē lof and wuldor**, the addressee receives the wished-for praise and glory. **Sīe** is the source’s present subjunctive wish form. This is an independent concluding wish, not another subordinate purpose clause.

“So that I may love you” and “May praise and glory be yours” preserve their different functions. Do not make God the actor of the first-person loving merely because he is addressed throughout the prayer.

### R05

<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U15-R05 -->

<!-- output: NEW-U15-R05-O001 -->

Lord, almighty God, Maker and Ruler of all created things, I ask you, by your great mercy, by the sign of the holy cross, by Saint Mary’s virginity, by Saint Michael’s obedience, and by the love of all your saints and their merits, to guide me better than my conduct toward you has deserved. Guide me according to your will and for the benefit of my soul, better than I myself could know how; establish my mind according to your will and for the benefit of my soul; strengthen me against the devil’s temptations; remove from me filthy lust and every unrighteousness; shield me against my adversaries, visible and invisible; and teach me to do your will, so that I may love you inwardly above all things, with pure thought and a pure body. For you are my Creator, my Redeemer, my Help, my Comfort, my Trust and my Hope. May praise and glory be yours now and for ever and ever and ever, world without any end. Amen.

Guide/direct, establish/settle, shield/protect, and benefit/need of my soul are suitable alternatives. Keep all grounds of appeal, the distinction between the initial requested action and six commands, both comparisons, both occurrences of the soul’s need, the purpose and the concluding praise. Mīnre sāwle is genitive dependent on ðearfe in both places; it does not name a separate recipient of the verb.

## Sources and grading limits

Cook’s 1895 *Exercises in Old English*, printed pp. 15–18 (one-based PDF 23–26), supplies the 50 numbered English prompts.
