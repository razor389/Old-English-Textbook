## Practice M

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-M01 -->

**M1.** **Hīe swimmað on ðǣm sǣ:** “They swim in the sea.” Swimmað is present indicative, third plural with hīe; the utterance asserts the activity. **Swimmen hīe on ðǣm sǣ:** “Let them swim in the sea” / “May they swim in the sea.” Swimmen is present subjunctive, third plural here, expressing the requested or wished-for activity. Both retain dative ðǣm sǣ for location.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-M02 -->

**M2.** **Gāð:** “Go!” addressed to more than one person, second-plural imperative of gān. **Gān wē:** “Let us go,” first-plural present subjunctive in a joint exhortation, including the speaker. The second utterance is not a second-person command with an added first-person pronoun. Its plural subjunctive form is identified as first person by wē. The imperative reading of bare Gāð is established by its use as the command in this exercise; gāð can elsewhere be a present indicative plural spelling.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-M03 -->

**M3.** **Hē mæg swimman:** “He can swim,” asserting capability; mæg is present indicative third singular of magan, with bare infinitive swimman. **Hē mōt swimman:** “He may/is allowed to swim,” asserting permission; mōt is present indicative third singular of mōtan, with the same infinitive. **Swimme hē:** “May he swim” / “Let him swim,” a present subjunctive third-singular wish. The permission sentence is not itself the grammatical equivalent of the speaker's wish.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-M04 -->

**M4.** “If he has food, he gives the birds food.” **Hæfð** is present indicative third singular of habban; **sęlð** is present indicative third singular of sęllan. Hē is the nominative subject in each clause. Męte is accusative object in each; **ðǣm fuglum** is the dative-plural recipient of the giving. Gif introduces an open condition. It does not independently require subjunctive morphology, and neither verb here is subjunctive.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-M05 -->

**M5.** “If you help me, we can bind the animals.” **Helpe** is present subjunctive second singular with ðū; the stated setting presents that help tentatively. **Magon** is present indicative first plural with wē; the main clause asserts capability if the condition is met. Mē is dative after helpan; ðā dēor is accusative plural after infinitive bindan. A subjunctive condition does not force the main clause to have the same mood.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-M06 -->

**M6.** **Cōme** is preterite subjunctive third singular of cuman with hē; **hulpe** is preterite subjunctive first singular of helpan with ic. Him is dative after helpan. In the expressly supplied unreal, nonpast setting, “If he came, I would help him” concerns a hypothetical coming and help, not an asserted past event. “Preterite” names the morphological system; it does not by itself determine the temporal reference of this use.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-M07 -->

**M7.** **Wolde** is preterite indicative third singular of willan under the stated past-assertion reading: “He wanted/wished to travel.” Faran is the uninflected infinitive. The singular preterite subjunctive has the same spelling wolde. Homography does not make the narrated assertion subjunctive or require “would” as an English translation. Person and mood must be read with the subject and stated context.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-M08 -->

**M8.** **Libbe hē:** “May he live.” **Helpen wē ðǣm męnn:** “Let us help the man.” Libbe is present subjunctive third singular in this clause; helpen is present subjunctive first plural with wē. Ðǣm męnn remains dative singular, the case governed by helpan. The wish uses libb-, not indicative leofað; the exhortation uses -en, not indicative -að.

**P1.** **Bindende**, from bind- + -ende; **nęriende**, from nęri- + -ende; **lufiende**, from lufi- + -ende; **cweðende**, from cweð- + -ende. These are the uninflected dictionary forms requested. Retain i in the two -ian verbs; do not replace either with a past stem.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-P02 -->

**P2.** **Bunden**, **boren**, **hīered**, **gehālgod**, respectively. Bindan and beran are strong verbs: their past participles use the separately selected stems bund- and bor- plus -en. They are not weak dental past participles. Hīered and gehālgod are the selected weak dental forms; gehālgod retains the verb's ge- prefix, and hīered is not a truncation of hīerde.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-P03 -->

**P3.** (a) **bindende mann**, strong masculine nominative singular; (b) **se bindenda mann**, weak masculine nominative singular after se; (c) **bindendre**, strong feminine dative singular; (d) **bindendra**, weak genitive plural after the definite determiner implied by “the.” The genitive-plural spelling coincides with the strong form, but the syntactic setting selects weak inflection in (d). No unattested noun or new preposition is needed in (c) or (d).

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-P04 -->

**P4.** (a) **bunden dēor**, strong accusative neuter singular; (b) **ðæt bundene dēor**, weak accusative neuter singular; (c) **bundenra fugla**, strong genitive plural; (d) **ðǣm bundenum fuglum**, weak dative plural. The requested participial forms are bunden, bundene, bundenra, bundenum. The documented syncopated variants **bundne** in (b) and **bundnum** in (d) are linguistically acceptable alternatives; bundenra is not changed by the stated vowel-initial-ending syncope rule.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-P05 -->

**P5.** **Ðæt dēor is bunden.** “The animal is bound.” In **Hē bint ðæt dēor**, hē is nominative subject and dēor accusative singular object. In the passive, ðæt dēor is nominative singular subject, though the neuter form has the same spelling; is is present indicative third singular. The agent is omitted. Bunden is the selected past participle; its surface shape is compatible with the uninflected predicate and neuter singular agreement.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-P06 -->

**P6.** **Ðā dēor wurdon bunden.** “The animals were/became bound.” Wurdon is preterite indicative third plural of weorðan and presents the binding event as requested. The former accusative plural object becomes nominative plural subject; the agent hīe is not retained in an agent phrase. **Ðā dēor wurdon bundene** is also permissible within the taught verbal-predicate variation. Wǣron would be valid for a different assigned auxiliary but would not meet this item's explicit weorðan target.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-P07 -->

**P7.** In both clauses, **ðā dēor** is nominative neuter plural and **sindon** is present indicative third plural. The verbal predicate can use uninflected **bunden**, or the permitted masculine-like plural **bundene** despite the subject's neuter gender. An attributive participle is instead declined with its noun according to the strong/weak setting: the selected strong neuter nominative/accusative plural is **bundenu dēor**, and a definite noun phrase has weak **ðā bundenan dēor**. The learner also explicitly admits strong neuter plural bunden and bundene as variants. Consequently, these surface spellings can overlap: neither spelling alone proves that a participle is predicative or attributive. The distinction is syntactic and concerns the agreement requirement, not a prohibition on an admitted adjective variant.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-P08 -->

**P8.** **Bēo geblētsod:** “Be blessed.” Bēo is second-singular imperative under the explicit command setting, with an understood singular male addressee. Geblētsod is the past participle of geblētsian, retained uninflected in the passive predicate; its form also coincides with masculine nominative singular agreement. Bēo is otherwise homographic with first-singular present indicative and singular present subjunctive forms, so its spelling alone does not make this a command.

**Bēon ðā fuglas gemanigfielde:** “May the birds be multiplied” / “Let the birds be multiplied.” Bēon is present subjunctive, third plural with ðā fuglas; gemanigfielde is the plural predicate past participle of gemanigfieldan, nominative plural in this use. The birds are the subject of a third-person wish, not plural addressees of a direct command. Bēon is a plural subjunctive form for all persons and also an infinitive spelling; the finite clause with ðā fuglas and the stated wish establish this analysis.

## Practice D

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-D01 -->

**D1. Complete translation of the 56-word new account.**

Two men were on the shore. They had five animals. The first man said, “If you help me, we can bind the animals.” The second man said, “I am willing to help you. Let us bind the animals.” They bound the animals, and the first man gave his companion food. The animals were bound, and the men had food.

The first quotation's “you” is the second man and “me” the first. The second quotation reverses the speaker-relative pronouns. “His companion” is naturally the second man, the first man's companion; that reference is supplied by the narrative, not a reflexive ending on his. The final passive describes the resulting bound state. Nothing in the account states that the animals were killed or that the food came from them.

<!-- solution: NEW-U07-D02 -->

**D2.** In the first speech, **Gif ðū mē helpe** is the tentative condition with present subjunctive second-singular helpe. **Wē magon ðā dēor bindan** asserts the two men's capability on that condition: magon is present indicative first plural, with bare infinitive bindan. In the second speech, **Ic wille ðē helpan** states the second man's willingness, using present indicative first-singular wille with infinitive helpan. **Binden wē ðā dēor** is a joint exhortation, present subjunctive first plural. Wille's intended assertion follows the discourse; its form is also homographic with a singular subjunctive and cannot be assigned a mood from spelling alone.

**D3.** **Mē** is dative first-singular pronoun after helpe and refers to the first speaker. **Ðē** is dative second-singular pronoun after helpan and refers to the first man as addressee of the second speaker. **His gefēran** is the dative-singular recipient phrase after sealde: gefēran is dative singular, while his is a dependent genitive singular pronoun meaning “his,” not a dative adjective ending. Męte is what is given, accusative singular.

Thus the two dative relations are (1) the person helped, governed by helpan, and (2) the recipient of food, in the sęllan giving frame. The natural contextual possessor of “his companion” is the first man, who is the overt subject of sealde; his companion is the second man. The earlier **Hīe bundon ðā dēor**, “They bound the animals,” establishes the binding event. Hīe refers to the two men already introduced and joined in the agreement to bind the animals. The narrative consequently supplies the agents even though the final passive contains no agent phrase. The final clause states the result and does not introduce a second unidentified binding event.
