## Practice Q

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-Q01 -->

**Q1.** **Hæfð hē scip? Sindon ðā męnn gōde? Gesāwe ðū ðæt scip?** The first two remain present, respectively third singular and third plural; gesāwe remains second singular preterite. Each question places its finite verb first without adding a dummy “do”.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-Q02 -->

**Q2.** **Hwā hæfð ðæt scip? Hwone siehð se mann? Hwæt hæfð se mann?** Hwā is nominative common personal, subject of hæfð. Hwone is accusative common personal, object of siehð. Hwæt is neuter accusative, object of hæfð; the man is subject in the last two questions.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-Q03 -->

**Q3.** **Hwǣm sęlst ðū ðone fisc? Hwæs scip siehst ðū?** Giving puts the recipient hwǣm in the dative. Hwæs is a genitive possessor inside the noun phrase; scip itself is accusative, the thing seen. The verb's object case must not be transferred to the possessor.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-Q04 -->

**Q4.** **Hwæðer mann hæfð ðæt scip? Hūlic is ðæt scip?** Hwæðer is nominative masculine singular with mann, selecting one of the stipulated two men. Hūlic is a strong neuter singular predicate describing scip and asking its sort or character.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-Q05 -->

**Q5.** “How does the man swim?” and “Why does the man swim?” Hū requests the manner of swimming; hwȳ its reason. Both questions have third singular present swimð. Lexical why does not require an independently composed instrumental phrase.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-Q06 -->

**Q6.** **Hæfst ðū ǣnigne fisc?** Fisc is singular masculine accusative and ǣnigne agrees. Hæfst already supplies the finite verb of the question; Old English does not add dōn to reproduce the English auxiliary “do”.

## Practice N

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-N01 -->

**N1.** “A certain man has a ship.” “Some men have ships.” In the first, sum mann is nominative masculine singular subject, hæfð third singular, and scip accusative neuter singular object. In the second, sume męnn is nominative masculine plural, habbað third plural, and scipu accusative neuter plural. The noun and verb number changes together, with the appropriate adjective agreement.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-N02 -->

**N2.** **Gehwilc mann siehð scip. Gehwilc gefēra siehð scip.** Each subject is nominative masculine singular, so third singular siehð remains unchanged. Gefēra is the weak noun's singular nominative, not a plural merely because it ends in -a.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-N03 -->

**N3.** **Se ōðer mann hæfð ōðer scip.** The first ōðer is masculine nominative singular; the second is neuter accusative singular. Here the context contrasts another man/ship rather than numbering either as second. The morphology stays strong, including after se.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-N04 -->

**N4.** **Ic geseah ðone ilcan mann. Ic sęlle ðone fisc ðǣm ilcan męnn.** Ðone ilcan mann is masculine accusative singular, the object seen. Ðone fisc is also accusative singular, the thing given; ðǣm ilcan męnn is masculine dative singular, its recipient. Ilcan is weak in both requested man phrases.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-N05 -->

**N5.** “He himself saw that ship.” **Ic nęrie mē selfne.** In the first, self is nominative masculine singular emphasizing the subject hē; that ship is the accusative object. In the second, mē is an accusative personal pronoun referring back to ic, and masculine accusative selfne reinforces that reflexive object because the speaker is male. Selfne is not a nominative subject or an invariant English-style suffix.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-N06 -->

**N6.** “Such men are good.” Swilce is strong nominative masculine plural, agreeing with męnn, and gōde is their plural predicate. Adverbial swilce “likewise” does not agree with a noun and would not itself mean “such men”. Identical spelling can have these separate analyses.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-N07 -->

**N7.**

| Prompt | Noun phrase | Analysis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| of some men | **sumra manna** | Both masculine genitive plural; sumra strong, manna the noun's genitive plural. |
| to any companion | **ǣnigum gefēran** | Masculine dative singular; strong ǣnigum and weak-noun dative gefēran. |
| that same ship | **ðæt ilce scip** | Neuter accusative singular throughout; ilce weak. |
| such little birds | **swilce lȳtle fuglas** | Masculine accusative plural; both adjectives strong in this indefinite phrase, with the syncopated lȳtl- stem. |

The English “of/to” in the first two prompts labels the requested case, so it is not necessary to add a preposition to these inflected phrases.

## Practice L

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-L01 -->

**L1.** **Se mann, ðe ðæt scip hæfð, is gōd.** Ðe is the relative subject of third singular hæfð; ðæt scip is its accusative object. Se mann is nominative in the main clause.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-L02 -->

**L2.** **Ic geseah ðæt scip, ðe se mann hæfð.** The main clause still says “I saw the ship”. Within the relative, se mann is subject and invariant ðe represents the accusative object of hæfð.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-L03 -->

**L3.** **Ic sęlle ðone fisc ðǣm męnn, se ðe mē healp.** Antecedent ðǣm męnn: dative masculine singular recipient of the fish. Relative se: nominative masculine singular subject of healp. Mē is the dative person helped. The dative antecedent does not require a dative relative subject.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-L04 -->

**L4.** **Ic geseah ðone mann, ðǣm ðe ic healp.** Antecedent ðone mann is the accusative object of geseah. Relative ðǣm is dative because helpan governs the person helped in the dative; ic is that clause's subject. Both references are masculine singular, but their clause functions require different cases.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-L05 -->

**L5.** **Ðū, ðe mē hulpe, sęle mē ðone fisc.** Hulpe is second singular preterite, following the personal antecedent ðū. Sęle is second singular imperative. The first mē is dative with helpan; the second is dative recipient with sęllan.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-L06 -->

**L6.** **Gē, ðe mē hulpon, sęllað mē ðone fisc.** Gē replaces singular ðū; hulpon is second plural preterite and sęllað second plural imperative. Both mē forms remain first singular dative, ðe remains invariant, and ðone fisc remains singular masculine accusative.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-L07 -->

**L7.** In **Se mann, ðe ðæt scip hæfð, is mīn gefēra**, the antecedent is nominative masculine singular se mann, and ðe is the relative subject of hæfð. In **Ic geseah ðæt scip, ðe se mann hæfð**, the antecedent is accusative neuter singular ðæt scip, and ðe is the relative object of hæfð. Its spelling remains ðe in both roles.

**Se mann, ðæs scip ic geseah, is gōd** means “The man whose ship I saw is good.” Ðæs is genitive masculine singular: it refers to mann and marks possession inside the relative. Scip is neuter accusative singular, the object of geseah; it is not genitive merely because it is possessed. Ic is subject of the relative; the antecedent mann remains nominative in the main clause.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-L08 -->

**L8.** An inflected relative does not have to copy the antecedent's case. In L3, dative ðǣm męnn has nominative relative se; in L4, accusative ðone mann has dative relative ðǣm. The ordinary productive target selects case from the role inside the relative while retaining the antecedent's gender and number. Case attraction, in which the antecedent's case influences the relative, is an attested recognition exception, not a requirement or this task's production target.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-C01 -->

**C1.** **Hīe gefyllað ðæt weorc. Hīe gefyllað ðā wæteru.** The first means that they finish or accomplish the work; the second that they fill the waters. The verb has the same third plural present inflection, but its lexical sense differs. Each noun phrase is accusative; weorc is singular and wæteru plural.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-C02 -->

**C2.** **Se mann hæfð eall ðā nīetenu on his gewealde. Hafa eall ðā nīetenu on ðīnum gewealde.** Eall ðā nīetenu is neuter accusative plural. In the statement, his is the invariant possessor; gewealde is dative singular after on in the control construction. In the command, hafa addresses one person and ðīnum agrees with neuter dative singular gewealde. A spatial “under” construction is not substituted for the taught idiom.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-C03 -->

**C3.** **Ic libbe mīn līf. Wē libbað ūru līf.** Mīn līf is neuter accusative singular; ūru līf is neuter accusative plural. Līf is the cognate object naming the life or lives lived. The construction is licensed for libban and this related noun, without making arbitrary intransitive verbs transitive.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-C04 -->

**C4.** **Drȳgnes ætēowað. Ætēowie drȳgnes.** The first has third singular present indicative: dry land appears. The second has third singular present subjunctive, expressing the wish or direction “Let dry land appear.” Drȳgnes is nominative subject in each; ætēowian is intransitive.

**I.8.9.** **His sǣd weaxað on ðǣre eorðan.** His is invariant. Sǣd is nominative neuter plural, distinguished here by plural weaxað. The stationary location is on plus dative feminine singular ðǣre eorðan.

## Practice D

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-D01 -->

**D1. Complete translation.** On the shore are three men and one ship. A certain man says, “Who is the man who has that ship?” His companion says, “The man is my companion. He has the fish that we see.”

The first speaker asks, “To whom do you give that fish?” His companion answers, “I give it to the man who helped me.”

The first speaker asks, “Which ship does he have?” His companion answers, “He has that little ship which you saw.”

The first speaker and the answering companion are two of the men; the man discussed is the third. The answering man identifies the third as his companion and as the man who helped him. In the final answer, “you” is the first speaker, the person being addressed. His at the start of the first reply refers to the first speaker's companion; it is not an extra fourth man.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-D02 -->

**D2.** The three direct questions are:

1. **Hwā is se mann, ðe ðæt scip hæfð?** Hwā is the nominative personal interrogative in a copular identification question. It asks the identity of the man who has the ship. Since this is a copular equation of nominative expressions, the essential analysis is nominative identification, not an accusative object of “have”; hæfð belongs to the embedded relative.
2. **Hwǣm sęlst ðū ðone fisc?** Hwǣm is the dative recipient. It asks to which person the fish is given; ðū is subject and ðone fisc accusative object.
3. **Hwilc scip hæfð hē?** Hwilc modifies accusative neuter singular scip, the object of hæfð. It asks which ship the third man has; hē is subject.

The relative clauses inside these questions/answers are not additional direct questions.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-D03 -->

**D3.** In **Ic sęlle hine ðǣm męnn, se ðe mē healp**, hine is masculine accusative singular, referring to the fish; ðǣm męnn is masculine dative singular recipient. Se is masculine nominative singular relative subject of healp. Mē is the first singular dative person helped, governed by helpan. The relative “who helped me” identifies the man receiving the fish.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-D04 -->

**D4.** **Hē hæfð twā lȳtle scipu, ðā ðe ðū gesāwe.** This says that he has two little ships which the singular addressee saw. Twā and scipu are accusative neuter plural; lȳtle is strong accusative neuter plural in the indefinite numeral phrase. The relative remains inflected demonstrative plus ðe, now plural accusative ðā ðe. Hæfð stays singular because the owner has not changed; gesāwe stays second singular because the person who saw them has not changed.

If the original demonstrative definiteness is retained as “those two little ships”, **Hē hæfð ðā twā lȳtlan scipu, ðā ðe ðū gesāwe** is also coherent. Then the attributive lȳtlan is weak after the demonstrative. The two adjective choices must be matched to their respective definite/indefinite noun phrases, not interchanged mechanically.

## Practice R

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-R01 -->

**R1. Complete translation.**

**Teacher:** What do you say, merchant?

**Merchant:** I say that I am useful to the king and the nobles, and to wealthy people, and to all the people.

**Teacher:** And how?

**Merchant:** I board my ship with my cargo, and row across stretches of sea, and sell my goods, and buy valuable things which are not produced in this land; and I bring the goods here to you at great risk across the sea. Sometimes I suffer shipwreck, with the loss of all my goods, barely escaping alive.

Ge … and supplies the linked “both … and” relationship in the first answer. In the second, source singular hit refers collectively to the merchandise and is naturally rendered “the goods” here. The closing participle describes the merchant's escape accompanying the shipwreck/loss; it does not say he failed to escape or that someone else escaped. The answer stops at the assigned excerpt, without adding the subsequent goods catalogue or claims about profit.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-R02 -->

**R2.** **Hwæt sægst ðū, mangere?** asks what the merchant says or has to say: hwæt is neuter accusative object of second singular sægst, with ðū subject. Mangere is the nominative form used in direct address, as the supplied help states; it is not an object of the verb.

**And hū?** asks how he is useful, with that predicate recovered from the preceding answer under the supplied ellipsis. Hū asks manner. It is not a new question about what goods he brings, and the following unassigned Hwilc question must not be counted here.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-R03 -->

**R3.** The antecedent is **ðing dēorwierðu**, “valuable things”, accusative neuter plural as the object of bycge. Relative **ðā** is nominative neuter plural, the subject of “are not produced”. **Bēoð** is the plural present indicative auxiliary, third person with these things. The supplied **ācęnnede** is the plural predicate past participle, “produced”. On ðissum lande locates their non-production in this land and does not make the relative dative.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-R04 -->

**R4.** He boards his ship with cargo and travels across sea regions. He sells his goods and buys valuable things that are not produced in this country, then brings the merchandise across the sea here to the people he addresses. Those are the transport and buying/selling activities that explain his usefulness. Great risk, shipwreck, loss of all his goods and difficult escape are dangers or consequences he experiences, not ways of buying the goods. The text does not give purchase prices or profit margins.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-R05 -->

**R5.** Two explicit danger expressions are **mid miclum plihte**, “at great risk”, and **hwīlum forlidennesse ic ðolie**, “sometimes I suffer shipwreck”. **Mid lyre ealra ðinga mīnra** adds the loss of all his goods. The closing **unēaðe cwic ætberstende** means “barely/with difficulty escaping alive”: the present participle describes the merchant, cwic says he remains alive, and unēaðe qualifies the difficulty of escape. It should not be reduced to the unrelated claim that he is merely “barely alive”.

**X1.** **Hwilce męnn habbað ðā scipu, ðe ðū gesāwe?** Hwilce is strong nominative masculine plural with the subject męnn. The antecedent ðā scipu is accusative neuter plural, the object of habbað. Inside the relative, ðū is subject and invariant ðe represents the accusative object of second singular preterite gesāwe. The whole sentence is a direct question.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-X02 -->

**X2.** **Ic sęlle ōðerne fisc ðǣm ilcan męnn, se ðe mē healp.** Ōðerne fisc is masculine accusative singular, another fish. Ðǣm ilcan męnn is masculine dative singular, with weak ilcan. Se ðe is nominative masculine singular relative subject, although its antecedent is dative; mē is dative with healp. This retains the required inflected demonstrative plus ðe.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-X03 -->

**X3.** **Siehst ðū ǣnige lȳtle fuglas?** Siehst is second singular present and ðū subject. Ǣnige lȳtle fuglas is masculine accusative plural. Both adjectives are strong in this indefinite phrase; the plural of fugol is fuglas, and lȳtel shows the selected syncopated stem before -e.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-X04 -->

**X4.** **Gehwilc gefēra self geseah ðæt scip.** Question: **Geseah gehwilc gefēra self ðæt scip?** Each companion remains a singular masculine subject; self is strong nominative masculine singular emphasis. Geseah stays third singular preterite in both. The yes/no question moves the finite verb to the ordinary initial position without adding “do”.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-X05 -->

**X5.** **Se mann, ðe ðæt scip hæfð, cwið: “Ætēowie drȳgnes.”** The relative ðe is subject of hæfð and the ship its accusative object; the main man is subject of cwið. The quoted wish has present subjunctive ætēowie and nominative drȳgnes. The quotation remains direct; no untaught reported-content construction is introduced.

<!-- solution: NEW-U08-X06 -->

**X6.** In **ðæt scip**, ðæt is the neuter accusative singular demonstrative/determiner “that”, agreeing with scip in the answer's object phrase. In **ðæt ðe ðū gesāwe**, ðæt is the inflected neuter accusative singular relative pronoun, followed by invariant ðe. Its accusative comes from being the object seen, while its gender and number refer back to scip. These are nominal inflected uses, not two content conjunctions. The identical spelling alone cannot establish conjunction status.
