# Unit 8. Questions and relative clauses

This unit gives direct questions, the remaining selected pronoun and pronominal-adjective paradigms, and ordinary relative clauses. The additional verb systems and lexical constructions prepare the complete Cook I.8 exercise and two connected readings.

Tables distinguish singular and plural, masculine, neuter and feminine, and nominative, genitive, dative and accusative. In verb tables, **1**, **2** and **3** mean first, second and third person. A row labelled “persons 1, 2, 3” includes every person in that number. Dual subjects take plural finite forms. Forms labelled **reference** are for recognition; their display does not require independent use of a new construction. Preserve vowel length and the distinction between e and ę.

The numbered examples and “The ship and the fish” are new teaching material. The merchant dialogue is an exact source excerpt with separate local help and original notes.

## 1. Direct questions

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-direct-questions -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-clause-order -->

A direct **yes/no question** commonly begins with its finite verb, followed by the subject. The verb retains the person, number and tense required by that subject. Old English does not need an added verb corresponding to the English question auxiliary “do”.

<!-- example: NEW-U08-EXAMPLE-01 -->

Hæfð se mann scip?

Does the man have a ship?

A question word or phrase commonly stands early, often before the finite verb. A question word that is itself the subject needs no second subject. These are common orders, not an exceptionless rule requiring one fixed arrangement. A phrase such as “whose ship” is one constituent, even though it contains two words.

More generally, the finite verb commonly stands early in an independent clause and later, often finally, in a dependent clause. The relative clauses below illustrate that tendency. The case endings and the meaning of the clause remain essential when the order varies. This unit uses direct questions; indirect questions and general content complements come later.

### Who? What?

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-VOC-hwa -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-hwa-paradigm -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-interrogative-case -->

**Hwā** asks about a person; **hwæt** asks about a thing. Their paradigm is singular only. Masculine and feminine share one personal series; there is no separate feminine or plural paradigm. Choose the case from the interrogative’s own function: nominative subject, genitive possessor, accusative direct object, or dative recipient or other governed complement.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-PAR-HWA -->

#### hwā / hwæt

<!-- table: NEW-U08-PAR-HWA -->

| Case | Common masculine/feminine singular | Neuter singular |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | hwā | hwæt |
| Genitive | hwæs | hwæs |
| Dative | hwǣm | hwǣm |
| Accusative | hwone | hwæt |

<!-- example: NEW-U08-EXAMPLE-02 -->

Hwā hæfð ðæt scip? Hwone siehð se mann?

Who has that ship? Whom does the man see?

Hwā is the subject of hæfð. Hwone is the object of siehð; se mann remains the nominative subject. English “whom” corresponds here to an accusative because “see” has an accusative object.

<!-- example: NEW-U08-EXAMPLE-03 -->

Hwǣm sęlst ðū ðone fisc? Hwæs scip siehst ðū?

To whom do you give that fish? Whose ship do you see?

Hwǣm is the dative recipient of the fish. Hwæs is genitive because it asks whose ship is seen; scip itself is the accusative object of siehst. The possessor and the possessed noun have different functions within that phrase.

**Hwǣm** is the selected dative in this unit. Cook also gives **hwām** as a valid alternative. The distinct instrumental forms below are reference forms; learning them does not introduce general instrumental syntax.

### How? Why?

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-VOC-hu -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-VOC-hwy -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-interrogative-adverbs -->

**Hū** asks about manner; **hwȳ** asks for a reason. Both are invariant question words in the following use. Lexical hwȳ “why?” can be used without constructing a general instrumental phrase.

#### Question adverbs

<!-- table: NEW-U08-MATRIX-QUESTION-ADVERBS -->

| Function | Form |
| --- | --- |
| How | hū |
| Why | hwȳ |

<!-- example: NEW-U08-EXAMPLE-04 -->

Hwæt hæfð se mann? Hū swimð hē? Hwȳ swimð hē?

What does the man have? How does he swim? Why does he swim?

Hwæt is the accusative thing possessed. Hū and hwȳ ask different questions about the same swimming: how it is done, and why it is done.

## 2. Interrogative adjectives

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-interrogative-adjectives -->

**Hwilc** means “which?” or “of what kind?”; **hwæðer**, “which of two?”; **hūlic**, “of what sort?”. They have strong adjective inflection and agree with their noun or referent in gender, number and case. No weak interrogative-adjective paradigm is needed here.

The strong endings are the familiar adjective endings: masculine and neuter singular genitive -es and dative -um, masculine accusative -ne, feminine genitive and dative -re; plural genitive -ra and dative -um. The tables give the complete forms, including the nominative and accusative distinctions. Feminine plural -a is selected consistently; the previously noted feminine plural alternative -e remains valid.

Hwæðer loses its unstressed e before the ordinary vowel endings: hwæðres, hwæðrum, hwæðre, hwæðra. It retains e before the consonantal endings in hwæðerne, hwæðerre and hwæðerra. Its feminine nominative singular and neuter nominative/accusative plural are hwæðer. Do not copy the ōðru cells of ōðer into this paradigm. Attested alternatives include hwæðeres and feminine hwæðere.

Hwæðer always distinguishes **two alternatives** in the use taught here. A singular noun can name one of two people or objects; plural morphology can distinguish two explicitly plural groups. It does not turn the choice into one among an unlimited number. The conjunction “whether” is a separate later use.

In the second question below, two men are being compared.

<!-- example: NEW-U08-EXAMPLE-05 -->

Hwilc mann hæfð ðæt scip? Hwæðer mann hæfð ðæt scip? Hūlic is ðæt scip?

Which man has that ship? Which of the two men has that ship? What sort of ship is that?

Hwilc and hwæðer modify nominative masculine singular mann. Hūlic is the strong neuter singular predicate referring to scip. The question word’s English meaning does not remove ordinary adjective agreement.

## 3. Some, any, each, and such

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-indefinites -->

The following words also take complete strong adjective inflection. **Sum** means “a certain” in the singular and “some” in the plural. **Ǣnig** means “any” in the ordinary direct questions used here. **Gehwilc** means “each, every”; its ordinary use in this unit is with a singular noun. **Swilc** means “such”, referring to a kind already understood from the context.

## 5. Relative clauses

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-VOC-the-relative -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-VOC-se -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-relative-the -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-relative-demonstrative -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-relative-internal-case -->

A **relative clause** identifies or describes an antecedent in another clause. Old English can introduce it with invariant **ðe**, with an inflected member of **se/sēo/ðæt** alone, or with that inflected member followed by **ðe**. English often uses “who”, “which” or “that”; an English personal relative does not require the Old English interrogative hwā.

#### Invariant relative particle

<!-- table: NEW-U08-MATRIX-RELATIVE-PARTICLE -->

| Function | Form |
| --- | --- |
| Who / which / that, with role determined inside the clause | ðe |

Ðe does not change its spelling for gender, number or case. Determine its role from the relative clause itself.

<!-- example: NEW-U08-EXAMPLE-11 -->

Se mann, ðe ðæt scip hæfð, is mīn gefēra.

The man who has that ship is my companion.

Ðe is the subject of hæfð; the ship is its accusative object. The antecedent se mann is the subject of the main clause is mīn gefēra.

<!-- example: NEW-U08-EXAMPLE-12 -->

Ic geseah ðæt scip, ðe se mann hæfð.

I saw the ship that the man has.

Here ðe is the object of hæfð, while se mann is the relative clause’s own nominative subject. The antecedent ðæt scip is the object of the main verb geseah. Invariant spelling does not erase these different functions.

The inflected relative reuses the complete demonstrative forms. Its **gender and number** normally follow the antecedent. In the ordinary pattern used for composition here, its **case** follows its function inside the relative clause. The antecedent has the case required by its separate function in the main clause.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-PAR-RELATIVE-SE -->

#### se / sēo / ðæt: relative forms, singular

<!-- table: NEW-U08-PAR-RELATIVE-SE-SG -->

| Case | Masculine | Neuter | Feminine |

<!-- example: NEW-U08-EXAMPLE-13 -->

Ic sęlle ðone fisc ðǣm męnn, se ðe mē healp.

I give the fish to the man who helped me.

Ðǣm męnn is dative, the recipient of sęlle. The relative se is nominative, the subject of healp. The person helped is dative mē. The antecedent’s dative does not make the relative subject dative.

<!-- example: NEW-U08-EXAMPLE-14 -->

Ic geseah ðone mann, ðǣm ðe ic healp.

I saw the man whom I helped.

Ðone mann is accusative after geseah. Within the relative, ic is subject and helpan governs dative ðǣm. The relative’s gender and number still refer to the masculine singular man.

<!-- example: NEW-U08-EXAMPLE-16 -->

Se mann, ðæs scip ic geseah, is gōd.

The man whose ship I saw is good.

Ðæs is genitive, expressing possession inside the relative: **whose ship**. Its antecedent mann is masculine singular and nominative in the main clause. Scip is neuter singular but accusative, the object of geseah; ic is the subject. The possessor’s antecedent determines the relative’s gender and number, not the possessed ship. A possessive relative therefore does not force its possessed noun into the genitive.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-relative-person-agreement -->

When a relative subject refers to a personal-pronoun antecedent, its verb can retain that antecedent’s person. In the selected second-person clauses, use the second-person verb form; the English word “who” does not automatically make the Old English verb third person.

<!-- example: NEW-U08-EXAMPLE-15 -->

Ðū, ðe mē hulpe, sęle mē ðone fisc.

You, who helped me, give me that fish.

Hulpe is second-person singular preterite, agreeing with the antecedent ðū. Sęle is the singular command addressed to that person. With a plural antecedent the relative verb and the command must both change where their forms require it.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-relative-case-attraction -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-relative-personal-reference -->

**Recognition notes.** An inflected relative sometimes takes its antecedent’s case instead of the case expected from its own role. This is **case attraction**, an attested exception; it does not replace the ordinary internal-case pattern required in this unit’s compositions. A less common strategy uses ðe together with a personal pronoun whose inflection marks the internal function. Recognize that strategy when explained in a text; independent composition with it comes later. Neither note changes the earlier limits on personal genitives or instrumental syntax.

Neuter **līf “life”** can name an individual life, with the ordinary plural “lives”. Its long stem has no ending in nominative or accusative plural. The full declension is repeated here for this use.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-PAR-LIF -->

#### līf: complete declension

<!-- table: NEW-U08-PAR-LIF -->

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | līf | līf |
| Genitive | līfes | līfa |
| Dative | līfe | līfum |
| Accusative | līf | līf |

**Libban** can take the related noun līf as a **cognate accusative**: “live one’s life”, “live our lives”. The object names the life being lived; it is not a locative phrase. ### Fill; have in one’s control

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-VOC-gefyllan -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-gefyllan-fill -->

**Gefyllan**, weak I, now also has the ordinary active sense **“fill”**, with the thing or place filled in the accusative. Keep this sense distinct from the earlier “finish, accomplish”, as in finishing a work. The already learned finite and imperative forms serve both senses; no new endings are required.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-VOC-on -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U08-GRAM-u08-control-on -->

**Habban + accusative controlled entity + on + dative possessive/gewealde** means “have in one’s control”. Geweald is neuter; its dative singular is **gewealde**. An agreeing possessive takes dative neuter singular: for one addressee, **ðīnum**. The invariant possessor **his** keeps its form. This is the established control expression, not a spatial translation of English “under”. The controlled persons or things remain the accusative object of habban.
