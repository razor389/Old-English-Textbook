# Unit 12. Tense, sequence, and voice

Old English has two inflectional tenses, present and preterite. Their interpretation depends on the situation. A present may refer to a future event; a preterite may narrate an event, describe a past state, or refer to something already completed at a past viewpoint. English may consequently need a simple, perfect, pluperfect or future expression where Old English has one finite word.

The twelve translated examples, exercises F, A, C, R and S, and “A house and a return” are new teaching material. Cook VI.19–21 retain all their original English prompts. The final historical reading is an exact connected extract, with separate English context, learner help and Cook’s original notes.

In tables, 1, 2 and 3 denote person. Nominative, genitive, dative and accusative are written out. Repeated spellings occupy distinct grammatical positions. **Reference** means recognize the stated form or construction; it is not required in independent composition. **Supplied help** remains available while answering the associated task. Preserve written vowel lengths and the distinction between e and ę.

## 1. Time in a narrative

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-narrative-time -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-preterite-anterior -->

A succession of preterites commonly advances the narrative. Temporal clauses, the meanings of the verbs and the surrounding account establish which event is earlier. A clause does not acquire a different inflection simply because English translates it with “had”.

<!-- example: NEW-U12-EXAMPLE01 -->

Se mann cōm, and hē geseah ðæt hūs.

The man came, and he saw the house.

Cōm and geseah are third singular preterite indicatives. In this account the arrival precedes the seeing.

Context: The house was finished before the man’s visit.

<!-- example: NEW-U12-EXAMPLE02 -->

Se mann geseah ðæt hūs ðe his sunu geworhte.

The man saw the house which his son had built.

Geworhte is also a simple preterite indicative. Here the stated context places the son’s building before the man’s seeing. English “had built” makes that relation clear without changing the Old English form.

Context: The visit is still expected.

<!-- example: NEW-U12-EXAMPLE12 -->

Hē cymð.

He will come.

Cymð is present indicative. The context makes the coming future. Do not add willan merely because the English translation contains “will”; willan can retain its meaning “wish; intend”.

## 2. Habban with a past participle

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-habban-perfect -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-VOC-HABBAN -->

With a transitive past participle and an accusative object, finite **habban** can present completed action and its result. The auxiliary agrees with the subject. The object is the person or thing affected by the participle. In the following construction the speaker has performed the building, and the completed house is the result.

<!-- example: NEW-U12-EXAMPLE03 -->

Nū hæbbe ic ðæt hūs geworht.

Now I have built the house.

Context: The building was already complete when the son arrived.

<!-- example: NEW-U12-EXAMPLE04 -->

Ðā se sunu cōm, hæfde se mann ðæt hūs geworht.

When the son came, the man had built the house.

Present hæbbe presents the completed work from the present viewpoint. Past hæfde places the completed work before the stated past arrival. The difference is in the finite auxiliary; geworht remains the selected participle base.

## 3. Active clauses, passives, and other uses of be

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-active-passive -->

An active transitive clause names an agent as subject and the affected entity as object. In an agentless passive the affected entity becomes nominative subject, and the active agent is no longer named. Use the already learned passive auxiliary with the selected past participle. Do not add a new expression for a passive agent.

<!-- example: NEW-U12-EXAMPLE05 -->

Se mann band ðæt hors. Ðæt hors wearð bunden.

The man bound the horse. The horse was bound.

The man is the active subject, and the horse is the active object. In the second clause the horse is the subject. Wearð often presents the event or entrance into a condition; the passive itself does not tell who performed the binding.

Context: The context presents the horse in its resulting bound state; wesan does not invariably exclude an event reading.

<!-- example: NEW-U12-EXAMPLE06 -->

Ðæt hors wæs bunden.

The horse was bound.

Here wæs presents the resulting condition. This is a contextual contrast: wesan can also occur in accounts of events, and weorðan need not always be translated literally “became”.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-wesan-intransitive-perfect -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-VOC-WESAN -->

For recognition, **wesan + gewiten**, the past participle of gewītan, can express a completed departure. The subject is the person who departs, not a patient acted on by an unnamed agent.

Context: In a past account, the man’s departure precedes the next event.

<!-- example: NEW-U12-EXAMPLE07 -->

Se mann wæs gewiten.

The man had departed.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-wesan-present-participle -->

Also recognize **wesan + sittende**, the present participle of sittan. The context can support a continuing activity or state. English may use a simple past or a progressive; the Old English form does not impose a modern English progressive category.

Context: The account describes a period during which the man was seated.

<!-- example: NEW-U12-EXAMPLE08 -->

Se mann wæs sittende.

The man sat / was sitting.

These two uses are for recognition in the assigned examples and reading. Do not construct an unrestricted intransitive perfect or progressive system from them.

## 4. Time in reported speech

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-reported-sequence -->

A reported statement has its own temporal viewpoint. Ask whether the reported event is simultaneous with the saying, already completed before it, or intended afterward. The earlier reporting subjunctive remains available. A subjunctive does not mean that the report is false, and a past reporting verb does not require mechanical replacement of every dependent tense.

Context: In each report, the presence is simultaneous with the saying.

<!-- example: NEW-U12-EXAMPLE09 -->

Hē sægð ðæt hē sīe hēr. Hē sægde ðæt hē wǣre hēr.

He says that he is here. He said that he was here.

Sīe is present subjunctive and wǣre preterite subjunctive. Each places the reported presence at the time of its own saying. The past report does not by itself say that the person is absent now.

Context: The building precedes the past saying.

<!-- example: NEW-U12-EXAMPLE10 -->

Hē sægde ðæt hē ðæt hūs geworht hæfde.

He said that he had built the house.

In this assigned report, hæfde is preterite subjunctive singular. It has the same spelling as first and third singular indicative hæfde. The reported-statement construction determines the mood; the spelling alone does not. Geworht with hæfde places the building before the past saying. The complete table in §2 remains labelled indicative.

Context: The speaker reports an intention; the sentence does not establish whether the arrival occurred.

<!-- example: NEW-U12-EXAMPLE11 -->

Hē sægde ðæt hē wolde cuman.

He said that he intended to come.

Wolde with a plain infinitive reports a wish or intention viewed from the past. The intended coming is later than that intention. The sentence does not assert that the coming took place. This is the use of willan required here, not a general future auxiliary.

### Objects and recipients

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-gierwan-accusative -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-writan-accusative -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-sendan-recipient -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-weorpan-accusative -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-gemetan-beneficiary -->

**Gierwan** takes the accusative thing prepared. The same thing remains accusative with habban + gierwed. **Wrītan** takes an accusative text or letter written. **Sęndan** takes the accusative person or thing sent and, if expressed, a dative recipient. **Weorpan** takes the accusative thing thrown, including a fishhook. ### Duration, extent, and possession

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-duration-accusative -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-extent-genitive -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-predicate-possession -->

An accusative quantity of time can express **duration**: how long an action or state lasts. This differs from identifying the day on which it happens. Higher numerals may govern a genitive noun; the phrase for forty years is supplied at Cook 19.2, so do not force its noun into an accusative shape.

A numeral with a **genitive measure noun** can state breadth or height. The noun fōt has genitive plural **fōta**. The number of feet measures the extent; the predicate adjective still agrees with the subject being described. The twenty-feet phrase is supplied at Cook 21.3.

Possession can also be predicative. A **genitive possessor** after be means “is someone’s”; the genitive is not a nominative noun predicate. An **agreeing possessive adjective** after be agrees with what is possessed. Thus the person/number of the possessor selects ðīn, while the gender and number of the possessed dæg or niht determine its grammatical position. The same surface can occupy more than one such position.

### Comparison

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-more-numerous -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-equative-swa-swa -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-swa-predicative-comparison -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-VOC-MA -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-VOC-SWA -->

With a plural subject, indeclinable **mā** can mean “more in number”. A comparison standard after ðonne uses the earlier parallel-subject construction. This quantitative predicate is distinct from the earlier use of mā for a greater amount of doing; it does not require a new comparative adjective māran.

For equality of degree, use **swā … adjective … swā**. The first swā marks the degree, “as”; the second introduces the standard, “as”. A positive predicate adjective retains strong agreement with the subject. The nominal standard can be nominative, understood as the subject of be. These are two separate words with an adjective between them, not the hyphenated source conjunction swā-swā.

A single **swā + noun phrase** can instead compare the persons sent with sheep: “as; like”. The comparing noun phrase refers to the persons who are the object of sending. This is the particular nominal comparison assigned here, distinct from the earlier comparison between finite clauses.

### Never and never again

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-negative-concord-bounded -->

In the assigned **nǣfre … ne** construction, nǣfre and the negative finite clause reinforce one negative meaning. Place ne before the finite verb. **Nǣfre mā** means “never more; never again”. Do not cancel the two negatives as though they made an affirmative. Some verbs have contracted negative forms; nis, nāh and the required nolde remain visibly supplied below. This introduces the stated expressions only, not an entire family of negative pronouns or negative paradigms.

**2. and hīe fela gēara iermdon and hīendon.** Iermdon and hīendon mean afflicted and oppressed. Fela gēara means for many years, with genitive after fela. Hīe can be the Britons as object with the raiders continuing as subject, or the raiders as subject with a Briton object understood; the events do not change. Do not infer a unique case from the spelling.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-NEW-U12-SRC-03 -->

<!-- support: U12-SRC-HELP-03 -->

**3. Ðā, on ðǣre unstilnesse ... bēne.** On ðǣre unstilnesse, amid that unrest. Onsęndon is the exact prefixed sent; the Britons are now the subject. Ǣrendwrecan, messengers; tō Rome, to Rome. Mid gewritum and wēpendre bēne means with letters and a tearful petition. Wēpendre is a present participle, strong dative feminine singular agreeing with bēne.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-NEW-U12-SRC-04 -->

<!-- support: U12-SRC-HELP-04 -->

**4. him fultumes bǣdon ... underðēodnesse.** In him fultumes bǣdon, him names the Romans asked and fultumes is the genitive thing requested: help. Hīernes is obedience; singāl is continual; underðēodnes is subjection. Ēaðmōde and singāle are accusative feminine singular. The promise is made by the Britons to the Romans.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-NEW-U12-SRC-05 -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-u12-source-reported-condition -->

<!-- support: U12-SRC-HELP-05 -->

**5. gif hīe him gefultumoden ... oferwinnan.** Gefultumoden is a preterite subjunctive: if they, the Romans, would help them, the Britons. The following ðæt clause gives the purpose, so that they might overcome their enemies. Meahten is the known preterite subjunctive plural of magan; oferwinnan means overcome. This whole reported condition/purpose sequence is supported locally.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-NEW-U12-SRC-06 -->

<!-- support: U12-SRC-HELP-06 -->

**6. Ðā onsęndon ... tō fultume.** Here the subject of onsęndon is the Romans, while him refers to the Britons. Tō fultume means as help/to their aid. The Old English says they sent an army in the active voice; Cook compares a passive Latin expression. Preserve the actual Old English voice when analyzing it.

**7. and, sōna ðæs-ðe ... cōmon, ðā.** Sōna ðæs-ðe means as soon as. On ðis īgland has the accusative goal, to/onto this island. Hīe refers to the Roman force. The following ðā resumes the narrative.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-NEW-U12-SRC-08 -->

**8. campedon ... āflīemdon.** Campedon, fought; wið hiera fēondum, against their enemies, with the source dative after wið. Him micel wæl ongeslōgon means inflicted great slaughter on them. Of hiera gemǣrum means out of their borders, with dative after of; the borders belong to the Britons, Rome’s allies. Āflīemdon means put to flight, reinforcing ādrifon. The enemy object carries on through the coordinated verbs.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-NEW-U12-SRC-09 -->

**9. lǣrdon ðæt ... fēondum.** Lǣrdon means advised/instructed. Fæsten is a fortification; worhten is the known unprefixed wyrcean preterite subjunctive, here make/build in the advice clause. Him tō gebeorge means for their own protection. The Romans give the advice and the Britons are to build. The source’s wið + dative remains locally supplied.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-NEW-U12-SRC-10 -->

**10. and swā, mid micle sige, hām fōron.** Sige means victory. Mid micle sige is with great victory: the adjective has the instrumental micle beside the dative noun, as Cook’s note directs. Treat this as a supplied source construction. Hām is the directional home, without a preposition.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-NEW-U12-SRC-11 -->

<!-- support: U12-SRC-HELP-11 -->

**11. Ðā ðæt ðā ongēaton ðā ǣrran gewinnan, ðæt.** Read the three ðā forms in order as when, then, the. The first ðæt is an anticipatory object, this/that, expanded by the later that-clause. Ongēaton means realized; ǣrran gewinnan means former enemies. Gewinnan is a weak masculine plural noun, distinct from the previously supplied neuter gewinn, trouble.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-NEW-U12-SRC-12 -->

**12. se Rōmanisca hęre wæs onweg gewiten.** Rōmanisca is the weak masculine adjective Roman. Wæs onweg gewiten means had gone away: gewiten is the past participle of intransitive gewītan. This is an intransitive completed-state/perfect expression, not a passive. The source has plain-e onweg; retain it beside the earlier selected onwęg.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-NEW-U12-SRC-14 -->

**14. slōgon ealle and cwealdon ðæt hīe gemētton.** Cwealdon means killed. Slōgon and cwealdon reinforce the attack. Ealle is all, naturally people in context; the following neuter ðæt is a headless/collective all that they encountered. A translation all they encountered need not pretend that ðæt is an overt masculine personal relative. Cook’s Latin note reads cædunt.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U12-GRAM-NEW-U12-SRC-15 -->

**15. swā-swā rīpe ierðe fortrǣdon and fornōmon.** Swā-swā means just as/like. Rīpe ierðe means ripe crops, a collective feminine accusative phrase. Fortrǣdon, trampled down; fornōmon, destroyed/laid waste. The Britons are treated as a crop that is trampled; the object continues from the preceding clauses.
