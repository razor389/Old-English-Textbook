# Unit 12. Answer key

The key covers all 76 tasks. Cook's three complete sets retain their original prompts and numbering; the answers, translations and explanations are new teaching material. The twelve translated teaching examples are not additional exercises. Equivalent English wording, defensible Old English word order and documented inflectional alternatives are acceptable. A grammatical answer can nevertheless miss a stated target, such as a particular auxiliary, mood or selected paradigm. [1]

In form tasks, supply every labeled position, including positions with identical spellings. In translation and analysis, distinguish morphological tense from the time relation established by context. An English perfect, pluperfect or future does not mechanically require a corresponding Old English auxiliary. In reports, identify the content's time relative to the reporting situation; a subjunctive does not by itself make the report false. [2]

The productive **habban** construction below has an active subject, an accusative object and a selected past participle. Its finite auxiliary agrees with the subject. The models use bare participles; source usage also permits participles inflected in agreement with the object. A correctly evidenced agreeing alternative is not wrong merely because it differs from the model, but participial agreement is not agreement in person with the finite subject. The twelve indicative construction positions do not constitute twelve new inflected words or exhaust the construction's possible moods. [3]

The **wesan + gewiten** and **wesan + sittende** tasks require recognition, not independent composition. Local Cook and reading helps remain available during the tasks they support. Supplied expressions are not tests of unaided recall. An agentless passive does not name the agent; do not restore one merely from a nearby model.

## F. Complete forms and constructions

**F01. Fōt.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-F01 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-F01 -->

| Case | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | fōt | fēt |
| Genitive | fōtes | fōta |
| Dative | fēt | fōtum |
| Accusative | fōt | fēt |

**Explanation:** The selected root-noun system has **fēt** in the dative singular and nominative/accusative plural. Genitive singular and genitive/dative plural retain **fōt-**. Thus neither vowel can be extended through the whole paradigm. The documented dative singular **fōte** and occasional nominative/accusative plural **fōtas** are valid alternatives in those cells; the displayed forms give the requested selected table. A genitive plural made from **fēt-** would miss the assigned alternation. [4]

**F02. Brycg and hęre.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-F02 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-F02 -->

| Case | Brycg singular | Brycg plural | Hęre singular | Hęre plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | brycg | brycga | hęre | hęras |

| Dative | brycge | brycgum | hęre | hęrum |

**Explanation:** Feminine **brycg** retains **brycg-** before its endings, including accusative singular **-e**. Do not add these endings to the whole nominative as though its final **e** were retained everywhere. A masculine **-as** plural for **brycg** would import the wrong system. [4]

**Explanation:** Contracted **flēon** has the selected present contrast **flēo-/flīeh-** and strong past **flēah** beside **flug-**. Documented variants remain valid in their proper cells; a strong past invented for **grētan** does not complete its selected weak system. [5]

**F04. Diagnostic indicative forms.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-F04 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-F04 -->

| Verb | Present second singular | Present third singular | Preterite second singular | Preterite third plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| gemētan | gemētst | gemētt | gemēttest | gemētton |
| ādrīfan | ādrīfst | ādrīfð | ādrife | ādrifon |
| geopenian | geopenast | geopenað | geopenodest | geopenodon |

**Explanation:** Weak **gemētan** has present **gemētt** and preterite stem **gemētt-**, with **-e** in first and third singular, **-est** in second singular, and **-on** in all plural persons. Strong **ādrīfan** has long **ī** in the present but short **i** in the second-singular and plural past stem. Weak-II **geopenian** uses present **geopena-** in these singular cells and preterite **geopenod-**. In selected **cȳst/cȳð**, the dental consonants do not remain as a mechanically concatenated stem and ending; the past retains **cȳðd-**. **Cȳðst** follows the documented analogical retained-dental pattern and is a valid alternative, although **cȳst** matches the selected table. Do not transfer **ādrāf** into its second-singular past or give **geopenian** a weak-I past. [5]

**F06. Habban with object and past participle.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-F06 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-F06 -->

**Present indicative construction.**

| Person and number | Old English | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 sg. | Ic hæbbe ðæt hūs geworht. | I have built the house. |
| 2 sg. | Ðū hæfst ðæt hūs geworht. | You have built the house. |
| 3 sg. | Hē hæfð ðæt hūs geworht. | He has built the house. |
| 1 pl. | Wē habbað ðæt hūs geworht. | We have built the house. |
| 2 pl. | Gē habbað ðæt hūs geworht. | You have built the house. |
| 3 pl. | Hīe habbað ðæt hūs geworht. | They have built the house. |

**Preterite indicative construction.**

| Person and number | Old English | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 sg. | Ic hæfde ðæt hūs geworht. | I had built the house. |
| 2 sg. | Ðū hæfdest ðæt hūs geworht. | You had built the house. |
| 3 sg. | Hē hæfde ðæt hūs geworht. | He had built the house. |
| 1 pl. | Wē hæfdon ðæt hūs geworht. | We had built the house. |

| 3 pl. | Hīe hæfdon ðæt hūs geworht. | They had built the house. |

**Explanation:** The finite auxiliaries are **hæbbe, hæfst, hæfð, habbað** and **hæfde, hæfdest, hæfdon**, assigned to the labeled persons and numbers. **Ðæt hūs** remains the accusative object; **geworht** remains the selected past participle. The present construction presents completed building from a present viewpoint; the past construction places it before a past viewpoint. These are twelve construction positions, not twelve additional synthetic forms. Other grammatical moods or different objects do not supply the requested positions. The unchanged base does not mean that every attested participle must be uninflected: valid object agreement is distinguished from the finite auxiliary's subject agreement. [3]

**F07. Present and past participle bases.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-F07 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-F07 -->

| Verb | Present participle | Past participle |
|---|---|---|
| gierwan | gierwende | gierwed |
| sęttan | sęttende | sętt |
| gewyrcean | gewyrcende | geworht |

**Explanation:** The **past** bases **gierwed, sętt, geworht** enter the selected completed-action construction with **habban**. The present bases in **-ende** do not replace them there. Each pair is nonfinite; it supplies no person ending and does not itself assign a complete participial adjective declension. Preserve the lexical stem change in **gewyrcende/geworht** and the contracted **sętt**. The documented participle variants may be accepted with their correct analysis; a finite preterite in the participle position would change the construction. [5]

## A. Tense, sequence and voice

**A01. Successive events.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-A01 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-A01 -->

**Se mann cōm, and hē geseah ðæt hūs.**

The man came, and he saw the house.

Arrival precedes seeing in this ordinary narrative sequence. **Cōm** and **geseah** are third-singular preterite indicatives. Equivalent English past narration is acceptable; neither verb is an infinitive, and the coordination does not state that one event caused the other.

**A02. An earlier event in a simple preterite.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-A02 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-A02 -->

**Context:** The house was finished before the visit.

**Se mann geseah ðæt hūs ðe his sunu geworhte.**

The man saw the house which his son had built.

Both **geseah** and **geworhte** are morphologically preterite. The context makes the son's building earlier than the man's seeing; English **had built** makes that relation explicit. **His sunu** is the builder, and relative **ðe** represents the house as object of **geworhte**. **Which his son built** is also possible English if the supplied sequence remains clear. Do not call **geworhte** a morphological pluperfect or place the building after the visit. [2]

**A03. Present and past viewpoints.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-A03 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-A03 -->

**Context:** The speaker describes completed building work that they performed.

**Ic hæbbe ðæt hūs geworht.** — I have built the house.

**Ic hæfde ðæt hūs geworht.** — I had built the house.

**Ic** is the subject and builder; **ðæt hūs** is the accusative object. **Hæbbe/hæfde** is the finite auxiliary; **geworht** is the past participle. The first clause presents completed building from the present; the second places it before a past viewpoint. The participle stays nonfinite in both. Natural contextual English alternatives are acceptable if they preserve the given speaker's agency and the different viewpoints. Neither sentence makes the house the grammatical subject or leaves the builder unnamed. [3]

**A04. Possession and completed action.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-A04 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-A04 -->

**Ic hæbbe ðæt hūs.** — I have the house.

**Context for the second sentence:** The speaker has just finished building the house.

**Ic hæbbe ðæt hūs geworht.** — I have built the house.

In the first sentence **habban** expresses possession. In the second it combines with **geworht** to present the speaker's completed building and its present result. **Ðæt hūs** remains accusative in both. Possession alone does not assert that the possessor built the house, and **hæbbe** alone does not mean **build**. A resultative English paraphrase is defensible if it preserves the instructed builder and completion; an unsupported causative interpretation would change the supplied situation.

**A05. Active and passive roles.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-A05 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-A05 -->

| Old English | Translation |
|---|---|
| Se mann band ðæt hors. | The man bound the horse. |
| Ðæt hors wearð bunden. | The horse was bound. |
| Ðæt hors wæs bunden. | The horse was bound. |

In the active clause **se mann** is the nominative subject and **ðæt hors** the accusative object. In both passives **ðæt hors** is nominative subject. The horse is the affected participant throughout; only the active sentence names the binder. Its neuter nominative and accusative spellings happen to coincide.

**Wearð** commonly foregrounds the binding event; **wæs** commonly presents the resulting state. **Became bound** and **was in a bound state** can make those tendencies explicit, but context also permits event readings with **wesan**. The forms alone do not impose two different English tenses. Do not invent an agent in either passive or keep the active agent as the passive subject. [2]

**A06. An intransitive past participle.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-A06 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-A06 -->

**Se mann wæs gewiten.**

The man had departed; or, the man was gone.

**Wæs** is finite third-singular preterite **wesan**; **gewiten** is the past participle of intransitive **gewītan**. The man himself has departed. He is not the object of an unnamed agent's transitive action. **Had departed** makes anteriority explicit; **was gone** emphasizes the resulting absence. This recognition example does not require an invented passive agent or establish a general rule for all English **be + participle** expressions. [2]

**A07. A present participle in a past setting.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-A07 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-A07 -->

**Se mann wæs sittende.**

The man sat; or, the man was sitting.

**Wæs** is the finite third-singular preterite; **sittende** is a present participle. An ongoing situation can make **was sitting** idiomatic English, but the Old English expression does not mechanically require the modern English progressive. **Sat** is a valid simple-past rendering. The name **present participle** identifies the nonfinite form, not the time of the whole sentence; **sittende** is neither a finite present nor a past participle. [2]

**A08. Presence simultaneous with a report.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-A08 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-A08 -->

**Context:** In each sentence the presence is simultaneous with the saying.

**Hē sægð ðæt hē sīe hēr.** — He says that he is here.

**Hē sægde ðæt hē wǣre hēr.** — He said that he was here.

**Sægð** is present indicative; **sægde** is preterite indicative in the main narrative clause. Dependent **sīe** is present subjunctive and **wǣre** preterite subjunctive. The first presence is related to the present saying; the second to the past saying. The second sentence does not, in this context, mean that the presence ended before he spoke. The report's subjunctive does not assert absence or falsity. Equivalent indirect English reports are valid; do not infer **had been** merely from preterite **wǣre**. [2]

**A09. Building anterior to a report.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-A09 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-A09 -->

**Context:** The building precedes the saying.

**Hē sægde ðæt hē ðæt hūs geworht hæfde.**

He said that he had built the house.

Main-clause **sægde** is the finite preterite indicative. Dependent **hæfde** is the finite preterite subjunctive in the assigned reported-statement analysis; **geworht** is the past participle. **Hē** is the reported builder and **ðæt hūs** the accusative object. The auxiliary–participle construction presents completed building before the past report. **Hæfde** has the same spelling as indicative forms, so its spelling alone does not identify mood. A grammatical alternative order retains the same roles and relation; **geworht** must not be analyzed as another finite verb. [2–3]

**A10. Reported intention.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-A10 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-A10 -->

**Hē sægde ðæt hē wolde cuman.**

He said that he intended to come; or, that he wished/wanted to come.

The intended action is the coming, expressed by infinitive **cuman** with **wolde**. It is prospective from the past saying. The sentence reports the intention without asserting an actual arrival. **Would come** can convey that intention in suitable English context, but does not justify an automatic future-tense analysis. **He came** would assert an event which this sentence leaves open. [2]

**A11. Expected arrival and desire.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-A11 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-A11 -->

**Context for the first sentence:** The visit is still expected.

**Hē cymð.** — He will come; or, he is coming.

**Hē wille cuman.** — He wishes/intends to come.

In the first, **cymð** is morphologically present indicative, with future reference supplied by the situation. In the second, **wille** contributes desire or intention and **cuman** names the intended action. **Wants to come** is also apt. English **will** therefore need not be translated with **willan**; conversely, the stated volition in the second sentence should not disappear into a purely mechanical tense label.

## C. Controlled composition

**C01. We have built the house.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-C01 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-C01 -->

**Wē habbað ðæt hūs geworht.**

**Habbað** agrees with first-plural **wē**. The house remains the accusative object and **geworht** the selected past participle. Other grammatical positions of the object and participle preserve the target. Singular **hæbbe** would not agree with **wē**; a simple preterite might be appropriate in another context but would omit the requested present **habban** construction.

**C02. They had built the house.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-C02 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-C02 -->

**Hīe hæfdon ðæt hūs geworht.**

The preterite plural auxiliary **hæfdon** puts the completed building before the supplied past viewpoint. Other grammatical word orders are acceptable. A simple preterite can express an earlier event in context, as A02 shows, but does not meet this task's explicit compound-construction target. Present **habbað** would change the requested viewpoint.

**C03. The men and the horse.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-C03 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-C03 -->

**Ðā męnn bundon ðæt hors.** — The men bound the horse.

**Ðæt hors wearð bunden.** — The horse was bound.

The plural active subject requires **bundon**. The passive makes singular **hors** the subject and therefore has singular **wearð**. It leaves the binder unexpressed, so the information that men performed the action is no longer stated. A passive with **wæs** can be grammatical but misses the requested **weorðan** target. Plural **wurdon** would wrongly retain agreement with the former active subject.

**C04. The monk's reported presence.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-C04 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-C04 -->

**Se munuc sægð ðæt hē sīe hēr.**

**Se munuc sægde ðæt hē wǣre hēr.**

The monk's presence is simultaneous with the saying in each version. **Sīe/wǣre** supplies the requested report subjunctive. An indicative report may be valid Old English in an appropriate presentation, but it does not satisfy that stated choice. Other documented copular subjunctive forms or grammatical word orders are acceptable with the same temporal relation. Do not change simultaneous presence to an earlier **had been** or make **hē** refer to a different person without context.

**C05. Building before the report.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-C05 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-C05 -->

**Hē sægde ðæt hē ðæt hūs geworht hæfde.**

The dependent finite form is preterite subjunctive **hæfde**; the past participle is **geworht**. The completed action precedes the saying. **Hē sægde ðæt hē hæfde ðæt hūs geworht** is an acceptable alternative order. The identical indicative spelling of **hæfde** does not remove the task's specified mood analysis. A passive with the house as subject would change the participant structure. [2–3]

**C06. An intention to come.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-C06 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-C06 -->

**Hē sægde ðæt hē wolde cuman.**

**Wolde cuman** expresses the intention from the past reporting situation; the infinitive is not an assertion that the coming occurred. Defensible alternative word order is acceptable. Replacing the dependent expression with an asserted past arrival would lose the intended meaning, while translating **wolde** as **intended**, **wished** or **wanted** preserves it.

**C07. Preparing food.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-C07 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-C07 -->

**Giere ðone męte.** — Prepare the food.

**Ic hæbbe ðone męte gierwed.** — I have prepared the food.

**Giere** addresses one person. The food is the accusative object in both clauses. In the reply **hæbbe** is finite and **gierwed** the past participle. Documented forms of **gierwan**, correctly analyzed object-agreeing participles and grammatical word-order alternatives can be accepted. Plural **gierwað** does not meet the singular address; a finite preterite substituted for **gierwed** would not complete the compound construction.

**C08. The completed bridge and a new arrival.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-C08 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-C08 -->

**Sēo brycg wæs geworht. Ðā cōm mann.**

The bridge is the feminine subject of the first clause; **wæs geworht** presents its completed state without naming a builder. The second clause introduces a man with finite preterite **cōm**. Indefinite English **a** does not require cardinal **ān**. Other grammatical word orders are possible. A suitable passive with **weorðan** can be valid language but misses the specified **wesan** here; a masculine or neuter article with **brycg** would violate agreement.

## Cook, Group VI, Exercise 19

**19.1.** At length the king ceased to bear rule.

<!-- item: EX-VI-19-01 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-VI-19-01 -->

**Æt nīehstan se cyning geswāc rīces.**

**Geswāc** is the simple preterite of **geswīcan**; **rīces** is the genitive complement, rule or dominion. The supplied **æt nīehstan** gives **at length**. The construction conveys the king's cessation of rule without a separate infinitive meaning **bear**. Equivalent supported wording is possible, but an invented infinitival frame would not follow the taught government.

**19.2.** He had the kingdom forty years.

<!-- item: EX-VI-19-02 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-VI-19-02 -->

**Hē hæfde ðæt rīce fēowertig gēara.**

Here **hæfde** means possessed or held: it is lexical **habban**, not a perfect auxiliary. **Ðæt rīce** is its accusative object. The supplied **fēowertig gēara** expresses the forty-year duration; **gēara** is the genitive plural used with the numeral. Do not add a participle merely because English has **had**, or turn the kingdom into the subject. **He held the kingdom for forty years** gives the same meaning.

**19.3.** When were the bridges built?

<!-- item: EX-VI-19-03 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-VI-19-03 -->

**Hwonne wurdon ðā brycga geworht?**

**Hwonne** asks when; **ðā brycga** is the plural subject and **wurdon** the plural preterite auxiliary. The model foregrounds the building event. **Wǣron … geworht** can also be defensible when context supports that passive presentation; **wesan** is not restricted to states in every occurrence. The selected bare participle is legitimate with a plural subject. Singular **wearð** would fail to agree, and an active answer with a newly invented builder would add information.

**19.4.** To-day thou shalt be with me.

<!-- item: EX-VI-19-04 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-VI-19-04 -->

**Tō-dæg ðū bist mid mē.**

The present form **bist** has future reference in this promise; **mid mē** names the accompanying person in the dative. The sentence does not need **willan** simply because the English has **shalt**. **Today you will be with me** is the intended meaning. A translation introducing personal desire or an obligation to be present would add a different modal meaning.

**19.5.** What (om. did) the Romans build (pret.) in Britain?

<!-- item: EX-VI-19-05 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-VI-19-05 -->

**Hwæt geworhton Rōmane on Breotone?**

**Hwæt** is the thing built, and **Rōmane** the supplied plural subject. **Geworhton** is the explicitly requested preterite; no separate word translates auxiliary English **did**. The local **on Breotone** supplies the location. A grammatical order with the subject earlier is acceptable. Do not put the question word into the subject role or use a present verb against Cook's **(pret.)** control.

**19.6.** I send my angel before (beforan, 166) thy face.

<!-- item: EX-VI-19-06 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-VI-19-06 -->

**Ic sęnde mīnne ęngel beforan ðīnum andwlitan.**

**Mīnne ęngel** is the masculine accusative person sent. **Beforan** takes the taught dative phrase **ðīnum andwlitan** here, with the possessive agreeing with **andwlitan**. The literal reference to the addressee's face is retained. Grammatical word-order alternatives are acceptable; using an accusative after this selected **beforan** frame, or making the angel dative without a new construction, would change the required relations.

**19.7.** Come (sing.), and bring those letters that you wrote.

<!-- item: EX-VI-19-07 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-VI-19-07 -->

**Cum, and bring ðā gewritu ðe ðū write.**

**Cum** and **bring** address one person, as Cook expressly requires for **come**. **Ðā gewritu** is the plural object, and **ðe** represents it as object of **write**. The unit's directions also select singular **ðū** in the relative clause: preterite second-singular **write** has short **i**, unlike present first-singular **wrīte**. Cook's unnumbered relative **you** could instead yield grammatical **ðe gē writon** under a plural interpretation, but that does not meet this unit's selected singular target. Do not change the first command to plural or silently read **write** as a present.

**19.8.** I promise you obedience, if you help (196. d) me.

<!-- item: EX-VI-19-08 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-VI-19-08 -->

**Ic gehāte ēow gehīersumnesse, gif gē mē helpen.**

The model takes both occurrences of **you** as plural: **ēow** is the dative promisee, **gehīersumnesse** the promised thing, and **gē … helpen** the conditional clause. The reference **196. d** selects the subjunctive **helpen**; **mē** is the dative person helped. Singular **ðē/ðū … helpe** is also possible if the addressee is consistently singular. An indicative can occur in other conditional presentations, but does not satisfy this source control. Do not give the recipient the case of the promised thing.

**19.9.** Send me (om. a) large army.

<!-- item: EX-VI-19-09 -->
<!-- key: KEY-EX-VI-19-09 -->

**Sęnd mē micelne hęre.**

## R. A house and a return

**R01. Complete translation.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-R01 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-R01 -->

The man had built one house. Then his son came, and the son saw the house. The father said, “Now I have built the house.” The son said that he intended to travel. When the son departed, the father sat in the house. Then the son came, and he gave his father food.

**Interpretation:** The opening **hæfde … geworht** places the completed building before the son's first arrival. The direct **ic** belongs to the father; the next reported intention belongs to the son. **Intended/wanted to go** is also a defensible rendering of **wolde faran**. The following clauses separately narrate departure and return, so fulfillment is established by those events rather than by **wolde** alone.

**One house** preserves the assigned cardinal **ān**; the account has not introduced an indefinite article. Contextual English **was sitting** can render the father's **sæt**, although the Old English word is a simple finite preterite. Preserve the son's return and his gift of food to his father. Making the father the giver to himself, or treating the last arrival as the first one, would break the connected reference and sequence. [8]

**R02. The order of events.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-R02 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-R02 -->

1. Building the house.
2. The son's first arrival.
3. The father's announcement.
4. The son's departure.
5. The son's return with food.

The phrase **hæfde … geworht** puts the building before the story's first arrival. Between the announcement and departure the son expresses his intention to travel. A prose description of the same order is equivalent. Do not put building after the announcement merely because the father speaks about it then, or merge the two arrivals.

**R03. Speakers, intentions and events.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-R03 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-R03 -->

**Ic** in the direct quotation is the father. **Hē** in **ðæt hē wolde faran** is the son whose intention is being reported. **Hē** in the last sentence is the returning son, who gives food to his father.

The journey is initially an intention; **se sunu gewāt** then explicitly narrates the departure. The subsequent **cōm se sunu** supplies the return. Thus the narrative shows action beyond the statement of intention, although it gives no detailed account of the intervening travel. Masculine pronoun morphology alone cannot distinguish father from son: the event chain and local subjects identify the referents. Do not infer completed travel solely from **wolde** or make every **hē** refer to the same man regardless of context.

**R04. The house as passive subject.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-R04 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-R04 -->

In **Nū hæbbe ic ðæt hūs geworht**, **ic** is the nominative subject and builder; **ðæt hūs** is the accusative object.

**Ðæt hūs is geworht.** — The house is built.

The new sentence makes **ðæt hūs** nominative subject and leaves the builder unexpressed. Its completed state is conveyed with present **is** and the same past-participle base. **Geworht is ðæt hūs** is a defensible alternative order. The article and noun have identical nominative and accusative spellings, but their grammatical role has changed. Keeping **ic** as the subject of this agentless passive would not perform the requested transformation.

## S. The Picts and Scots

**S01. Complete translation.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-S01 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-S01 -->

Then two peoples, the Picts from the north and the Scots from the west, began to attack the Britons, take their possessions and plunder; and they afflicted and oppressed them for many years. Then, amid that unrest, the Britons sent messengers to Rome with letters and a tearful petition. They asked the Romans for help and promised them humble obedience and constant submission if the Romans helped them so that they could overcome their enemies. Then the Romans sent them a large army for support; and as soon as the soldiers came onto this island, they fought against their enemies, inflicted great slaughter on them, and drove them out of the Britons' borders and put them to flight. They instructed the Britons to build a fortification for their own protection against their enemies; and so the Romans went home with great victory.

When the former adversaries then realized this, that the Roman army had gone away, they soon came with a fleet into the Britons' territory, struck down and killed all they came across, trampled and destroyed them like ripe crops, and utterly reduced them to misery.

**Interpretation:** The English names some participants where the Old English uses pronouns: the initial raiders are the Picts and Scots; the petitioners are the Britons; the senders and relieving soldiers are Roman; the final attackers are the former adversaries. Retaining English pronouns is equally acceptable if those relations stay clear. The full sequence includes both the successful relief and the renewed attack after withdrawal.

In opening **and hīe fela gēara iermdon and hīendon**, **hīe** can be accusative, the Britons, with the raiders continuing as subject, or nominative, the raiders, with the Britons understood as object. The surface does not require a unique case analysis; either preserves the same oppression. **Hiera gemǣrum** in the relief describes the Britons' borders, as Cook's note explains.

**Had gone away** makes the Roman departure earlier than the enemies' realization; **was gone** can express the same resulting absence. The phrase is intransitive, not passive. **All they came across** allows the breadth of the neuter headless **ðæt** clause; **all whom they encountered** can also make the human victims explicit in context without turning **ðæt** into an overt masculine relative. **Ripe crops**, **ripe corn**, or a suitably understood **ripe field** can convey the comparison. It describes the Britons' devastation, not an unrelated literal harvest.

Closely related words for aid, oppression, submission, fortification and borders are acceptable. Keep both the instruction and its prospective building; do not add an assertion that the fortification was completed. Keep the final **mid ealle**, completely or utterly, and the last reduction to misery. The nineteenth-century ethnological speculation in the original apparatus is archival commentary, not part of this translation or a historical conclusion being tested. [7]

**S02. Raid, relief and renewed attack.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-S02 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-S02 -->

1. First raids.
2. Appeal to Rome.
3. Roman relief.
4. Roman withdrawal.
5. Renewed attack.

The former adversaries discover that the Roman army has gone away; they then return by fleet. The narrator thus presents the loss of Roman protection as the opportunity for the renewed attack. Equivalent wording or a labeled timeline is acceptable. The withdrawal precedes its discovery, and the first relief genuinely occurs within the narrative; neither should be omitted or placed after the renewed attack.

**S03. Three occurrences of ðā.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-S03 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-S03 -->

**Ðā ðæt ðā ongēaton ðā ǣrran gewinnan** can be unpacked as **When the former adversaries then realized this**.

| Form in order | Function |
|---|---|
| First **Ðā** | Temporal conjunction, when. |
| Second **ðā** | Temporal adverb, then, within that clause. |
| Third **ðā** | Nominative plural article with **ǣrran gewinnan**, the former adversaries. |

The first **ðæt** is an anticipatory accusative object of **ongēaton**, this or that. Its content is expanded by the following **ðæt se Rōmanisca hęre …** clause. The later main clause begins **ðā cōmon hīe**, then they came. English may omit an unnecessary **then** while preserving the structure. Do not classify all three **ðā** forms as articles, or confuse the anticipatory object with the following content-clause conjunction. Cook's original note supplies the when–then–the distinction. [7]

**S04. Changing subjects.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-S04 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-S04 -->

The **Britons** send messengers to Rome. The **Romans** send the army to the Britons. The **Roman soldiers** arrive and fight the enemies.

The first act is the appeal of the afflicted people. The second is Rome's response. In the arrival and fighting clauses the Roman soldiers supply the contextual subject; Cook's note calls them the legionaries, apparently. His next note identifies the borders from which the enemies are driven as the Britons' borders. Repeated **hīe** does not by itself name a people or guarantee that one subject persists through every clause. **The Romans** or **the Roman authorities** is sufficient for the sending: no particular ruler has to be invented. [7]

**S05. Wæs onweg gewiten.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-S05 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-S05 -->

**Wæs** is third-singular preterite **wesan**, agreeing with **se Rōmanisca hęre**. **Onweg** means away. **Gewiten** is the past participle of intransitive **gewītan**.

The army's departure precedes the former enemies' realization that it has left, and their renewed attack follows that realization. **Had gone away** or **was gone** preserves the relation. The army itself has departed; it is not an object on which an unnamed agent has performed a transitive action. Do not analyze the phrase as an agentless passive merely because it contains **wesan** and a past participle, or place the departure after the renewed invasion.

**S06. Active Old English and an English passive.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-S06 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-S06 -->

In **Ðā onsęndon hīe him micelne hęre**, **hīe**, the Romans, is nominative subject; **him**, the Britons, is the dative recipient; and **micelne hęre** is the accusative army sent.

**Then a large army was sent to the Britons by the Romans for their support.**

This English passive makes the army the grammatical subject and retains the recipient and the contextually identified agent. **A large army was then sent to them to help them** is also apt when the Roman sender is clear from context. Cook compares an active Old English clause with a passive Latin source; **onsęndon** itself remains active. No Latin analysis or new Old English agent phrase is required. Making the Britons the army being sent would confuse recipient and object. [7]

**S07. A prospective instruction.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-S07 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-S07 -->

The Romans recommend that the Britons build a fortification for their own protection against their enemies. The building is **prospective** from the instruction, and this clause alone does not state that the work was completed.

**Lǣrdon** reports the instruction in the past. Dependent **worhten** is preterite subjunctive in that reporting setting; its morphology does not make the recommended building earlier than the advice. **Instructed/advised them to build** conveys the same relation. Translating it as **they had already built** merely because the dependent form is preterite would reverse the task's temporal meaning. Cook's note about construction in an omitted Latin passage does not turn this Old English advice clause into a narrated completion. [7]

**S08. The crop comparison.**

<!-- item: NEW-U12-S08 -->
<!-- key: KEY-NEW-U12-S08 -->

The victims are the Britons encountered by the returning attackers. **Rīpe ierðe**, ripe crops, is the supplied collective phrase used in comparing their treatment to a crop trampled down and destroyed. The image intensifies the narrative's account of devastation and vulnerability after the Roman departure.

**Ripe corn** or an appropriately understood **ripe field** can also convey the image. A broader rendering of the preceding **all they came across** remains possible; the grammatical neuter **ðæt** must not be converted into a uniquely personal relative merely to match an English model. The comparison does not begin a separate literal farming episode or give the noun for crops a newly taught ordinary meaning of people. Making the Romans the victims would disregard their departure and the changed actors. [7]
