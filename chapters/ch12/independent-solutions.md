## Analysis

### NEW-U12-A01

“The man came, and he saw the house.” The arrival is first; the seeing follows it in this narration. Cōm and geseah are the finite verbs, both third singular preterite indicative. Se mann is the subject of cōm; hē refers to that man and is the subject of geseah. Ðæt hūs is the accusative thing seen.

### NEW-U12-A02

“The man saw the house which his son had built.” Geworhte is third singular **preterite indicative**, not a separately inflected pluperfect. The stated context establishes that the building preceded the visit and seeing; English “had built” makes that anteriority explicit. The relative ðe represents the house as the object of the son's building.

### NEW-U12-A03

“I have built the house.” “I had built the house.” The builder is the speaker, ic, by the explicit prompt context. Ðæt hūs is the accusative object in both. Hæbbe is present indicative first singular; hæfde is preterite indicative first singular in this comparison. Geworht is the past participle of gewyrcean. The first presents the completed building from the present viewpoint; the second presents it as completed before a past viewpoint. Changing the finite auxiliary does not change the object's case.

### NEW-U12-A04

“I have the house.” “I have built the house.” In the first, hæbbe expresses ordinary possession of the accusative house. In the second, the supplied just-finished context and geworht give completed building with its result; hæbbe functions as the auxiliary of that construction. Geworht contributes the building event, which is absent from the bare possession sentence. The identical finite form does not make the two senses identical.

### NEW-U12-A05

1. “The man bound the horse.” Se mann is the nominative subject and named binder; ðæt hors is the accusative object, the affected horse.
2. “The horse was bound.” Ðæt hors is now nominative subject of wearð bunden. The binder is not named. Wearð ordinarily directs attention to the binding event or entrance into the bound condition in this contrast.
3. “The horse was bound.” Ðæt hors is again nominative subject. Wæs bunden can describe the resulting condition; no binder is named.

Only the first sentence identifies the binder as the man. The last two English renderings can coincide. The event/state distinction requires context; wesan is not categorically excluded from event accounts, and wearð need not always be translated “became”. The article and neuter noun have the same surface in nominative and accusative, so their syntactic roles cannot be decided from that surface alone.

### NEW-U12-A06

“The man had departed.” Wæs is finite third singular preterite indicative of wesan. Gewiten is the past participle of intransitive gewītan; the phrase presents completed departure from a past viewpoint. The man is the person who departed. There is no transitive action imposed on him by an omitted binder or other agent, so this is not an agentless passive merely because it contains be plus a past participle.

### NEW-U12-A07

Simple past: “The man sat.” A contextually appropriate progressive: “The man was sitting.” Wæs is third singular preterite indicative; sittende is the present participle of sittan. The described period of being seated can motivate the second English rendering, but this Old English periphrasis does not impose a grammatical contrast identical to the modern English progressive. A simple Old English finite verb can also receive progressive English in suitable context.

### NEW-U12-A08

“He says that he is here.” “He said that he was here.” Sægð is present indicative third singular; sægde is preterite indicative third singular. The dependent sīe is present subjunctive singular and wǣre preterite subjunctive singular, each with a third-person subject. In the first report the presence is simultaneous with the present saying; in the second it is simultaneous with the past saying. The latter does not itself establish whether he is still here now, and neither subjunctive marks the report as false.

### NEW-U12-A09

“He said that he had built the house.” Sægde is the finite reporting preterite indicative, third singular. Hæfde is the finite preterite subjunctive, third singular, in this selected report; its surface is also possible as an indicative elsewhere. Geworht is the past participle. Ðæt hūs is the accusative object of the building, not the subject of hæfde. The past auxiliary with the participle and the explicit context locate the completed building before the past saying. The reporting subjunctive is identified from the assigned construction rather than from an exclusive visible ending.

### NEW-U12-A10

“He said that he intended to come.” Coming, cuman, is the intended action, with wolde expressing the past-viewed wish/intention within the report. The sentence does not say that he actually came. The intention can be later fulfilled or frustrated without changing what was reported.

### NEW-U12-A11

With the expected visit: “He will come.” Cymð is present indicative with future reference supplied by the setting. The second means “He wishes/intends to come.” Wille expresses desire or intention, with plain cuman. English “will” in a future rendering does not compel an Old English willan construction, since a present form can have future reference and willan retains a lexical meaning.

## Composition

### NEW-U12-C01

Habbað is present indicative first plural; the house is accusative and geworht the selected bare past participle.

### NEW-U12-C02

**Hīe hæfdon ðæt hūs geworht.**

Hæfdon is preterite indicative third plural. The stipulated later past event gives the past viewpoint before which the building was complete.

### NEW-U12-C03

**Ðā męnn bundon ðæt hors. Ðæt hors wearð bunden.**

The active subject męnn is nominative plural and requires bundon. The horse is accusative in the active clause and nominative in the passive. Wearð is singular because the horse is singular. The passive omits the information that the men were the agents; it is not a full restatement of every participant.

### NEW-U12-C04

**Se munuc sægð ðæt hē hēr sīe.**

**Se munuc sægde ðæt hē hēr wǣre.**

Sīe and wǣre are the selected dependent subjunctives. Each presence is simultaneous with its respective saying. The first main clause is present, the second past; the second sentence does not assert current absence.

### NEW-U12-C05

**Hē sægde ðæt hē ðæt hūs geworht hæfde.**

The final hæfde is assigned as preterite subjunctive third singular here. The object remains accusative; the completed building precedes the saying.

### NEW-U12-C06

**Hē sægde ðæt hē wolde cuman.**

Wolde with cuman reports intended coming; it does not report a completed arrival. A dependent verb-late arrangement is also possible without altering the target.

### NEW-U12-C07

Giere is the previously taught singular command. Męte is masculine accusative singular, with ðone. In the reply the food remains the affected accusative object and gierwed is the new productive past-participle base.

**Sēo brycg wæs geworht. Ðā cōm ān mann.**

The bridge is feminine nominative singular; wæs geworht supplies the requested agentless completed-state passive. The following cōm is a simple third singular preterite advancing the account. No agent of the building is specified.

Supplied on ōðre has feminine accusative singular with ceastre understood: the goal is another city. The identical possible dative surface ōðre does not override the explicitly taught accusative goal analysis. An overt city noun is not required by the supported ellipsis.

### EX-VI-21-12

Grēte is first singular present indicative. The masculine accusative native land is the object of taking leave; mīnne agrees with it. An added departure preposition would not meet the stated grētan-with-accusative target.

## New connected reading

### NEW-U12-R01

The man had built a house. Then his son came, and the son saw the house. The father said, “Now I have built the house.” The son said that he intended to go. When the son departed, the father sat in the house. Then the son came back, and he gave his father food.

The first past compound places completion before the first narrated arrival. The quoted hæbbe has the father's present viewpoint. Wolde faran reports intention without itself proving fulfillment; the later finite gewāt does narrate departure. Came back renders cōm in its return context, without claiming that cōm has a distinct return inflection. In the last clause his fæder is dative recipient and męte the accusative thing given.

### NEW-U12-R02

Order: building the house → the son's first arrival → the father's announcement → the son's departure → the son's return with food. The report of intention falls between the announcement and the departure, though it is not one of the five listed events. **Hæfde … geworht** in the opening places the completed building before the first arrival, cōm.

### NEW-U12-R03

Ic in the quotation is the father, who is speaking. Hē in **ðæt hē wolde faran** refers to the son, whose intention is reported. Hē in the last sentence is also the son, the preceding subject of cōm. The report alone gives intention, but the next narration **se sunu gewāt** expressly says that the son departed. It does not merely repeat the wish. The passage does not supply a distant destination or a detailed itinerary.

### NEW-U12-R04

In **Nū hæbbe ic ðæt hūs geworht**, ic is nominative subject and builder; ðæt hūs is accusative object and the affected thing. The requested agentless state is **Ðæt hūs is geworht.** Here the same neuter noun phrase is nominative subject, is is present third singular, and geworht remains the bare participle. The builder is not named in the new version.

## Picts and Scots source reading

### NEW-U12-S01

Then two peoples, the Picts from the north and the Scots from the west, began to attack them, take their possessions and plunder; and for many years they afflicted and oppressed them. Then, amid that unrest, the Britons sent messengers to Rome with letters and a tearful petition. They asked the Romans for help and promised them humble obedience and continual subjection if the Romans would help them so that they could overcome their enemies. Then the Romans sent them a large army to their aid, and as soon as the soldiers came to this island, they fought against the Britons' enemies, inflicted great slaughter on them, and drove them out of the Britons' borders and put them to flight. They instructed the Britons to build a fortification for their own protection against their enemies; and thus the Romans went home with great victory.

When the former enemies then realized this—that the Roman army had gone away—they immediately came with a fleet into the Britons' territory, struck down and killed all they encountered, trampled them down and destroyed them like ripe crops, and reduced them utterly to misery.

The explicit names supplied in English recover referents from the printed context/help; they are not claimed as additional nouns in the source. In the first repeated hīe clause, assigning hīe as Briton object with a continuing raider subject, or as raider subject with a Briton object understood, yields the same events. The Britons request the help; the Romans give it. The report/condition is prospective from the promise. The advice clause does not itself narrate completion of the fortification. In the final paragraph, ealle and the following collective/headless ðæt need not be forced into an overt masculine personal-relative analysis. The object of the crop comparison is the attacked Britons, not a separate account of literal agricultural destruction. All stages through foriermdon are included; the subsequent renewed appeal is outside this extract.

### NEW-U12-S02

Order: first raids → appeal to Rome → Roman relief → Roman withdrawal → renewed attack. The enemies learn that the Roman army has gone away; that recognition precedes and motivates their immediate return in the narrator's account. The text does not say that the Britons invited the renewed attack or that the Romans themselves return as the attackers.

### NEW-U12-S03

Within **Ðā ðæt ðā ongēaton ðā ǣrran gewinnan**:

- The first ðā introduces the temporal clause, “when”.
- The second ðā is an adverb, “then”.
- The third ðā is the nominative plural article/demonstrative with **ǣrran gewinnan**, “the former enemies”.
- The first ðæt is an anticipatory accusative neuter object of ongēaton, “this/that”; the following content clause explains what they realized.

These functions are distinct despite the repeated surfaces. The temporal clause is resumed by the later main-clause ðā cōmon in the full passage. Gewinnan here is the supplied weak masculine plural noun, not the earlier neuter trouble noun or an infinitive.

### NEW-U12-S04

The Britons send the messengers to Rome and ask the Romans for help. The Romans then send the army to the Britons. The Roman soldiers fight the enemies after arriving on the island. The pronoun hīe alone does not encode which group is meant; the request/promise, response and arrival sequence supplies the referents. Cook146.4 explicitly identifies the arriving group as the legionaries; note146.5 explains that the borders from which the foes are driven are the Britons' borders. Repeating a single assumed referent for every hīe would reverse the request and aid roles.

### NEW-U12-S05

Wæs is third singular preterite indicative agreeing with **se Rōmanisca hęre**. Onweg is the supplied away adverb. Gewiten is the past participle of intransitive gewītan. Together they mean “had gone away”. The departure precedes the enemies' realization, and therefore their ensuing arrival/renewed attack. The army is the departing participant, not an affected patient of another person's departure; this is the recognized intransitive completed-state/perfect expression, not an agentless passive.

### NEW-U12-S06

In **Ðā onsęndon hīe him micelne hęre**, hīe, the Romans, is nominative plural subject; him, the Britons, is dative recipient; micelne hęre is masculine accusative singular object, the large army. An English passive preserving all these roles is: **“Then a large army was sent to the Britons by the Romans.”** The Old English is nevertheless active. The Latin passive compared in Cook's note is evidence about the translation, not a reason to reparse hīe as the recipients or the Old English sentence as passive.

### NEW-U12-S07

The Romans advise/instruct the Britons to build a fortification for their own protection. The recommended building is **prospective from the instruction**. Worhten is a preterite subjunctive in the advice clause, not evidence that the building had already been completed. The clause reports the recommendation; it does not independently assert execution or completion. Him refers to the beneficiaries/builders, the Britons, in the supported for-themselves phrase.

### NEW-U12-S08

The victims are the Britons. **Rīpe ierðe**, supplied as the collective “ripe crops”, is the standard of a comparison: the invaders trample and destroy the people as they would a crop. It intensifies the depiction of vulnerability and comprehensive devastation. It does not change the ordinary lexical identity of the people into a new noun meaning crops, nor require a literal harvest scene as an additional event. The final **mid ealle foriermdon** continues the portrayal of utter ruin.
