# Unit 5. Strong verbs IV–VII

This unit completes the principal strong-verb classes with selected present and preterite indicative systems. Four further verbs from Classes I–III provide the forms needed for the complete fifth Cook exercise.

In the tables, **1**, **2** and **3** mean first, second and third person; **sg.** and **pl.** mean singular and plural. **1–3 pl.** gives the same form for all three plural persons. Dual subjects also take plural verbs. Each verb’s paired finite tables cover all six present and all six preterite indicative cells. Preserve the printed quantities and the distinction between e and ę.

## 1. Principal parts and endings

Strong verbs change their stem vowel in the preterite; the weak verbs learned earlier add a dental element. Four principal parts identify the strong stem series. The infinitive names the verb, the singular preterite gives the first/third singular stem, the plural preterite gives the plural stem, and the participle completes the cited series.

<!-- table: U05-ENDINGS -->

| Person and number | Present | Preterite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 singular | -e, with the special contracted and -ie forms below | singular preterite stem; no added ending |
| 2 singular | -st or -est, with the listed stem changes | plural preterite stem + -e |
| 3 singular | -ð or -eð, with the listed stem changes | singular preterite stem; no added ending |
| 1, 2, 3 plural | -að, with the listed contracted and -iað forms | plural preterite stem + -on |

The **second singular preterite uses the plural stem**. Thus beran has bær for the first and third singular, but bǣre beside plural bǣron. In other verbs the singular and plural stem vowels coincide; their different endings still identify the cells.

The infinitive and participle columns are **supplied citations** throughout this unit. Consult them in the principal-part exercises. They do not require independent nonfinite recall or the use of infinitive, participial, perfect or passive constructions. A participle in a four-part entry is not another finite preterite.

The second and third singular present often have a changed vowel and a reduced ending. Short e may appear as i; short a gives æ, short ea gives short ie, long ēa gives long īe, ā gives ǣ, and ō gives ē in the stated paradigms. These changes are lexical patterns, not an instruction to alter every similar-looking vowel. Dental combinations give forms such as -tst and -tt, and doubled stem consonants simplify before consonantal endings. The tables and notes give the resulting forms together.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-PREFIXED-LEXEMES -->

**Gesēon, gescieppan and geswīcan** are learned as separate verbs. Their meanings and forms are stated explicitly. The prefix is not an instruction to create further verbs or to assign a fixed difference in meaning to every prefixed pair. The checked headword **gesēon** follows Cook’s dictionary ge-sēon, with the dictionary hyphen omitted; the preterite geseah keeps its printed spelling.

**Cweðan** has cwist and cwið in the selected second and third singular present. Its past singular cwæð retains ð, while cwǣde and cwǣdon have d; the supplied participle cweden also has d. Keep this consonant change with these stem positions.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-PALATAL-V -->

**Giefan** has the palatal series giefan, geaf, gēafon, giefen. In particular, geaf has short ea, but gēafe and gēafon have long ēa. The present forms retain the selected ie spelling.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-J-PRESENT-V -->

**Biddan, licgan and sittan** have doubled consonants in the present first singular and plural, but a single stem consonant before the second/third singular endings. The resulting forms are bitst/bitt, ligst/ligð and sitst/sitt. Their strong pasts use bæd/bǣdon, læg/lǣgon and sæt/sǣton. These are often called **j-presents**: the label explains a present-stem pattern, not a weak preterite.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-ETAN-EXCEPTION -->

**Etan** has itst and itt in the present singular. The selected preterite ǣt has long ǣ, as do ǣte and ǣton. Its singular past therefore differs from the ordinary short-æ Class V pattern.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-CONTRACT-V -->

**Sēon** and **gesēon** have contracted presents. Their first singular ends in -o and their plural in -oð. The second and third singular have **short ie**: siehst/siehð and gesiehst/gesiehð. Their plural-based past stems have āw: sāwe/sāwon and gesāwe/gesāwon. Preserve the difference between these forms and the singular seah/geseah.

**Alternatives.** Third-singular bit, sit and it occur beside selected bitt, sitt and itt. Cook gives līst and līð beside ligst and ligð, and short æt beside selected ǣt; Wright also gives lāgon beside lǣgon. For sēon, Cook gives the participle segen beside sewen, and Wright records sǣgon and sawen beside sāwon and sewen. The corresponding prefixed citation alternative is gesegen beside gesewen. These spellings do not replace the selected tables or require extra nonfinite recall.

**Standan** keeps n in the present but loses it in the past stōd/stōdon. Its changed present singulars are stęntst and stęnt. This is the ordinary verb of standing; the earlier reading’s opposing-winds construction requires its own supplied explanation.

Cook also gives the participle slegen beside slægen; Wright records slōh beside singular slōg and the participle slagen. The selected cells remain those in the tables.

## 5. Class VII

Cook groups these verbs as **reduplicating verbs**; they are commonly called Class VII. For present purposes, learn two past-vowel groups, ēo and ē. Within each verb the selected preterite stem is the same in singular and plural. The present and participle usually share their vowel, but the complete entries preserve exceptions such as wēpan/wōpen and fōn/fangen.

| swimman | swamm | swumme | swamm | swummon |

Geswīcan and scīnan have long ī in the present, long ā in the first/third singular past, and short i in the plural-based past. ## 7. Ordinary uses

The glosses in the principal-part tables identify the verbs. The constructions below supply the ordinary uses needed in sentences. For other verbs, the practice asks for labelled person, number and tense forms; a gloss alone does not specify which objects or clauses may follow.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-CARRY-TAKE-OBJECTS -->

**Beran**, “carry,” and **niman**, “take,” take an accusative person or thing. The subject controls the finite verb. The following eight examples are new teaching material.

<!-- example: NEW-U05-E01 -->

Ðū birst ðone męte.

You (singular) carry the food.

<!-- example: NEW-U05-E02 -->

Hē nimð ðone fisc.

He takes the fish.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-GIVING-RECIPIENT -->

With **giefan**, the thing given is accusative and the recipient is dative. English may express the recipient with “to”; Old English expresses the relation here by the case of the recipient itself.

<!-- example: NEW-U05-E03 -->

Ic giefe ðǣm męnn ðone męte.

I give the man the food.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-INTRANSITIVE-POSTURE -->

**Sittan**, “sit,” **licgan**, “lie; be at rest,” and **standan**, “stand,” describe the subject’s posture. A locative **on + dative** phrase can state where that subject is. No object is needed for these ordinary posture uses.

<!-- example: NEW-U05-E04 -->

Se mann ligð on ðǣm hofe.

The man lies in the dwelling.

<!-- example: NEW-U05-E05 -->

Se mann stęnt on ðǣm strande.

The man stands on the shore.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-INTRANSITIVE-MOTION -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-BRIDGE-INTRANSITIVE -->

**Feallan**, “fall,” **weaxan**, “grow,” **flēogan**, “fly,” **scīnan**, “shine,” and **swimman**, “swim,” have ordinary intransitive uses. The growing tree, flying bird, shining star or swimming fish is the subject. A locative on-phrase may state where the swimming occurs.

<!-- example: NEW-U05-E06 -->

Ðæt trēo fielð.

The tree falls.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-EAT -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-SEE-OBJECT -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-STRIKE-HOLD-OBJECTS -->

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-U05-CREATE-OBJECT -->

**Etan**, “eat,” takes an accusative food object; it also occurs with the food understood. **Sēon** and **gesēon**, “see,” take an accusative person or thing in the use practised here. **Slēan**, “strike; smite,” and **healdan**, “hold,” likewise take an accusative object. “Strike” does not by itself say that the object is killed. **Scieppan** and **gescieppan** take the created being or thing as accusative object in the Creation sentences.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-CESSATION-GENITIVE -->

**Geswīcan**, “cease,” takes the **genitive** of the work or action from which one ceases. Keep this government when English uses “cease from.” An agreeing possessive takes the case, number and gender of the work noun; the personal genitives his and hiera remain the possessor forms already learned.

<!-- example: NEW-U05-E07 -->

Wē geswīcað ūres weorces.

We cease from our work.

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-DIRECT-QUOTATION -->

**Cweðan**, “say,” can introduce direct quotation. The words inside the quotation form their own clause; a first-person pronoun there denotes the quoted speaker. Content clauses introduced by “that” remain separately supplied in the reading.

<!-- example: NEW-U05-E08 -->

Se mann cwið: “Ic sēo ðone fugol.”

The man says, “I see the bird.”

<!-- teaching: NEW-U05-GRAM-SIMPLE-NEGATION -->

For these ordinary indicative clauses, put **ne before the finite verb** to negate the statement. A past negative still uses the appropriate strong preterite; English “did not” does not add a separate Old English auxiliary here. Subjects in the dual take plural verb forms, while dual possessives still identify two possessors and agree with the possessed noun.

No new productive noun pattern is needed. Continue the complete noun, adjective, article, pronoun and possessive paradigms of Units 1–4. In particular, the learned masculine heofon supplies plural “heavens”; the reading’s weak feminine heofone receives separate local help.
