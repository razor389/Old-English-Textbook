## Forms and case use

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-F01 -->

**F01. Sunu.**

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | sunu | suna |
| Genitive | suna | suna |
| Dative | suna | sunum |
| Accusative | sunu | suna |

Dative singular is **suna**, ending in -a; nominative and accusative singular are **sunu**, ending in -u. Neither the spelling suna alone nor the ending -a alone fixes a single case or number.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-F02 -->

**F02. Bycgan, indicative.**

| Person and number | Present | Preterite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First singular | bycge | bohte |
| Second singular | bygest | bohtest |
| Third singular | bygeð | bohte |
| First plural | bycgað | bohton |
| Second plural | bycgað | bohton |
| Third plural | bycgað | bohton |

The present alternates bycg- with byg-; the preterite uses boh-, not a dental past built directly from bycg-.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-F03 -->

**F03. Commands.**

| Verb | One addressee: second singular | More than one addressee: second plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| rōwan | rōw | rōwað |
| bycgan | byge | bycgað |

The plural forms address two or more people. These selected commands can omit their subject; no postposed-pronoun variant is needed.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-F04 -->

**F04. Rīce and geðeahtend.**

| Case | Rīce singular | Rīce plural | Geðeahtend singular | Geðeahtend plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | rīce | rīcu | geðeahtend | geðeahtend |
| Genitive | rīces | rīca | geðeahtendes | geðeahtendra |
| Dative | rīce | rīcum | geðeahtende | geðeahtendum |
| Accusative | rīce | rīcu | geðeahtend | geðeahtend |

Rīce has plural -u in nominative/accusative, -a in genitive and -um in dative. Geðeahtend has selected zero nominative/accusative plural, genitive plural -ra and dative plural -um.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-F05 -->

**F05.** **Ðāra geðeahtendra rīcu.** The counsellors are genitive plural because their phrase depends on rīcu: “the counsellors’ kingdoms”. Rīcu is nominative plural; the possessive genitive comes first and no separate head article is inserted.

**Ic giefe ðone fisc ðǣm geðeahtendum.** The counsellors are dative plural as recipients of the giving. The singular fish is accusative.

## Content clauses

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-A01 -->

**A01.** (a) In **Ðæt scip is gōd**, ðæt is the inflected neuter nominative singular article/demonstrative determining scip, the subject of is. (b) In **Ic wāt, ðæt ðæt scip gōd is**, the first ðæt is an invariant content conjunction, with no case; the second is neuter nominative singular determining the subject scip. (c) In **Ðæt scip, ðæt ðū hæfst, is gōd**, the first ðæt is neuter nominative singular with the main-clause subject scip; the second is a neuter accusative singular relative, the object of hæfst within the relative clause. The relative has the antecedent’s gender and number but its own clause determines its case.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-A02 -->

**A02.** **Ic wāt, ðæt ðā scipu īdlu sindon.** The content verb is indicative and its subject is neuter plural; īdlu agrees with scipu.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-A03 -->

**A03.** **Se fiscere sægð, ðæt hē twēgen fiscas hæbbe.** Hē refers to the fisherman, formerly the “I” of the quotation; hæbbe is third-person singular present subjunctive.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-A04 -->

**A04.** **Se mann sægde, ðæt hē twēgen fiscas hæfde.** Hē denotes the same man. Hæfde is the selected third-person singular preterite subjunctive for his possession at the time of the past speech. The change is grounded in the stated time relationship.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-A05 -->

**A05.** **Hē sægð, ðæt hē gōd is** is not necessarily ungrammatical: indicative is can present the reported proposition as a fact. It does not meet this task’s express subjunctive target. The requested version is **Hē sægð, ðæt hē gōd sīe**. Subjunctive sīe marks the reported assertion without requiring that the narrator assert its truth; it does not itself call the assertion false.

## Dependent questions

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-Q01 -->

**Q01.** (a) **Ic wāt, hwā ðone fisc hæbbe**: “I know who has the fish.” Hwā is nominative, subject of hæbbe. (b) **Ic wāt, hwǣm ðū ðone fisc sęlle**: “I know to whom you give the fish.” Hwǣm is dative, the recipient of the giving; ðū is nominative subject and ðone fisc is accusative object. Both singular dependent verbs are subjunctive in the given clauses.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-Q02 -->

**Q02.** **Saga mē, hwæt ðū hæbbe.** “Tell me what you have.” The addressee remains ðū; hwæt is accusative object and hæbbe is second-person singular present subjunctive.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-Q03 -->

**Q03.** **Saga mē, hwæðer se mann twēgen fiscas hæbbe.** “Tell me whether the man has two fish.” Hwæðer introduces the yes/no question and hæbbe is third-person singular present subjunctive.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-Q04 -->

**Q04.** (a) **Hwæðer mann is ðīn gefēra?** means “Which of the two men is your companion?” Hwæðer is an inflected masculine nominative singular interrogative adjective agreeing with mann. (b) **Saga mē, hwæðer se mann ðīn gefēra sīe** means “Tell me whether the man is your companion.” Here hwæðer is an invariant conjunction introducing a dependent yes/no question; it has no agreement with mann. Sīe is present subjunctive.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-Q05 -->

**Q05.** **Ic wāt, hwǣm ðū helpe.** “I know whom you help.” Helpan governs the dative, hence hwǣm rather than an accusative question form. Helpe is second-person singular present subjunctive.

## Infinitives and predicates

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-I01 -->

**I01.** (a) **Ic wille ðone fisc beran**: “I want to carry the fish.” The speaker ic is the understood carrier and the subject of wille. (b) **Ic hāte hine ðone fisc beran**: “I order him to carry the fish.” Hine is the accusative commanded actor; ic orders, while the person denoted by hine carries. In both sentences ðone fisc is the object carried. Beran is a plain infinitive, not a finite verb agreeing with either ic or hine, so it has no person ending.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-I02 -->

**I02.** **Se fiscere hēt hīe ðone fisc beran.** “The fisherman ordered them to carry the fish.” Hīe denotes the two companions and is accusative plural; hēt reports the past command. The original plural command Berað becomes the uninflected-for-person plain infinitive beran.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-I03 -->

**I03.** **Ic gesēo ðā męnn ðone fisc beran.** “I see the men carrying the fish.” **Ðā męnn** is the accusative plural perceived actor of beran; **ðone fisc** is its accusative singular object. Gesēo is the finite perception verb.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-I04 -->

**I04.** **God geworhte ðone mann gōdne. God geworhte ðā męnn gōde.** Gōdne is strong masculine accusative singular; gōde is strong masculine accusative plural. Each predicate agrees with the object it describes. The object’s article does not require weak inflection on a separate predicate adjective.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-I05 -->

**I05.** **Hē hēt ðone mann cyning**: “He called the man king.” Cyning is an accusative singular noun predicate identifying the accusative object ðone mann. **Hē hēt ðone mann gān**: “He ordered the man to go.” Gān is a plain infinitive, and ðone mann is its commanded actor. The complements distinguish naming from commanding despite the identical finite hēt.

## Wishes

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-W01 -->

**W01.** **Hæfde ic ān scip!** “Would that I had one ship!” Hæfde is first-person singular preterite subjunctive expressing the unreal present wish; ān agrees with neuter accusative singular scip.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-W02 -->

**W02.** (a) **Ic hæfde ān scip** in the last-year setting means “I had one ship.” Hæfde is first-person singular preterite indicative describing actual past possession. (b) **Hæfde ic ān scip!** in the no-ship-now setting means “Would that I had one ship!” It is preterite subjunctive with present unreal reference. The form hæfde is compatible with both analyses; its spelling alone establishes neither mood nor actual past possession. Context and construction settle the interpretation.

## The fisherman and his companion

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-R01 -->

**R01.** The companion asks for information: **Se gefēra cwið: “Saga mē, hwæt ðū hæbbe.”** The fisherman is reported as having food: **Se fiscere sægð, ðæt hē męte hæbbe.** Hē in that report refers to the fisherman.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-R02 -->

**R02.** **Hwæt ðū hæbbe** is a dependent wh-question after “tell me”; hwæt is the accusative object and hæbbe is second-person singular present subjunctive. **Ðæt hē męte hæbbe** is a reported content clause after sægð; hæbbe is third-person singular present subjunctive. **Ðæt ðū mē hilpst** is a factual content clause after wāt; hilpst is second-person singular present indicative. The first two identical spellings hæbbe have different persons fixed by their subjects; the last clause asserts the speaker’s knowledge of help being given.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-R03 -->

**R03.** In **Hē hǣtt his gefēran ðone fisc beran**, the fisherman is the subject of finite hǣtt, his gefēran is the accusative singular commanded actor, and ðone fisc is the accusative object carried. In **Ic gesēo ðē ðone fisc beran**, ic is the fisherman speaking and the subject of finite gesēo; accusative ðē denotes the observed companion. The fish is again the object of beran, not the observed actor. The plain infinitive does not change its form when its actor changes from a noun phrase to a pronoun.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-R04 -->

**R04. Complete translation.** The fisherman and his companion stand on the shore. The companion says, “Tell me what you have.” The fisherman says that he has food. He orders his companion to carry the fish. The companion carries the fish. The fisherman says, “I see you carrying the fish. Now I know that you help me.”

The earlier food clause reports the fisherman’s assertion; the final clause presents his knowledge of the companion’s help. “Carrying” translates the plain infinitive in the perception construction, while “carries” translates finite birð in the preceding sentence.

## The Choice of Occupations

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-S01 -->

**S01.** Service of God holds first place among the occupations considered overall. Agriculture holds first place when the question is restricted to worldly occupations. Those are different comparison groups, so the answers do not contradict each other.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-S02 -->

**S02.** The Gospel quotation begins **Fyrmest sēceað** and ends **tōgēiecte ēow**. It commands the plural addressees first to seek God’s kingdom and his righteousness, and promises that all these things will be added to them. Sēceað is the source plural command; bēoð tōgēiecte expresses the supplied passive promise. The introductory “just as it is read in the Gospel” is outside the quotation.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-S03 -->

**S03.** In **Ic sęcge ðē, mē is geðūht**, ðē is the dative recipient of sęcge: the counsellor tells the teacher. Mē is the dative experiencer in “it seems to me”: the judgment is the counsellor’s. The two datives have different grammatical roles and refer to different people.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-S04 -->

**S04.** Agriculture is first among worldly occupations because the farmer feeds everyone in the relevant community. **Ūs ealle** means “us all”, including the speaker and the addressed/discussed group; it is the accusative object of fēt. **Se ierðling**, “the farmer”, is the nominative subject of finite third-person singular fēt. The farmer is not the object being fed.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-S05 -->

**S05. Complete four-turn translation.**

Teacher: “What do you say, wise man? Which occupation among these seems to you to be foremost?”

Counsellor: “I tell you, it seems to me that service of God holds first place among these occupations, just as it is read in the Gospel: ‘First seek the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you.’”

Teacher: “And which among worldly occupations seems to you to hold first place?”

Counsellor: “Agriculture, because the farmer feeds us all.”

“Service of God” means serving God; God is the one served. The first comparison is general, while the teacher’s second question expressly narrows the comparison to worldly occupations. The quotation includes both the command and the complete promise. The English “seems” follows the locally supplied impersonal expression rather than imposing an accusative analysis on every word in that source construction.

## Cumulative practice

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-X01 -->

**X01.** **Se fiscere hēt ðā męnn ðone fisc beran. Ic geseah hīe hine beran. Ic wāt, ðæt hīe him hulpon.**

The first sentence commands accusative plural actors to carry a singular accusative fish. In the second, hīe denotes those men and masculine accusative singular hine denotes the fish. The last sentence is factual indicative content: hīe are still the men and dative him denotes the fisherman they helped. Old English masculine pronouns can refer to a grammatically masculine fish where English uses “it”.

<!-- solution: NEW-U09-X02 -->

**X02.** (a) **Ic gesēo hine ðone fisc beran.** Replace nominative hē with accusative hine, the actor in the perception-infinitive construction. (b) **God geworhte ðone mann gōdne.** Replace nominative-shaped gōd with strong masculine accusative singular gōdne, agreeing with the accusative object of which the quality is predicated. Beran and the object-predicate construction are both retained.
