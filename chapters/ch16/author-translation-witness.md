# Unit 16: author’s translation witness

Prepared after the pre-prose contract checkpoint `543e214`, directly from the 444-word Old English transcript and personally re-opened images of Cook, printed pages 168–170 (one-based PDF pages 184–186). The assigned span runs from **Æfter ðisum wordum** to **tō his gereorde**. The following feast is outside it.

This witness was written before consulting the coordinator’s or curriculum analyst’s complete English translation. It is independent translation preparation, not a fresh blind review. During the preceding linguistic audit I had already read the whole Old English, its notes, lexical helps and verbal analyses. I had discussed the negative **næfde** and uncertain reference at 169.5–7, the uncertain **top** and the differing Latin account at 169.11–13, pronoun reference during the king’s departure, substitute **dōn**, the identity of **āgan/āgān**, and the mixed direct/indirect invitation. I also knew the corrected explanation of the Latin ointment passage. These exposures are acknowledged rather than concealed.

## Translation

After these words he went along the way that had been shown to him, until he reached the city gate and entered there. While he was thinking whom he might ask for help in sustaining his life, he saw a naked boy running along the street. The boy was anointed with oil and girded with a cloth, and carried in his hand the equipment for young men’s games belonging to the baths. He called out in a loud voice and said: “Listen, citizens! Listen, strangers, free and enslaved, noble and lowborn! The baths are open.”

When Apollonius heard this, he took off the half-cloak that he was wearing and went into the bathing place. As he observed each of them at their activity, he looked for his equal, but could not find him there in the crowd. Then suddenly Arcestrates, king of that whole country, arrived with a large company of his men and entered the bath. The king began playing ball with his companions. Apollonius joined in the king’s game, as God willed; running, he caught the ball and, after striking it with swift speed, sent it back to the king who was playing. The king sent it back again; Apollonius struck it swiftly, so that he never let it fall. The king then recognized the young man’s agility, so that he knew that he had no equal in that game. He then said to his companions: “Go away from here; this young man, as it seems to me, is my equal.”

When Apollonius heard that the king was praising him, he ran quickly and approached the king, and with a skilled hand he swung the *top*—apparently the ball—with such great speed that it seemed to the king as though he had been changed from old age back to youth. Afterwards Apollonius attended the king agreeably at his royal seat. When the king came out of the bath, Apollonius led him by the hand, and then Apollonius went away from there along the way by which he had come earlier.

After Apollonius had gone away, the king said to his men: “I swear by our common safety that I have never bathed better than I did today, through the service of a young man whose identity I do not know.” He then turned his gaze towards one of his men and said: “Go and find out what sort of man the young man is who served me so well today.”

The man then went after Apollonius. When he saw that Apollonius was wrapped in a dirty cloak, he returned to the king and said: “The young man you asked about is a shipwrecked man.” The king said: “How do you know that?” The man answered him: “Even if he himself keeps silent about it, his clothing reveals his condition.” Then the king said: “Go quickly and tell him that ‘the king asks you to come to his meal.’”

## Decisions and limits retained in this witness

- **168.6–7:** “Equipment for young men’s games” gives the concrete sense needed by **bær … on handa**. The literal expression is “young men’s games”; **belimpende** modifies **plegan**, as the original note states. This wording does not identify a particular implement that the source never names.
- **169.2–3:** The participle **geslægene** is awkwardly attached to the ball. “After striking it” makes the event intelligible in English; it does not assert that the Old English has a separate finite verb there.
- **169.4–5:** The exchange is rendered with the king returning the ball and Apollonius striking it. The repeated masculine pronouns alone do not identify both players; the game sequence supplies that interpretation. The final **hine** denotes the ball.
- **169.5–7:** The translation deliberately preserves “he knew that he had no equal” instead of silently making the disputed pronouns explicit. **Næfde** must remain negative. Cook’s note says the clause is unclear; the Latin parallel makes the king the person who knew he had no equal, but does not remove the uncertainty of the actual Old English. “The king knew that the young man had no equal” is another possible interpretation, not a demonstrated correction of the text.
- **169.11–13:** The Old English says that he **swang ðone top**. Cook tentatively connects **top** with **ðoðor**, the ball. His Latin parallel instead describes rubbing/anointing the king with ointment so rapidly that he seemed young again. It supplies no shoulder. The translation keeps the Old English action and its uncertain object rather than substituting the Latin event.
- **169.14–17:** The selected reference is Apollonius serving and leading the king, then turning away himself. Cook’s notes explicitly identify the leading **hē** as Apollonius and **hine** as the king; **him** with **gewęnde** is the dative reflexive. The royal seat belongs to the king.
- **170.2–4:** “Our common safety” preserves the oath’s **gemǣnan hǣle** without expanding the note about Christian influence into extra narrative content. “Whose identity I do not know” is an idiomatic rendering of the awkward **nāt ic ðurh hwilces geonges mannes ðēnunge**; the following inquiry asks what the young man is or what his condition is, not merely for his name. The English present perfect is a translation choice for the retrospective comparison, not a claim that the Old English has a perfect auxiliary.
- **170.13–16:** The concession remains “even if,” with the clothing revealing the man’s condition despite his possible silence. The final English retains the conspicuous **tell him that ‘the king asks you …’** mixture. The outer speaker is the king, the immediate addressee his messenger, and the inner “you” Apollonius.
