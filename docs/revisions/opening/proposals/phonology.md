# Opening pronunciation: source proposal

The guide needs an ordered c/g procedure, not just a list of possible sounds. Its original practice largely repeats its worked words. The bounded repair is to teach positions and combinations, reserve fresh words before drafting, and make genuine lexical exceptions explicit. No answer key was read; no Cook exercise is changed or reassigned.

## The source distinction that matters

Fulk §18 (printed 12–13 / PDF 24–25) separates two decisions for **g**. First identify palatal **y** versus nonpalatal g. Then nonpalatal g is the stopped sound of *go* initially, after **n** or in gemination; elsewhere it is the continuous voiced back sound. **Glæd** demonstrates why a later front vowel does not override an intervening consonant; **āgan** provides a fresh continuous-g application. This distribution can be taught without historical reconstructions.

Fulk §135a–b (103 / PDF 115) does **not** give c and g the same medial/final rule. G can become y after any earlier front vowel when a back vowel does not immediately follow. Noninitial c became ch after earlier **i/ī**, or before **i/j**; later consonant contact, back-vowel endings and analogy complicate the result. His explicit **dīċ/dīcas** and **miċel/micles** are useful small comparisons. The initial front-vowel tendency is helpful, but **cēlan** and **gǣstan** show that the written vowel may reflect a later change. In particular, modern written **y/ȳ** is not an automatic ch/y instruction. Cook §10 (9 / PDF 25) itself places y/ȳ with guttural environments. Keep the historical explanation in a source note or the existing appendix.

The practical procedure should therefore be:

1. Read **sc, cg/gg, ng** as combinations first; obey an explicit word/form pronunciation note.
2. For initial **c**, use k before a consonant or a/o/u and their long counterparts as the ordinary pattern; ch before e/i/æ and front diphthong spellings is a **default**, with stated exceptions. Do not infer a sound merely from y/ȳ.
3. For c elsewhere, distinguish the after-i/ī pattern from the before-consonant pattern. Do not export g’s broader front-vowel tendency to c, and do not invent a current-spelling rule that makes every c before a back vowel hard: old-j words such as **sēcan** expose that error.
4. Identify the y branch of **g** using the stated front-vowel positions and supplied lexical information, then apply the stopped/continuous distribution to nonpalatal g. **Regn** usefully differs from c-before-consonant examples.
5. State the fallback plainly: some words require a supplied pronunciation. The exercise pool is vetted to follow the taught default unless a lexical exception is explicitly supplied. A beginner is not expected to reconstruct an invisible old vowel or j.

Use *kit*, *chin*, *go*, *yes*, *ship*, the last sound of *bridge*, and *finger* versus *singe*. Keep the current tongue/airflow instruction for continuous g and at most its single existing IPA introduction. No new IPA system, trill or aspiration drill is needed.

For exceptions, supplying the exceptional sound and asking the learner to apply it in a new word/context is legitimate. Asking the learner to derive it from misleading spelling is not. Most assessed items should instead select a regular sound and state the relevant immediate context. The voicing selection deliberately includes **ǣfre** before voiced r, **fīf** at both word edges, and **hæfð** beside a voiceless consonant.

## Boundaries for the contract

Keep every Cook prompt/item, existing paradigms, course spelling and the selected vowel/diphthong convention. Change the original guide exposition/examples and original practice only. Reserve the final assessment forms before prose; do not later use them as worked examples. Supply every meaning, and give unrelated difficult pronunciations where necessary, while leaving the assessed regular feature unsolved.

Two bounded new-context candidates are **Gold is hāt.** (“Gold is hot.”) and **Regn is fæger.** (“Rain is beautiful.”). Their construction and meaning are supplied; they are not grammar-production tasks. Reserve their content words if selected, and do not print their target g decisions in an adjacent help table. These are editorial candidates, not final guide prose or a key.
