## Sequence and outcomes

1. Distinguish a lexical base, a derivational element and an inflectional ending. A family relationship can help explain a word; it does not establish that every imaginable new formation exists. Meaning and inflection belong to the attested whole word. Existing vocabulary supplies most introductory comparisons.
2. Analyse selected transparent compounds through their components and final head. Compare an ordinary compound with an inflected noun phrase; separate an inflection such as source -e or -um from the compound itself. Give the meanings of any component used only as a citation. Do not silently teach its whole declension or every sense.

Separate the derivational suffix from the ending in an actual inflected adjective. Do not infer an unattested adverb, a new adjective paradigm or an independent syntactic use from a displayed word family.

Put important spelling/stem changes beside the system, including the distinct weak-I ætīewan. Supplied rare finite and participial expressions do not establish additional productive paradigms.

The compositions apply noun classes and giving, leaving, belief and mercy frames; they are new teaching material. The Cook sets consolidate prior clause systems with the new lexical material.
9. Read the exact opening argument of *The Dangers of Greatness*. Its three concrete comparisons lead to an explicit conclusion about human power, wealth and earthly wonders.
