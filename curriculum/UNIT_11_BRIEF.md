## Clause relations and mood

Begin with the relation between the clauses, then the conjunction, mood and context. Distinguish a subordinating word from an identically spelled adverb, determiner or content-clause introducer. Reuse earlier relative and content-clause work only within its actual scope.

Teach past **ðā … ðā** and recurring or prospective **ðonne … ðonne** correlations, selected simultaneous **ðā-hwīle-ðe** and **mid-ðȳ**, and anticipated **ǣr** and **oð-ðæt**. Explain ordinary word-order tendencies without presenting rigid inversion or verb-final transformations. The relation's presentation and the speaker's viewpoint matter; a subjunctive temporal clause can concern an event that subsequently occurred.

Teach finite purpose **ðæt**, including different subjects in the two clauses, alongside the earlier bounded same-subject purpose infinitive. Teach intended prevention with **ðȳ-lǣs-ðe**. Contrast intended purpose with asserted result in **swā … ðæt**. Purpose normally takes the subjunctive; a factual result normally takes the indicative. Cook §196.g's consecutive example is an optative curse, not evidence that every result requires a subjunctive. Reconcile Cook §§194–196 with Fulk §45 rather than deriving a universal mood rule from one example.

Teach a stated reason with **for-ðon-ðe** and the selected concessive **ðēah** frame. A concessive subjunctive can concede a fact; it need not imply doubt or falsity. Contrast an open **gif** condition with an explicitly contrary-present situation. The latter's past subjunctive morphology does not by itself locate the event in past time. Give contexts and defensible alternatives rather than grading solely by the conjunction or English tense. Broad tense sequence and compound tense/voice systems remain for Unit 12.

In the sea passage distinguish the purpose of seeing how the voyage would turn out, the dependent question, the reason for the travellers' return journey, the degree/result expressions and the concluding content clause. Explain the understood accusative actor in the perception-plus-infinitive frame locally. The supplied **wæs gebiddende** and **wæs blinnende** do not require mechanical English progressives; preserve Cook's cross-reference allowing a simple past. The narrated subject in the calming clause remains Æthelwald. Explain **Gode lēofan** as the dative complement of *dear*, rather than changing the case of the person seen.
