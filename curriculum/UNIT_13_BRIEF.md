## Clause order and reference

Begin by identifying each finite verb, its subject and the complete constituents belonging to its clause. A noun phrase can contain several words. A verb in a second-constituent position need not be the second written word. Case, agreement and government continue to determine roles when an object or adverbial precedes the verb.

Compare subject-first main clauses with a fronted object or adverbial. Explain common verb placement and the frequent early position of personal pronouns. A coordinator is not automatically the first constituent of the following clause. Use checked exceptions to prevent a rigid universal rule. The short exact fragment **Æfter ðon hē underðēodde**, outside the assigned reading, may serve as explicitly labelled grammatical evidence with a locator and complete local help; it does not assign an additional productive verb system or an unannounced continuation.

Dependent clauses often place the finite verb late, but neither their type nor the English word order imposes a compulsory rearrangement. Distinguish the ordinary tendencies from a particular exercise target. A defensible Old English alternative may be valid language while failing an expressly requested arrangement, such as Cook 25.7's final verb.

Trace pronouns through context, grammatical gender, number and case. A masculine pronoun can refer to masculine **sang**, “song”. The nearest preceding noun is not an automatic antecedent. When two referents remain plausible, acknowledge the ambiguity; when a composition requires clarity, repeat the relevant noun. Quoted first and second person belong to the quotation's speaker and addressee; reported reference follows the actual reporting context.

Develop bounded coordination and omitted repeated predicates. A continued unexpressed subject belongs to the coordinated action only where the construction and context support it. If an omitted copula is restored with a newly expressed plural subject, its form must agree with that subject: an earlier singular **wæs** does not license a restored plural **wæs**. Do not assign a general theory of ellipsis from one example.

Reuse earlier relative, content and adverbial constructions to analyse longer sentences. Distinguish temporal conjunction **ðā**, correlative adverb **ðā** and the article **ðā**. Place a relative beside its antecedent, trace its internal role, and recover the surrounding main clause. Teach the difference between a familiar form and a new contextual function.

The exact reading supplies a particularly important case: **Ðā stefne and ðone sang** are fronted accusatives governed by the embedded **gehīerde**, as note 152.8 specifies. They are not objects of **cwæð**. This larger fronting remains visibly supported; ordinary object fronting does not establish unaided production across clause boundaries. Likewise supply the actual preposition relationship in **ðe se bisceop in wæs** rather than inventing a case ending for invariant **ðe**.

Distinguish the song's **hē becōm** and **ðe hē ǣr cōm** from Owini's acts of listening and considering. Preserve the explicit reference repair in **Cwæð hē, se bisceop, him tō**. The closing **mid wes** calls Owini to be present as well; it does not exclude him from the summons or create a newly prefixed verb.
