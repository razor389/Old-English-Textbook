## Provisional translation from the actual Old English

After these words he went on the way that had been shown him, until he reached the city gate, and entered there. While he was considering whom he might ask for the support of life, he saw a naked boy running through the street. He was anointed with oil and girded with a sheet, and carried in his hand the young men's play equipment belonging to that bathhouse. He called out in a loud voice and said: “Hear, citizens! Hear, foreigners, free and enslaved, noble and lowborn! The bathhouse is open.”

When Apollonius heard that, he stripped himself of the half-cloak he was wearing and went into the bathing place. While he watched each one of them at their activity, he sought his equal, but he could not find him in that company. Then suddenly came Arcestrates, king of that whole people, with a large retinue of his men, and entered the bath. The king began to play with his companions with a ball. Apollonius joined in the king's game, as God willed; running, he caught the ball and sent it back to the playing king, struck with swift speed. Again he sent it back; he struck quickly, so that he never let it fall. The king then recognized the young man's skill, so that he knew that he had his equal in that game. Then he said to his companions: “Go away from here; this young man, as it seems to me, is my equal.”

When Apollonius heard that the king praised him, he ran quickly and approached the king, and with a skilled hand he swung the ball with such great speed that it seemed to the king as if he had been turned from old age to youth. After that he served him agreeably on his royal seat; and when he came out of the bath, he led him by the hand, and then turned away from there by the way that he had come before.

After Apollonius had gone, the king said to his men: “I swear by the common salvation that I never bathed better than I did today, through the service of I know not what young man.” Then he turned to one of his men and said: “Go and find out what the young man is who served me so well today.”

The man then went after Apollonius. When he saw that he was wrapped in a dirty cloak, he returned to the king and said: “The young man whom you asked after is a shipwrecked man.” The king said: “By what do you know that?” The man answered him and said: “Although he keeps it silent himself, his clothing reveals him.” Then the king said: “Go quickly, and tell him that ‘the king asks you to come to his meal.’”

## Decisions and unresolved translation points to retain for reconciliation

- Printed 168.6–7 has *geongra manna plegan … belimpende*, with note 6 making the participle modify *plegan*. “Play equipment” is a contextual rendering, not an emendation or a claim that a different Old English noun was printed. Confirm the noun's exact semantic support before finalizing the key.
- Printed 169.2–3 *mid swiftre hrædnesse geslægene* is awkwardly attached to the ball; Cook explicitly says the participle is awkward. Preserve the textual form and its difficulty. The provisional literal English should be made readable without silently changing the Old English.
- At 169.4–5, the two *hē* subjects in the exchange are not differentiated morphologically. A natural game sequence has the king return the ball and Apollonius strike it. That narrative inference should be explained if names replace pronouns in the final translation.
- At 169.5–7, the king/young man's knowledge of an equal is explicitly unclear in Cook's note 6. The provisional translation keeps the Old English's surface “he … his.” It does not insert the Latin's negation or assert an unambiguous antecedent. The direct speech that follows securely identifies the king's own assessment of Apollonius.
- At 169.11, *top* is not silently replaced with shoulder or neck. Cook's note 10 says the Old English departs from the Latin and tentatively equates *top* with the ball. “Ball” therefore needs a visible uncertainty/support note. The rejuvenation comparison is retained; it is not literal chronological reversal.
- At 169.14–17, the source itself identifies Apollonius as the subject of *lǣdde* and the king as its object. The subject of coming out is naturally the king; Apollonius then goes away. Translation should track these shifts explicitly without teaching a nearest-noun rule.
- At 170.2–4, *ðā gemǣnan hǣle* is rendered conservatively “the common salvation”; Cook notes Christian influence. Its interpretation is supplied and not a productive religious formula assigned by this unit. *dyde* replaces “bathed,” and *nāt ic … hwilces* retains the king's uncertainty.
- At 170.6, *hwæt … sīe* asks what sort/status of man he is, unlike personal *hwā*. The servant's inference from the cloak answers this question. “Who” is possible idiomatic English but must not erase that distinction in the analysis task.
- At 170.8–9, the subject who sees is the servant, the man seen is Apollonius. At 170.13, concession does not establish that Apollonius actually spoke or refused to speak.
- At 170.14–16, the Old English mixes a reporting *ðæt* with second-person address inside the proposed message; Cook's note 15 explicitly identifies the mixture. The quotation inside the final English sentence is deliberately marked. A wholly regular third-person indirect report would be a new composition, not this source text.

## Reconciliation addendum, 27 October 2026

The source editor's transcript prompted a second personal inspection of 169.6 at 600 dpi. The image unmistakably reads **næfde**, not *hæfde*. The initial translation above wrongly omitted the negation. Preserve that initial evidence of the error; the corrected literal clause is **“so that he knew that he had no equal in that game.”** The antecedents remain unclear, as Cook's note 6 says. One possible interpretation has the king recognize that the young man has no equal; the Latin has the king previously know his own unmatched skill. Neither licenses deleting the Old English negative. The earlier note above about not “inserting the Latin's negation” is consequently superseded: the negation is in the Old English itself. This is a coordinator reading error, not an error in Cook or the source editor's transcript. Evidence: `cook-fb-p169-600.png`, personally viewed at original resolution after comparison with the source editor's transcript. The final key and its fresh independent review must retain the negative and explain the genuine referential uncertainty.

## Latin support clarification, 27 October 2026

Cook's printed 169 note 10 quotes *ceroma fricavit eum*: he rubbed/anointed him with ointment. The Latin contains no explicit shoulder or neck noun. Editorial shorthand about a “shoulder massage” must therefore not appear as a literal translation of this quotation. The dictionary confirmation is Lewis and Short, *ceroma*, and Georges, *ceroma*, at https://logeion.org/ceroma; the latter also lists the feminine first-declension form and cites *Historia Apollonii* 13. This clarifies the supplied English aid to the Latin note. It does not replace the printed Old English *top*, resolve Cook's tentative ball interpretation, or require the learner to know Latin.
