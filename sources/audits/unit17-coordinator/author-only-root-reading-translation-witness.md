Then she went out and had her harp fetched. As soon as she began to play the harp, she mingled its sound with pleasant song. All the men began praising her for her musical skill; Apollonius alone was silent. The king said: “Apollonius, now you do ill, for all the men praise my daughter for her musical skill, and you alone, remaining silent, censure her.”

Apollonius said: “Ah, noble king, if you permit me, I say that I understand that your daughter has indeed chanced upon musical skill, but she has not learned it well. Now have the harp given to me; then you will know what you do not yet know.” King Arcestrates said: “Apollonius, I truly perceive that you are well instructed in all things.”

Then the king had the harp given to Apollonius. Apollonius went out, dressed himself, set a crown on his head, took the harp in his hand, came in, and stood in such a manner that the king and all those seated around thought that he was not Apollonius but Apollo, the heathens' god. Then quiet and silence fell in the hall. Apollonius took his plectrum, began skillfully moving the harp strings, and mingled the harp's sound with pleasant song. The king himself and all who were present cried out with a loud voice and praised him. After this Apollonius laid aside the harp and performed, displaying many beautiful things there which were unknown and unfamiliar to the people. Each of the things he performed pleased them all greatly.

## Decisions and limits

- *gefēoll on swēgcræft*: “chanced upon musical skill,” following the actual idiom and Cook's note 174.15 (*incidit*, “has chanced”). The passage distinguishes aptitude from having learned the art well; it does not report a physical fall.
- *hine* in *hēo næfð hine nā wel geleornod* refers to masculine *swēgcræft*. It is not a male person.
- *swīgende* remains the source spelling; original note 174.12a supplies *swīgiende*. The translation represents the participle without silently editing its source form.
- *wāst / nāst* contrast knowing and not knowing. Translation does not by itself license independent production of the contracted negative paradigm.
- *Apollines* is Apollo, as original note 175.2 states. It is distinct from Apollonius.
- *wearð ... geworden* is intransitive here (“came about,” hence “fell” in natural English), not a passive merely because a participle follows *wearð*.
- *fela fægerra ðinga* contains genitive plural dependents. The source nevertheless has singular *ungecnāwen wæs and ungewunelic*. English plural relative agreement is a translation choice, not an emendation of Old English.
- *plegode / forðtēah* describe performance and display. The Latin parallel in note 175.6 adds comic and tragic performance terminology; this translation does not insert that material into the Old English.
- “Had ... fetched/given” represents *hēt* plus infinitive. “Each ... pleased them” preserves *ǣlc* as subject and *him eallum* as dative experiencer.
- The selected episode ends at 175.17. The next paragraph, the princess's love and gifts, is outside the assignment. English scene-setting may identify the banquet, king and princess from the preceding unassigned lines.
