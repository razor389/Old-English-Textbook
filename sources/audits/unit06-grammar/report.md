## Finite systems and defects

The seven selected preterite-present systems are witan, cunnan, ðurfan, durran, sculan, mugan, and mōtan. The stable database ID for mugan is magan. Dōn, gān, and willan each receive complete present and preterite indicative systems. The alternate bēon present supplements the existing wesan entry and shares the already learned preterite; no separate bēon past is invented.

Cook’s selected spelling controls the tables. Thus present 3sg willan is **wille**, gān has **gǣð**, and durran has **dearr**, with **dearst** in 2sg. Explicit source alternatives include can beside cann, wisse beside wiste, scolde beside sceolde, and wile beside wille. Wright also documents miht and mehte/mihte beside the selected meaht/meahte. These references are not automatic extra recall targets.

Cook often prints a preterite in -e followed by “etc.” The audit marks the resulting 2sg -est and plural -on cells as declared rule-derived expansions. They are not disguised as individually attested continuous-text tokens. Fulk §134 independently prints the complete dōn/gān/willan past forms; those cells have explicit corroborating paradigm evidence. Expansion of one printed plural row into three person cells is likewise stated.

Fulk §123 expressly marks the conventional infinitive labels *durran, *sculan, *mōtan, and *magan as unattested. Cook §125 flags several of these labels with question marks. Neither Cook’s mugan display nor Wright’s unstarred magan label establishes an actual productive infinitive. No plain or inflected infinitive is generated for those four systems.

Cook and Wright call wite, and in Wright witaþ, imperative. Fulk distinguishes the absence of a formal preterite-present imperative system and the use of subjunctive substitutes. The course should therefore say that no regular modal imperative system is generated or assigned here. It should not say that these verbs can never function in commands. Witan command forms remain documented, unassigned evidence. No positive willan command pair is invented from willað, and no cunnan or ðurfan imperative pair is synthesized.

Āgan, unnan, dugan, munan/gemunan, nugan, and the contracted negative companions are outside the selected new roster. Existing nabban finite recognition remains intact; its new extension rows remain unassigned.

## Imperative and nonfinite completeness

The selected models cover ordinary strong verbs across all classes, the contracted h-patterns, strong j-presents, all earlier weak-I subpatterns, weak II, all four learned weak-III verbs, and the selected anomalous verbs. Complete here means second singular and second plural. First- and third-person optative commands remain later scope.

The important singular distinctions are retained together: hīer; nęre, gefręme, sęle, sęte; giere; cys and gefyl; lufa; hafa, leofa, saga, hoga; bide, site, lige, hęfe, sciepe, swęre. Contracted strong verbs have wrīh, tēoh, seoh, sleah, and fōh, with the short vowels in seoh/sleah distinguished from the selected long vowels elsewhere. Ordinary plural commands use their selected present-plural stems. Cook’s sęge, lifiað, and optional e in sęcg(e)að/hycg(e)að remain explicit alternatives.

Cys and gefyl are declared applications of Cook’s heavy weak-I model and frequent final-geminate simplification. They are not falsely labeled individually quoted source commands, and the rule does not prohibit every unsimplified final spelling. The separate sete type remains visible.

Cook §95’s **binde gē**, with shortened plural -e immediately before a following subject pronoun, is recorded as a positional reference variant. Assigned commands omit the subject or place it before the full form. This note must not be generalized into free plural -e or silently conflated with indicative inversion.

Selected inflected infinitives use **-anne** after separate tō; -ian gives -ianne, and geminated stems remain geminated. Contracted -n gives -nne, including fōnne, sēonne, dōnne, gānne, and bēonne. Wright §480 and Fulk §58 document -enne beside -anne; a valid -enne form can fail a stated selected-form target without being ungrammatical. Rule-derived lexical cells are explicitly distinguished from printed model cells and existing supplied citations.

## Elementary usage

The lexical frames distinguish skill with cunnan, capacity with mugan, need with ðurfan, daring with durran, obligation with sculan, permission with mōtan, and volition with willan. All use the bounded same-subject plain infinitive. Past mōste is assigned as actual past permission in an explicit context, not automatically modern “must.” Wolde is assigned as actual past wanting/intention, not automatically hypothetical “would.”

Witan takes an ordinary accusative nominal fact or matter, supported by Cook Reader XII p173/PDF189 and the independently checked BT WITAN example with þing. The fact/matter sense of þing is an explicit new sense. Content clauses in the evidence are not learner dependencies. Dōn has an ordinary accusative action/task frame; gān, cuman, and faran have explicit ordinary intransitive-motion scope. Earlier finite tables alone did not establish all these uses.

Fulk §52 supports futurity and habitual/gnomic use of the bēon series. The selected productive example has an explicit expected-later state context. Predicate agreement remains prior; passive and perfect constructions are not imported. Bare bið must not be glossed as obligatorily future in all contexts.

Cook §200 supports cuman with a same-subject purpose expressed by tō plus an inflected infinitive. Cook §199.1 also permits bare infinitives with motion/specification/purpose. The selected target must not be taught as the only grammatical purpose construction. The source’s habban plus tō-infinitive is bounded recognition of something available for an action; it is not a general modern “have to” obligation rule.

The source editor’s directly checked **ne ondrǣd ðū…** supports ordinary negative imperative syntax; the coordinator independently corroborated it from BT wēpan. This teaches the construction, not the witness’s additional lexical government. Direct quotation can contain the newly taught command; indirect commands and optative syntax remain outside scope.
