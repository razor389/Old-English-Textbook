## Recommended editorial decision

A compact guide can be grounded in these sources without teaching phonetic transcription. Preserve the course’s Cook spelling. Adopt a declared practical reading convention: traditional short-vowel approximations explicitly permitted by Fulk §19; Fulk’s held **æ** for long **ǣ**; Fulk’s rounded, one-syllable diphthongs in §20; and Fulk’s voicing rule and stated exceptions in §17. Cook supplies the practical sounded-cluster directions. This is a coherent editorial selection, not a claim that the books prescribe the same sounds throughout.

Put the explanation of that selection in a short source endnote. The learner needs one usable target for each example, not competing systems in every row. The technical details below belong to the audit and do not require the learner guide to repeat the IPA.

## Vowels and American English cues

Fulk’s table (§16, pp.11–12) gives short and long vowels of the same quality. His discussion (§19, pp.13–14) nevertheless expressly accepts the traditional lax short **e, i, o, u, y** as a practical substitute for English speakers. Thus **bet**, **bit**, **foot**, and a rounded short **o** can be used in an accessible guide without pretending that this is his preferred detailed reconstruction. If those short vowels are selected, do not say that every pair in the classroom table differs only in duration: the practical **bit/beet** and **foot/boot** pairs also differ in quality.

Fulk’s **æ / ǣ** pair is the open-front vowel of **bat**, brief or held longer. **Cat** or **bat** is a better American cue than **man**, whose vowel is often raised before the nasal. Cook’s **care** cue for **ǣ** is especially unsuitable for rhotic American readers. Using held **cat** for **ǣ** follows Fulk explicitly; it is not merely a spelling change to Cook’s English cue.

**Father** is a useful approximate cue for **a / ā**, with shortness or length supplied separately. There is a substantive limit: Fulk gives a rounded vowel before nasals, as in **land/lond** and **man(n)/mon(n)** (§§16,19). Cook’s introductory table does not state that condition. The coordinator should either teach it in a small note or describe a uniform father-type classroom **a** as a Cook-based simplification. It should not be called Fulk’s exact complete system.

Fulk’s **bait** and **boat** cues (§9, p.8) are explicitly approximate. Modern American versions commonly glide, so an instruction to hold the initial vowel steady is needed for **ē / ō**. The short rounded **o** is not reliably taught by **bought**, **law**, or **broad** to a reader with the cot–caught merger. A plain mouth instruction can accompany the cue: begin with the first part of **go**, lower the jaw a little while keeping the lips rounded, and make it brief. That instruction is a new editorial teaching aid, not a quotation from Fulk.

The ordinary American sound inventory does not provide the front rounded vowels **y / ȳ**. Fulk §12 (p.10) gives an articulatory method: keep the tongue position for **ee** while rounding the lips for **oo**. Cook’s note also supplies the useful traditional pair of rounded **bit** and rounded **beet**. Avoid presenting French or German examples as sufficient help for a reader who knows neither language. One IPA symbol at introduction can be optional; repeated whole-word transcriptions are unnecessary.

Unstressed vowels should remain distinguishable (§19, pp.13–14). In particular, Fulk permits a light **bet** vowel for unstressed **e**, rather than reducing all endings to the **uh** of modern **about**. His bounded discussion of syllabic consonants can stay out of an introductory guide.

## Diphthongs and spelling exceptions

Fulk §§16,20 (pp.12,14) adopts **ea, eo, ie** with values **[æu, eu, iu]**, and long **ēa, ēo, īe** with a longer first element. He acknowledges disagreement about the reconstruction. The learner instruction can describe one smooth, single-syllable movement toward **oo**, beginning respectively near **bat**, steady **bait**, or **beet**. Neither the starting cue nor the final movement should introduce a second beat. There is no exact familiar American word that substitutes for the complete set.

This is a real choice between the sources. Cook §5 (p.7) prints **ea = æ + uh; ēa = ē + ah; eo = e + o; ēo = ē + o; ie = i + ĕh; īe = ī + ĕh**, with a very slight second element. Do not say that Cook and Fulk merely use different symbols for the same recommendation.

Fulk’s verified examples are **earm** ‘arm’, **ēare** ‘ear’, **eorl** ‘man’, **fēoll** ‘fell’, **hliehhan** ‘laugh’, and **hīeran** ‘hear’. Their use would be supplied sound examples, not new independent vocabulary or morphology assignments.

The same vowel-letter pairs sometimes do not denote diphthongs. Fulk §21 (p.14) underpoints the silent **e** in **sċẹamian**, **ġẹong**, and **senġẹan** to mark the preceding consonant. A guide using Cook’s undotted spelling must give local help for any such word it introduces. It should not pretend that every written **ea/eo** can be pronounced by a universal two-letter rule.

## Consonants: ordinary rules and their limits

**Thorn and eth:** Fulk §§15–17 (pp.10–12) confirms that the letter choice does not distinguish the sounds of **thin** and **this**. These are usable American cues. The guide should retain each selected source spelling.

**f, s, þ/ð:** Fulk’s ordinary rule (§17, p.12) voices these between voiced sounds and leaves them voiceless initially, finally, and beside voiceless consonants. **Cnīf / cnīfas** illustrates the resulting alternation. Historical stress and word-formation boundaries matter: Fulk specifically retains unvoiced consonants in **hǣlþu, forþām, beþenċan, hīersum, mānful**. These are bounded examples of why “between vowels” is not a complete rule. Cook §8 (pp.8–9) is broader, also prescribing voicing after a sonant at syllable end. Select Fulk’s rule openly. Do not derive Old English voicing mechanically from modern spellings or names; Fulk expressly warns against that inference.

**c and g:** The basic table (§16, p.11), explanation (§18, pp.12–13), and full account (§135, pp.103–104) show why a simple front-vowel rule cannot predict every written form. Fulk uses overpoints; Cook usually does not. Teach **k/ch**, hard **g**, the consonant **y**, and the continuous voiced **g** as sound choices, with checked examples. Avoid “c is always ch before e or i”: **cēlan** is a stated exception, and inflections such as **dīċ/dīcas** differ. Likewise **gǣstan** remains hard despite its front written vowel.

**Continuous g and noninitial h:** Fulk §§9,16,18 (pp.8,11–13) supplies a continuous back-of-mouth **h** sound and its voiced counterpart for **g**, exemplified by **brōhte, fugol, āgan**. These need mouth instructions because there is no regular native American equivalent. For **g**, the back of the tongue approaches the hard-g position without completely stopping the air; voicing continues. Cook’s **ach/ich** distinction can add a bounded front/back **h** note. Fulk’s basic table deliberately omits such positional variants.

**ng, cg/gg:** Ordinary unpalatalized **ng** contains the audible **g** of **finger**, but **senġan** and **menġan** have a palatal outcome resembling the end of **singe**. Fulk’s own contextual “ng” remark must not be turned into an unconditional rule. Most **cg/gg** has the **bridge** sound; **frogga/frocga, docga** are explicit hard-stop exceptions, and a few compound boundaries have other sequences (§135c). A compact guide can give the ordinary patterns and one supplied exception without assigning historical reconstruction as a learner task.

**sc:** Usually the sound of **ship**, but **frosc** has **sk**. Fulk §135d also shows inflectional differences, including **frosc/frosċes**, so “always sh” is false.

**Doubled consonants:** Fulk §17 compares **nn** with the long **n** in **pen-knife**. Keep the extra duration without an added vowel. With a stop, hold the closure rather than adding a second syllable. Cook §11 supplies the same compound-boundary comparison.

**r and stop aspiration:** Fulk §9 uses **run** for its approximate **r** symbol; §10 describes modern English **r**. These pages do not require a trill. His **p/t/k** examples **span/store/score** can support a small-puff articulation cue, but the inspected sound chapter does not formulate a separate Old English aspiration rule. Neither point needs to become a demanding learner test.

**Clusters and x:** Cook §7 explicitly sounds initial **h** in **hl/hn/hr/hw**, **w** in **wr/wl**, and **c** in **cn**. Do not substitute silent modern-English initials. Fulk’s **x** table gives an earlier continuous-h-plus-s value and later **ks**; Cook’s **hs** instruction uses modern **x**. A **ks** classroom reading can be adopted, but should not be attributed as Fulk’s sole historical value.

## Stress, marks, and assessment boundaries

Fulk §22 (pp.14–15) places stress normally on the first syllable, with unstressed **ge-**, usually unstressed **be-/for-**, and generally unstressed verb prefixes. Noun-derived verbs can preserve an accented first element. Teach a compact rule and mark any needed exceptional example explicitly; do not infer stress from the presence of a macron.

Fulk §23 (p.15) explains that function-word and prefix macrons can be conventional despite shortening in connected speech. Careful introductory reading may follow the printed lengths as a stated classroom convention. It should not claim that every historical occurrence of **sē**, **ā-**, or **tō-** always had equal duration.

A source-based guide need not reproduce Fulk’s IPA transcription exercises. Practical tasks can ask the learner to notice a printed length contrast, give the taught sound in a supplied word, maintain a doubled consonant, or locate the stressed syllable. Each task should test only the guide’s declared convention, with its example meaning supplied. New sound examples do not silently add the word’s declension, conjugation, or lexical sense to the course’s existing production milestones.
