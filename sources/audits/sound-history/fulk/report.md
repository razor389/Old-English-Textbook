## Decisions that matter for the appendix

**Breaking needs vowel-specific limits.** Fulk §62 gives æ→ea, e→eo and i→io, with later io→eo in West Saxon. All three can break before historical h and suitable r-clusters. Before l plus a consonant, however, æ is broadly affected while e regularly breaks only before lh; good i/lh evidence is absent. Thus **helpan** stays unbroken but **healp** reflects earlier *hælp. Some long vowels also break before h. A short introductory rule must identify its limited scope rather than rule long breaking out.

**Umlaut is not every vowel change.** Fulk §§74–76 treats i/j-umlaut as anticipatory fronting or raising under the influence of a following sound that may later disappear. **fōt/fēt** illustrates inflectional alternation, but fēt also serves as dative singular. **dōm/dēman** and **tūn/tȳnan** have explicit earlier -j- derivations in §109. Do not print a reconstructed complete ancestor of fēt without separate evidence; §82 gives the OE paradigm and identifies mutation, not that full derivation.

**Present raising requires historical precision.** Fulk §76 explicitly says that adding e→i and ēo→īe to a practical mutation list simplifies the teaching but is historically inaccurate as a one-step account. The earlier e-raising predates later front mutation. The Cook auditor reports that Cook §17 already warns that i is not strictly umlaut of e, immediately after helpan/hilpð. These accounts are compatible; Fulk adds the fuller chronology for the diphthong. Practical paradigm labels need not be condemned as errors.

**Ablaut columns are not a sound-change chain.** Fulk’s **drīfan, drāf, drifon, drifen** (§85) gives four principal parts, not four successive historical stages. Inherited ablaut and later sound changes jointly explain the visible forms. Label the columns or separate them with slashes; reserve an arrow for genuine historical development.

**The three major sources of short diphthongs must stay separate.** Breaking responds to particular following consonants. West Saxon initial-palatal diphthongization affects a following vowel. Back mutation responds to a vowel in the next syllable and has narrow West Saxon conditions. Fulk’s **heofon/hefon** shows the effect of analogy; do not frame back mutation as a rule that predicts every written form from its surviving ending.

**Consonant histories and vowel loss make the appendix useful beyond labels.** A short account of gemination explains why a vanished j can leave two consonants. Apocope and syncope explain **scipu/wīf** and **enġel/engles**, while analogy explains restored full stems such as **hēafodes** beside **hēafdes**. The earlier *seohan and surviving **sēon**, **sīehst**, **sīehþ** show why h may disappear in one part of a paradigm and remain in another. The course’s separately evidenced spellings should be used with explicit normalization notes where they differ from Fulk.

**Dialect labels prevent false universal rules.** “Smoothing” denotes more than one change: Late West Saxon smoothing of ea/ēa and the wider Anglian development need separate labels. The same written hēh can occur in more than one historical context. Fulk also acknowledges uncertainty in the detailed environments for palatalization. A concise appendix can identify a mechanism and show one secure comparison without claiming that spelling alone determines every earlier sound.

## Spelling, source reuse and limits

Exact example strings and asterisk status are separate from the original paraphrases in the JSON. Fulk uses editorial palatal dots; course-style omission is a declared normalization. Thorn/eth substitutions and vowel-spelling differences also need an explicit record. Gothic or German comparisons remain labelled non-OE. No reconstructed form becomes an attested Old English word by being useful pedagogically.
