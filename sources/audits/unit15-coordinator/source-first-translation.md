## Duties: opening argument, printed 159

The Savior said to the proud man: “Why were you so miserly with my goods which I gave you? Why did you neglect the gift which I gave you? I now deprive you of my gift which I bestowed on you; then you will be poor in worldly life. Why would you not consider that I wish to repay each person for the good deed which a person does for my name's sake? I repay it to him a hundredfold, as it is declared and stated in my gospel: ‘Whatever you give to one of my least ones, you always give to me; and I give you eternal joy in heaven in return.’”

This English witness closes the outer quotation for readability; the selected source page itself does not close the larger outer speech. The final learner transcript should retain source punctuation and explain that the extract is the first argument, not the whole speech.

Questions for evidence reconciliation: whether the temporal force of `þonne bist þū wǣdla on woruldlīfe` needs a more idiomatic contextual English rendering; avoid silently replacing its explicit worldly-life phrase with afterlife language. `For hwon noldest` combines why with the negative preterite of willan: “why would you not” preserves the volitional component. `Swā-hwæt-swā` is free choice, not a question. The amount is hundredfold, not merely one hundred objects. The recipients are the least ones; the implied plural noun must remain contextually supplied rather than invented as a lexical certainty.

## Complete preface, printed 162

King Alfred was the interpreter of this book, and translated it from book-Latin into English, as it is now done. Sometimes he set word for word, sometimes sense for sense, just as he could then explain it most clearly and intelligibly amid the various and manifold worldly cares which often troubled him both in mind and in body. Those cares which came upon the kingdoms he had taken over in his days are very hard for us to count. Yet, when he had studied this book and translated it from Latin into English prose, he then made it again into verse, as it is now done. And now he begs, and for God's sake entreats, every one of those who wishes to read this book to pray for him, and not to blame him if he understands it more correctly than he could; for every person must speak what he speaks and do what he does according to the measure of his understanding and according to his leisure.

The first `he` in the final comparison is the reader; the second is Alfred, as the original notes specify. The person who desires to read is the experiencer, not the grammatical subject of the impersonal desire frame. The translated book is feminine `hīe/hēo`. `Ægðer ge ... ge` correlates the two domains. `For þǣm ... bisgum` requires careful English causative/obstructive interpretation: “amid” is my provisional contextual rendering and should be checked against the exact prepositional use, rather than treated as a new general sense. `Geweorhte ... tō lēoðe` concerns the later verse rendering; do not merge the two acts of translation or erase `eft`.

## Complete prayer, printed 163

Lord, almighty God, maker and ruler of all creatures, I beseech you for your great mercy, for the sign of the holy cross, for Saint Mary's virginity, for Saint Michael's obedience, and for the love of all your saints and their merits, that you guide me better than I have acted toward you. And direct me to your will and to my soul's need better than I myself know; establish my mind toward your will and my soul's need; strengthen me against the devil's temptations; remove from me foul lust and every unrighteousness; shield me against my enemies, visible and invisible; and teach me to do your will, so that I may love you inwardly above all things, with pure thought and a pure body. For you are my creator, my redeemer, my help, my comfort, my trust and my hope. May praise and glory be yours now and always, for ever, world without any end. Amen.

Initial caution: `bęt þonne ic āworhte tō þē` is “better than I have acted toward you” in this witness; the relation to deserving must be checked lexically before selecting an idiomatic final translation. The comparative after the next command is “better than I myself know/can”; the source `cunne` has an optative note. Distinguish commands such as `gewissa` from the preceding subordinate `gewissie`; do not flatten all the forms into commands. `Þīnne willan` is explicitly the object of `wyrċenne` in the source note, even though the latter is an inflected infinitive. The final `Sīe` is a wish. Literal family names/virtue labels in the divine address must remain distinct.
