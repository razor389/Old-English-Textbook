# A guide to Old English pronunciation

This guide gives a practical pronunciation for reading the course. English examples refer to a particular sound in an ordinary American pronunciation, not to the whole word. Where English has no equivalent, the instructions describe the position of the tongue and lips. Every Old English word is glossed; its meaning need not be memorized.

## 1. Letters and length

**Æ æ**, called *ash*, is one vowel letter. **Þ þ**, *thorn*, and **Ð ð**, *eth*, represent the sounds written **th** in English *thin* and *this*. Either letter can represent either sound. This course normally uses **ð**. Cook's hooked **ę** and **ǫ** have the sounds of **e** and **o**; the hooks record their history, not additional sounds.[1]

A **macron**, the line above a vowel, marks length: **ā, ǣ, ē, ī, ō, ū, ȳ**. Hold a long vowel appreciably longer than a short one. Long does not mean the English letter name, and a macron does not mark stress. Thus **ac**, “but”, has a short vowel; **āc**, “oak”, has a long one. Both end with the sound of **k**.

In a long two-letter vowel spelling, the macron stands on the first letter: **ēa, ēo, īe**. The two letters belong to one syllable. Preserve the printed marks; a following pair of consonants does not turn an unmarked vowel into a long one.

## 2. Simple vowels

Use these short and long values. Some of the practical English approximations differ in quality as well as duration.[2]

| Letters | Short vowel | Long vowel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a, ā | The vowel of *father*, brief: **ac**, “but”. | Hold the same vowel: **āc**, “oak”. |
| æ, ǣ | The vowel of *cat*: **sæt**, “sat”. | Hold that same vowel: **sǣd**, “seed”. |
| e, ē | The vowel of *bet*: **męte**, “food”. | Hold the beginning of *day* without its final glide: **hē**, “he”. |
| i, ī | The vowel of *bit*: **is**, “is”. | The vowel of *see*, held: **īs**, “ice”. |
| o, ō | A brief rounded vowel, described below: **god**, “god”. | Hold the beginning of *go* without its final glide: **gōd**, “good”. |
| u, ū | The vowel of *foot*: **full**, “full”. | The vowel of *food*, held: **fūl**, “vile”. |
| y, ȳ | The tongue position of *bit* with rounded lips: **wyrt**, “herb; plant”. | The tongue position of *see* with rounded lips, held: **fȳr**, “fire”. |

For **o**, the vowel of *caught* is a useful starting point if you distinguish *cot* from *caught*. If they sound alike, begin with the first part of *go*, lower the jaw a little while keeping the lips rounded, and make the vowel brief. Keep **o** distinct from the unrounded *father* vowel. For **ō**, keep the jaw and tongue steady: do not finish with an **oo** movement.

For **ē**, likewise stop before the glide at the end of *day*. Do not pronounce **ǣ** like American *care*: it is the *cat* vowel held longer, without an **r** sound.

In a **stressed** syllable, short **a** before **m** or **n** takes the more rounded short-**o** quality just described. Use this in **mann**, “man; human being”, and **lang**, “long”. It is not the vowel of ordinary American *man*. This rule does not require the same rounding in every unstressed **-an** ending.

For **y** and **ȳ**, keep the tongue forward while rounding the lips as for **oo** in *food*. For short **y**, begin with the tongue position of *bit*; for long **ȳ**, use *see*. Rounding the lips must not pull the tongue back into an ordinary **oo** vowel. These sounds have no usual American English equivalent. Here **y** is a vowel, not the consonant at the beginning of *yes*.

## 3. Diphthongs

A *diphthong* is a vowel that moves from one position toward another within one syllable. For these spellings, make one smooth movement toward **oo**. Do not give the second letter a separate beat.[3]

| Spellings | Movement | Supplied examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ea, ēa | Start with the vowel of *cat*, then round toward **oo**. | **earm**, “arm”; **ēare**, “ear”. |
| eo, ēo | Start with the steady first vowel of *day*, then round toward **oo**. | **eorl**, “man”; **fēoll**, “fell”. |
| ie, īe | Start with the vowel of *see*, then round toward **oo**. | **giefan**, “give”; **hīeran**, “hear”. |

In **ēa, ēo, īe**, hold the first part longer before making the movement. The **eo** of **eorl** is short; the **ēo** of **fēoll** is long. The word **ēare** has two syllables: its long **ēa**, followed by a light final **e**. The opening **g** of **giefan** has the consonant sound of **y** in *yes*.

There are word-specific exceptions. In **geong**, “young”, **e** does not supply a vowel sound: the initial **g** is like **y** in *yes*, followed by short rounded **o**. Its **ng** has the sound heard in *finger*, including the **g**. Do not give this word the ordinary **eo** glide or copy the vowel of modern *young*.

## 4. Consonants

Read **b, d, l, m, n, p, t, w** as at the beginning of *boy, dog, lip, man, no, pen, top, win*. Use a pronounced American **r**, as in *run*, as a practical approximation. The remaining letters and combinations need the following distinctions.[4]

### c, g, and combinations

Work in the following order. A supplied pronunciation for a particular word or form takes precedence over a general pattern.

**1. Read a combination first.** Do not apply a rule for single **c** or **g** to one letter of these combinations.

| Spelling | Ordinary sound | Model and supplied exception |
| --- | --- | --- |
| sc | **sh** as in *ship*, including before a back vowel. | **scip**, “ship”, has one **sh** sound. The exception **frosc**, “frog”, ends with **s** followed by **k**. |
| cg; usually gg | The final sound of *bridge*. | **ecg**, “edge”. The exception **docga**, “dog”, has a prolonged hard **g**. |
| ng | As in *finger*, with audible **g**. | **lang**, “long”. In the supplied exception **sengan**, “singe”, **ng** has the sound at the end of *singe*. |

For the rules below, the **back-vowel spellings** are **a/ā, o/ō, u/ū**. The relevant **front-vowel spellings** are **e/ē, i/ī, æ/ǣ**; the diphthongs **ea/ēa, eo/ēo, ie/īe** also begin at the front of the mouth. Although **y/ȳ** are front vowels in articulation, their spelling is not a reliable instruction to give **c** or **g** a front sound. Use the supplied pronunciation in such words.

**2. At the beginning of a word, decide c from what immediately follows.**

| Following sound or spelling | Ordinary c sound | Model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A consonant, or a/ā, o/ō, u/ū | **k** as in *kit* | **cnīf**, “knife”; **cuman**, “come”. |
| e/ē, i/ī, æ/ǣ, or a front diphthong | **ch** as in *chin* | **cealf**, “calf”, begins with **ch**. |

These are useful defaults, with lexical exceptions. **Cēlan**, “cool”, begins with **k** despite **ē**. Both **c** letters in **cyrice**, “church”, are **ch**: learn its initial sound with the word, rather than deducing it from **y**. Keep all three syllables, including the final **e**.

**3. Inside or at the end of a word, use the neighbours of c.** Before another consonant, the ordinary sound is **k**. Immediately after **i/ī**, use **ch** when **c** ends the word or is followed by a vowel other than **a/ā, o/ō, u/ū**. The second **c** of **cyrice** follows this pattern. A following back vowel instead gives the ordinary **k** pattern.

Compare **dīc**, “ditch; dike”, with **dīcas**, “ditches; dikes”. Final **c** after **ī** has **ch**; before the **a** of the longer form it has **k**. An ending can therefore change the consonant sound. In other positions **k** is the ordinary starting point, but some words need a supplied pronunciation. Do not extend the after-**i/ī** rule to every front vowel.

Learn **sēcan**, “seek”, as a supplied exception: its **c** is **ch** as in *chin*, despite the following **a**. The back-vowel pattern does not override a word's stated pronunciation.

**4. For single g, first decide whether it has the consonant sound y in *yes*.**

| Position | Ordinary g sound | Model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| At the beginning, immediately before a front vowel or front diphthong | **y** as in *yes* | **giefan**, “give”. The prefix **ge-** also begins with this sound. |
| After a front vowel, at the end or when no back vowel immediately follows | **y** as in *yes* | **dæg**, “day”, ends with **y**. A following consonant does not by itself require hard **g**. |

Otherwise use the non-**y** branch below, unless a word has a supplied exceptional pronunciation. At the beginning, a consonant between **g** and a later front vowel prevents the first row from applying: look at the immediate neighbour. The **g** rule after a front vowel is broader than the **c** rule after **i/ī**; keep them distinct. The earlier caution about **y/ȳ** still applies.

**5. For non-y g, decide between stopped and continuous g.**

| Position | Sound | Model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| At the beginning; after n; in a supplied hard doubled consonant | Stopped **g**, as in *go* | **god**, “god”; **lang**, “long”; **docga**, “dog”. |
| Elsewhere | The continuous voiced back sound described below | **fugol**, “bird”: single **g** between back vowels. |

For the continuous **g**, put the back of the tongue near the position for hard **g**, but leave a narrow gap. Let air pass continuously while the throat vibrates: the sound can be held, unlike the stopped **g** of *go*. Its phonetic sign is **[ɣ]**. Do not omit it or substitute **y**. First selecting the **y** or non-**y** branch prevents this rule from changing the final sound of **dæg** into continuous **g**.

These patterns let you decide regular new examples. Where a word or form has an exceptional pronunciation, use its stated sound; spelling alone does not settle every case. The practice below supplies any required exception. No historical derivation is required.

### h and sounded clusters

At the beginning of a syllable, **h** is like English **h** in *hat*. Later in a syllable it has a stronger, continuous sound made by narrowing the passage above the tongue.[5]

In **brōhte**, “brought”, raise the back of the tongue toward the position for **k**, leaving a gap for breath. Keep the throat unvoiced. This back sound is **[x]**. Both it and the following **t** are heard.

In **niht**, “night”, the preceding **i** brings the tongue farther forward. Raise the middle of the tongue toward the roof of the mouth and let breath pass without voice. This front sound is **[ç]**. Keep the **t** too: the Old English word does not have modern English's silent **gh**. Front vowels generally favour this front articulation; back vowels favour the back one.

Sound the initials in **hl, hn, hr, hw, wr, wl, cn**. In **hlāf**, “loaf”, begin with audible breath before **l**; in **hwæl**, “whale”, keep **h** with **w**. In **wrītan**, “write”, pronounce **w** and **r**; in **cnīf**, “knife”, pronounce **k** and **n**. Join the consonants without putting a vowel between them. The spelling **cw** has **k** followed by **w**. Read **x** and **hs** as **k** followed by **s**; **weax**, “wax”, ends with that pair.

## 5. f, s, and th: voice

Compare *fan* with *van*, *sip* with *zip*, and *thin* with *this*. The initial consonant of the second word has voice: the throat vibrates. Vowels and consonants such as **l, m, n, r** also carry voice.[6]

The ordinary rule for single **f, s, þ/ð** is:

- At the beginning or end of a word, or beside a voiceless consonant, use the sounds of *fan, sip, thin*.
- Inside a word, between voiced sounds, use the sounds of *van, zip, this*.

| Word | Meaning | Sound to use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| cnīf | knife | Final **f** as in *fan*. |
| cnīfas | knives | **f** as in *van*, between vowels; final **s** as in *sip*. |
| rīsan | rise | **s** as in *zip*, between vowels. |
| hūs | house | Final **s** as in *sip*. |
| ōðer | other | **ð** as in *this*, between vowels. |
| ðā | then | Initial **ð** as in *thin*. |

Thus **is**, “is”, ends with the **s** of *sip*, not the **z** of its modern English equivalent. The letter **ð** does not by itself tell you to use the voiced sound.

Stress and the boundaries between word parts can preserve exceptions. In **hǣlðu**, “health”, and **forðām**, “therefore”, use the unvoiced **th** of *thin*. Learn these as supplied word-specific readings; the surrounding voiced sounds do not override them.

## 6. Duration, syllables, and stress

Keep vowel length separate from consonant length. In **full**, “full”, **u** is short; **fūl**, “vile”, has long **ū**. A doubled consonant at the end of a word normally has ordinary single-consonant duration: the final **ll** of **full** does not require extra holding.

Between vowels, a doubled consonant has extra duration, like the **n** across the join in *pen-knife*. Do not insert a vowel between its two letters.[7]

With a doubled stop, hold the closure longer. In **habban**, “have”, keep the lips closed for **bb**, then release into the following vowel. There are two syllables. Do not add another vowel or another syllable to pronounce the doubling.

Written unstressed vowels remain audible and distinguishable. In **męte**, “food”, there are two syllables, and final **e** is a light *bet* vowel. In **fugol**, “bird”, there are also two syllables; its **o** is heard. Do not reduce every ending to the **uh** at the beginning of modern *about*.

The main stress normally falls on the first syllable, as in **habban**, “have”. Prefixes complicate this: **ge-** is unstressed, **be-** and **for-** usually are, and a prefixed verb generally stresses its root. When **ge-** precedes a one-syllable root, the word has two syllables and the root receives the stress. A supplied division identifies the prefix where needed. A macron indicates duration regardless of which syllable is stressed.

For these careful reading exercises, observe printed length. In connected speech, unstressed function words and prefixes could be shorter than their conventional written macrons suggest. This does not authorize adding, removing, or moving marks in the course text.

| and | and |
